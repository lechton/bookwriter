### Barnett: The Doctor Who Would Not Come Downstairs

A man walks into a hospital at eight o'clock in the morning, vomiting so badly that he can hardly stand, and the doctor on duty, who is upstairs, refuses to come down and sends word through the nurse that he should go home and call his own doctor. Five and a half hours later the man is dead, and when his widow sues the hospital, she loses without getting a single pound.

Keep that ending in mind, because it sounds wrong, as if the law had taken the doctor's side, and yet by the end of this story I think you will agree that the judge had no other choice. The man's name was William Barnett, and the case is Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington Hospital Management Committee, decided in 1969, which we will simply call Barnett. Hold on to that name, Barnett, because Barnett teaches the most uncomfortable lesson in the whole law of negligence.

It is five o'clock in the morning on New Year's Day, 1966, in Chelsea, in West London, and Barnett and two other men are finishing a long night shift as watchmen guarding the buildings of a college. Before going home, they make tea and drink it together.

Twenty minutes later all three of them are being sick, not a little but violently and again and again, with terrible cramps, and they assume it must be something they ate. So they walk to the nearest hospital, St Stephen's, and at about eight o'clock they reach the casualty department, which is what the emergency room was called then. Barnett is the worst of the three, and he lies across two chairs in the waiting room, doubled over in pain.

A nurse sees them and does the right thing by telephoning the casualty doctor on duty, Dr Banerjee, but Dr Banerjee is upstairs and feeling unwell himself after New Year's Eve. When the nurse describes the vomiting, the doctor gives an answer that law students still read today: well, I am vomiting myself, and I have not been drinking, so tell them to go home and go to bed and call in their own doctors.

So nobody examines Barnett, the three men leave, and at half past one that afternoon William Barnett is dead.

What nobody knew that morning was that someone had put arsenic in the tea, a heavy metal poison that attacks the stomach and the organs, and whoever did it was never found.

Now imagine that you are the judge in Barnett. The widow says that her husband walked into the hospital asking for help and the doctor would not even look at him. The hospital answers that this is true, and that it was wrong, but that it made no difference. Before you hear what the court decided, take a moment to ask yourself who should win in Barnett.

Most people say the widow, because the doctor was clearly careless and nobody doubts it. So let us watch how the judge, Justice Nield, worked through Barnett one question at a time.

The first question was whether the hospital owed Barnett a duty of care, and the answer was yes, because a man who walks through the open door of a casualty department asking for help is exactly the person a hospital must look after.

The second question was whether the doctor broke that duty by refusing to come down and look at three men who were vomiting, and the answer, without any doubt, was yes again.

That makes two out of two for the widow, which brings us to the third question, the one that decides Barnett: did the doctor's carelessness actually cause Barnett's death?

To answer it, the court asked something very simple. If the doctor had done everything right and run downstairs at eight o'clock, would Barnett have lived? Lawyers call this the but for test, because it asks whether the harm would still have happened but for the mistake, and the person suing has to show that, more likely than not, the victim would have been saved.

So the experts in Barnett walked the court through that morning as it should have gone. A good doctor who sees a man with stomach pain and vomiting does not think of arsenic, and there is no reason why he should, so he admits him to a bed and puts him on a drip. To find arsenic, the hospital has to test his blood and what is in his stomach, and those tests take hours, so only when the results come back can a doctor give the antidote, a drug called BAL. On the best possible morning that antidote reaches Barnett around midday, and by then the poison has already done its work, so Barnett still dies at half past one.

The answer to the third question in Barnett is therefore no. The doctor was careless and the man died, but it was the arsenic and not the carelessness that caused the death, and so the hospital paid nothing.

Now go back to the feeling you had at the start, that this sounds wrong. It sounds wrong because we want someone to punish that doctor, but punishment is the job of criminal law and of the medical profession itself. Tort law does a different job: it makes a wrongdoer pay for the harm he caused, not for harm that someone else caused, and not for being a bad doctor in general. In Barnett a poisoner caused the harm, and the doctor's refusal, shameful as it was, changed nothing. That is the lesson of Barnett: being careless is not enough, because the carelessness must make the difference.

And that lesson leaves a door open. Barnett works because the experts could say clearly what would have happened, but what if they cannot? Imagine a man who breathes in deadly dust at work for years, for two different employers, until he gets cancer, and science cannot say which of them caused it. Does his widow lose again? That is the question in Fairchild.

## What Changed from v1

- **The sentences flow.** Linked thoughts are joined with and, but, because, and so, instead of being cut into fragments. The owner's example, "But it made no difference. Stop for a moment and decide. Who wins in Barnett?", now reads "The hospital answers that this is true, and that it was wrong, but that it made no difference. Before you hear what the court decided, take a moment to ask yourself who should win in Barnett."
- **Short sentences are kept for a few turning points only**, such as "Does his widow lose again?" near the end.
- **The but for test is stated correctly.** v1 said the claimant must show the answer is "more likely yes than no", which pointed the wrong way. v2 says the claimant must show that, more likely than not, the victim would have been saved.
- **Unchanged:** the structure the owner approved (the twist first, the reason to care before the background, the listener judging first, the three questions, the return to "this sounds wrong", the hook into Fairchild) and the heavy repetition of the name Barnett.
