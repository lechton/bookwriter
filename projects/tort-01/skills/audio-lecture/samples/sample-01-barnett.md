# Sample 01: Barnett, Written from Scratch

Draft for the owner to review before any rule changes. It is written to be heard, not read. Notes on the choices made are at the end.

---

### Barnett: The Doctor Who Would Not Come Downstairs

A man walks into a hospital at eight o'clock in the morning. He is vomiting. He can hardly stand. The doctor on duty is upstairs, and he will not come down. He sends a message through the nurse: go home, and call your own doctor. Five and a half hours later, the man is dead. His widow sues the hospital. And she loses. She does not get a single pound.

Keep that ending in your mind, because it sounds wrong. It sounds as if the law has taken the doctor's side. By the end of this story, I think you will agree that the judge had no other choice. The man's name was William Barnett, and the case is Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington Hospital Management Committee, decided in 1969. We will call it Barnett. Hold on to that name, Barnett, because Barnett teaches the most uncomfortable lesson in the whole law of negligence.

It is New Year's Day, 1966, in Chelsea, in West London. It is five o'clock in the morning. Barnett and two other men have been awake all night. They are night-watchmen, guarding the buildings of a college, and their shift is nearly over. They make tea and drink it together.

Twenty minutes later, all three of them are being sick. Not a little sick. Violently, again and again, with terrible cramps. They think it must be something they ate. So they walk to the nearest hospital, St Stephen's, and at about eight o'clock they reach the casualty department, which is what the emergency room was called. Barnett is the worst of the three. He lies across two chairs in the waiting room, doubled over.

A nurse sees them, and she does the right thing. She telephones the casualty doctor on duty, Dr Banerjee. But Dr Banerjee is upstairs, and he feels unwell too. Last night was New Year's Eve. The nurse describes the vomiting. And the doctor gives an answer that law students still read today. Well, I am vomiting myself, and I have not been drinking. Tell them to go home and go to bed and call in their own doctors.

Nobody examines Barnett. The three men leave. And at half past one that afternoon, William Barnett is dead.

Here is what nobody knew that morning. Someone had put arsenic in the tea. Arsenic is a poison, a heavy metal that attacks the stomach and the organs. Whoever did it was never found.

Now you be the judge in Barnett. The widow says: my husband walked into your hospital asking for help, and your doctor would not even look at him. The hospital says: that is true, and it was wrong. But it made no difference. Stop for a moment and decide. Who wins in Barnett?

Most people say the widow. The doctor was careless, and nobody doubts it. So let us watch how the judge, Justice Nield, worked through Barnett, one question at a time.

First question. Did the hospital owe Barnett a duty of care? Yes. A man who walks through the open door of a casualty department asking for help is exactly the person a hospital must look after.

Second question. Did the doctor break that duty, by refusing to come down and look at three men who were vomiting? Yes. Without any doubt.

Two out of two for the widow. And then comes the third question, the one that decides Barnett. Did the doctor's carelessness cause Barnett's death?

To answer it, the court asked something very simple. Imagine the doctor had done everything right. Imagine he had run downstairs at eight o'clock. Would Barnett have lived? Lawyers call this the but for test. But for the mistake, would the harm still have happened? And the person suing must show that the answer is more likely yes than no.

So the experts in Barnett walked the court through that morning as it should have gone. A good doctor sees a man with stomach pain and vomiting. He does not think, this man has been poisoned with arsenic. Why would he? He admits him to a bed and puts him on a drip. To find arsenic, the hospital must test his blood and what is in his stomach, and those tests take hours. Only when the results come back can a doctor give the antidote, a drug called BAL. On the best possible morning, that antidote reaches Barnett around midday. And by then, the poison has already done its work. Barnett still dies at half past one.

So the answer to the third question in Barnett is no. The doctor was careless. The man died. But the carelessness did not cause the death. The arsenic did. And so the hospital paid nothing.

Now go back to the feeling you had at the start, that this sounds wrong. It sounds wrong because we want someone to punish that doctor. But punishment is the job of criminal law, and of the medical profession itself. Tort law does a different job. It makes a wrongdoer pay for the harm he caused. Not for harm someone else caused. Not for being a bad doctor in general. In Barnett, a poisoner caused the harm, and the doctor's refusal, shameful as it was, changed nothing. That is the lesson of Barnett. Being careless is not enough. The carelessness must make the difference.

And that lesson leaves a door open. Barnett works because the experts could say clearly what would have happened. But what if they cannot? What if a man breathes in deadly dust at work for years, for two different employers, and science cannot say which of them gave him his cancer? Does the widow lose again? That is the question in Fairchild.

## What This Sample Tries

- **The reason to care comes first.** The listener hears the man, the refusal, the death, and the shock result before any date, place, or legal stage. The old version opened with a paragraph on Stage One, Stage Two, and Stage Three of negligence.
- **The twist is the hook, not the ending.** The widow's loss is announced in the first paragraph as a puzzle ("it sounds wrong"), and the rest of the case solves that puzzle. The closing paragraph returns to the same feeling.
- **Background arrives when the story needs it.** Arsenic is explained only after the death, when the listener asks why. The tests and the antidote BAL come only when the court asks whether he would have lived.
- **The listener judges first.** Both sides speak in one sentence each, then the listener is asked to decide before the judge does.
- **The best line is given room.** The doctor's own words stand alone, introduced as the line law students still read.
- **The name repeats heavily.** Barnett appears about thirty times. Because Barnett is also the man in the story, every mention of him anchors the case name too.
- **Left out on purpose:** the opening recap of the stages of negligence, the comparison with speeding on an empty motorway, the phrase "balance of probabilities" (said here as "more likely yes than no"), and the detail that his own doctor sent him back by ambulance.
