# Chapter 1: The Architecture of Civil Wrongs

## 1. The Blood, the Brick, and the Bank Balance: What Tort Law Is Actually Doing

### Forget the Latin and Ask One Question: When Life Breaks, Who Pays for the Pieces?

Walk with me out of this room, step onto the pavement, and look around you. A delivery van mounts the kerb because the driver is reading a text message on his phone. High above the street, a tall crane swings a heavy steel beam over the traffic. Inside a hospital, a surgeon holds a sharp blade three millimetres from a patient's throat. And in a quiet city office, an accountant signs a report saying a company is rich when in truth that company is bankrupt. Every single second, millions of strangers live and work side by side in a crowded modern world. We drive heavy machines, we give professional advice, and we build right next to our neighbours' homes. Most of the time, nobody gets hurt. But when things go wrong, a person's health is ruined, a family house burns down, or a lifetime of savings disappears overnight.

When a disaster like that happens, different branches of the law ask very different questions. Criminal law asks whether the state should punish the wrongdoer by sending them to prison or making them pay a fine. Contract law asks whether the two people had signed a private agreement before the accident happened. Tort law asks a much more direct and urgent human question. **When two strangers have never signed a contract with each other, should the courts force the person who caused the harm to pay money to repair the victim's life, or must the injured victim bear the financial loss alone as a piece of bad luck?**

That single question is the heart of tort law. The word *tort* comes from old Norman French and simply means a wrong. Before that, it came from the Latin word *tortum*, which meant something twisted or crooked rather than straight. Now, if you try to study tort law as a random list of twenty or thirty court cases to memorise before your summer exams, you will quickly feel lost. If you hear the subject for what it really is, though, you will remember every case with ease. British tort law is one continuous conversation across two hundred and fifty years about where to draw the line between your freedom to act and your neighbour's right to stay safe. Every case in this chapter happened because an older rule failed to solve a real human dispute. Every judge we will meet is trying to balance two competing dangers. **How do we give fair compensation to injured victims without burying ordinary citizens, businesses, and public services under an endless mountain of lawsuits?**

Think of these cases not as isolated flashcards, but as a relay race. You will hear how a judge in 1932 solved an injustice created in 1842, and how the Supreme Court in 2018 corrected a widespread misunderstanding of a case from 1990. Once you hear how the historical and legal dots connect, the whole architecture of tort law locks into place.

## 2. The Sacred Fortress: Trespass, State Power, and the Old World of Direct Wrongs

### Before Negligence Existed, the Common Law Guarded Two Things Above All Else: Your Body and Your Land

To understand where tort law begins, come back with me to the eighteenth century, long before factories, steam trains, or motor cars existed. In that early world of farms and small shops, English tort law was not mainly worried about accidental carelessness. Instead, it was obsessed with **physical boundaries**. A citizen's physical body and private home were treated as sacred fortresses, guarded by one of the oldest remedies in the English courts, called the writ of **trespass**. Under the rule of trespass, if someone crossed your physical boundary without your permission, the court did not care whether they meant well or whether they broke anything inside. Crossing the boundary line without lawful permission *was* the legal wrong.

To see why that old rule still protects your freedom today, picture London in the autumn of 1762. King George the Third had recently come to the throne, and his government ministers were deeply unpopular. There were no modern police forces at the time, and the government was terrified of political critics who used printing presses to publish weekly pamphlets attacking the King's policies. One of those writers was a schoolmaster and author named John Entick, who wrote for an opposition paper called *The Monitor, or British Freeholder*. Determined to silence him, the Secretary of State, Lord Halifax, signed what was called a general warrant. That was an executive order written by a government minister rather than a court judge. Lord Halifax sent four royal officers, led by Nathan Carrington, to raid the writer's home in Stepney. This came to court in **Entick v Carrington**, in 1765. Let's call it Entick. In Entick, the four officers broke into the house in the middle of the day. For four hours, they searched every private room, broke open locked desks and boxes, and carried away hundreds of personal papers and books. When the homeowner in Entick sued the officers for trespassing on his land, the government's lawyers gave a bold reply. They argued that government ministers had always signed warrants like this, and that the state needed this power to protect national security and keep the peace. Now ask yourself the question the court had to answer. **Can government officials enter a citizen's private home and take their private papers without a clear Act of Parliament or established common law rule giving them that exact legal power?**

Lord Camden, the Chief Justice in Entick, gave one of the most famous answers in the history of the common law, and his answer was a firm **no**. In Entick, he drew a permanent line between the freedom of an ordinary citizen and the limited power of the state. An ordinary citizen is free to do anything unless a law forbids it. A government official, by contrast, may do *nothing* unless a specific law, either an Act of Parliament or a clear common law rule, expressly gives them authority to do it. Lord Camden held in Entick that state necessity is never a valid legal excuse for invading a citizen's home. Even if the King's officers had not broken a single lock or scratched a single wooden board, the exact moment their boots stepped across the doorway without a lawful warrant, they committed the tort of trespass to land, which old lawyers called *trespass quare clausum fregit*, meaning trespass by breaking into another person's enclosed land. So Entick shows us that tort law is much more than a system for traffic accidents. It is the foundation stone of British constitutional liberty and the Rule of Law.

![Entick v Carrington (1765): Lord Halifax signs a general warrant, the King's officers break into John Entick's house to seize his private papers, and Lord Camden CJ holds them liable in trespass under the Rule of Law.](../images/01/07_Entick.jpeg)

### What Happens When Someone Hurts You Without Touching You or Stepping onto Your Property?

The tort of trespass in Entick works wonderfully when an official kicks down your door or a stranger punches you in the street. Trespass is what lawyers call **actionable *per se***, a Latin phrase meaning actionable in itself. You win the case simply by proving that the boundary was crossed, without needing to prove a single penny of financial loss or physical injury. Yet this old rule had a serious weakness. To count as trespass, the interference had to be **direct** and physical.

As cities grew larger in the late nineteenth century, the courts faced a difficult new problem. What if someone harms you terribly without ever touching your body or stepping onto your land without permission? Picture East London in the spring of 1897, in the case of **Wilkinson v Downton**, decided in 1897. We'll call it Wilkinson. In Wilkinson, a husband and wife ran the Albion public house in Limehouse. One day, the husband went off by train to watch the horse races in Harlow. That afternoon, a regular customer named Thomas Downton walked into the pub and decided to play what he thought was a clever practical joke on the landlady. With a completely serious face, he told her that her husband had just been thrown from a horse-drawn wagon on his way back from the races, that he was lying injured at a pub in Leytonstone with both of his legs broken, and that she needed to take two pillows in a horse-drawn cab right away to bring him home. Every word was a lie. Now notice the legal problem in Wilkinson. The customer had not touched her, and because he was standing in a public bar, he was not trespassing on her land. Yet the mental shock of hearing that her husband was crippled was so severe that the landlady in Wilkinson suffered weeks of violent vomiting, her hair turned permanently white, and she suffered a serious nervous breakdown that nearly drove her mad. So here is the question. **Can tort law hold a defendant responsible when they deliberately tell a false story that causes severe physical and psychological illness, even though they never physically touched the victim?**

In Wilkinson, the trial judge, Justice Wright, refused to let the prankster escape punishment simply because his cruel act did not fit the old categories. Battery, which is the tort of unlawful physical contact, did not apply in Wilkinson because he never touched her. Deceit, which is the tort of commercial fraud, did not fit neatly either, because fraud was designed for cases where someone tricks you into losing money rather than suffering a medical breakdown. So in Wilkinson, Justice Wright established a vital new rule for intentional indirect harm. Where a defendant **wilfully, meaning deliberately, does an act that is calculated, meaning plainly likely, to cause physical harm** to another person, and that act actually causes physical illness or a recognised psychiatric disorder, the law treats the defendant as intending to cause that harm. Spoken words can strike as hard as a physical blow, and Wilkinson proves that tort law will hold the speaker liable when a deliberate lie shatters a victim's health. In modern law, the UK Supreme Court in **Rhodes v OPO**, in 2015, which we can call Rhodes, confirmed that the rule in Wilkinson requires both a conduct element, meaning words or acts directed at the victim without justification, and a mental element, meaning an actual intention to cause severe distress or physical harm.

![Wilkinson v Downton (1897): Downton tells Mrs Wilkinson the false story at the Albion pub that her husband broke both legs in a wagon crash, causing immediate nervous shock and weeks of severe physical and mental illness.](../images/01/03_Wilkinson.jpeg)

> **Revision:** In this first block we saw how the old law protected boundaries and how it adapted when harm came through words instead of force. First, in Entick, from 1765, royal officers broke into a writer's house under a minister's warrant. Because trespass is actionable in itself, crossing the doorway without lawful authority was a tort even without proving damage, and state necessity was no excuse. Second, in Wilkinson, from 1897, a cruel lie in a pub caused a terrifying breakdown without a single physical touch. Because the harm was indirect, actual illness had to be proved, and deliberate words plainly likely to cause harm created liability, as later confirmed in Rhodes, from 2015. But what happens when nobody intends any harm at all, and an accident happens simply because someone was careless? That is the question we turn to in Vaughan, from 1837.

## 3. The Industrial Revolution: Creating the "Reasonable Person" and the Trap of Contract

### Why "I Did My Best" Is the Worst Excuse in a Courtroom

When the Industrial Revolution transformed Britain in the early nineteenth century, the old system of direct trespass could no longer handle the new world. Across the country, coal mines were dug deep underground, steam engines were built in crowded towns, horse-drawn mail coaches raced along narrow roads, and closely packed cottages stood beside farms and workshops. Most of the accidents in this new world were not deliberate attacks like Wilkinson. They were caused by **carelessness**. As the modern tort of **negligence** began to take shape in the 1830s, judges immediately had to answer a fundamental question about human nature. How should the law judge a careless person?

Come back with me to Shropshire in the summer of 1835, to **Vaughan v Menlove**, decided in 1837. We'll call it Vaughan. In nineteenth-century farming, farmers harvested grass in the summer and stacked it into giant outdoor piles called hayricks to feed their animals during the winter. Every experienced farmer knew a basic danger of biology and chemistry. If you stack hay while it is still wet or damp, bacteria inside the tightly packed grass begin to ferment. That fermentation traps intense heat inside the pile until the hay catches fire all by itself, a process called spontaneous combustion. In Vaughan, a farmer built a large stack of damp hay right next to the boundary fence where his neighbour owned two small country cottages. For five weeks, the damp haystack steamed and grew hotter. Neighbours warned the farmer in Vaughan again and again that the hay was going to burst into flames and burn down the cottages next door. He stubbornly refused to take the pile down, telling a neighbour that his hay was insured and that he would chance it. Even worse, in a clumsy attempt to cool the pile down, he cut an air hole right through the middle of the haystack. That hole let fresh oxygen rush into the hot centre, acting just like a chimney in a furnace. The haystack exploded into flames, spread to the barn, and burned the neighbour's cottages to the ground. When the lawsuit in Vaughan came to court, the farmer's lawyer made a remarkable argument. He admitted that his client did not have the highest intelligence, and argued that the jury should only ask whether the man had acted honestly to the best of his own limited ability. After all, how could the law blame a slow-witted man for not being cleverer than nature made him? Now stop and think about that question. **In the tort of negligence, is a defendant judged by whether they did their personal best, or by an objective standard of how an ordinary, sensible person would behave?**

Chief Justice Tindal and the Court of Common Pleas in Vaughan rejected that excuse forever. Chief Justice Tindal explained that if legal responsibility depended on how intelligent or careful each person happened to be, the law would be as variable as the length of each person's foot. A court cannot look inside every person's head to measure their intelligence. More importantly, tort law is not a religious court judging whether your heart was pure. It is a practical system that sets a minimum standard of safety for everyone living in society. Whether you are clever or slow-witted, the moment you do something that affects your neighbours, Vaughan says the law measures your conduct against the **objective standard of a person of ordinary prudence**, meaning ordinary carefulness and common sense. Because an ordinary sensible person would never have left a steaming haystack beside a neighbour's house for five weeks, the farmer in Vaughan was liable.

![Vaughan v Menlove (1837): Menlove cuts an air hole through his steaming damp haystack, feeding oxygen into the fermenting hay until flames spread across the boundary fence and burn down Vaughan's cottages.](../images/01/02_vaughan.jpeg)

### Does the "Reasonable Person" Have to Predict Record-Breaking Natural Disasters?

Once Vaughan established in 1837 that everyone is judged by an objective standard, companies and property owners became nervous. Does acting like a reasonable person mean that you are automatically at fault whenever your equipment breaks and damages someone's property?

Nineteen years later, the courts answered that question in the industrial city of Birmingham, in **Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co**, in 1856. Let's call it Blyth. To understand Blyth, we first need to picture how water and firefighting worked in a Victorian city in the 1850s. Growing cities were digging up their streets to lay cast-iron pipes that carried pressurized water under the ground. Because modern above-ground fire hydrants did not exist yet, Parliament required water companies to install a device called a **fireplug** every hundred yards along the underground main pipe. A fireplug was a wooden or iron stopper pushed into a vertical pipe about eighteen inches beneath the street surface, covered by an iron box in the road. When a nearby house caught fire, firefighters would pry open the street cover and pull out the plug so water would gush up into their fire engines. Around the neck of the plug, there was a small gap that allowed any leaking water to drain harmlessly into the earth. In Blyth, the water company installed a fireplug outside a homeowner's house in Birmingham according to the best engineering standards of the day, and for twenty-five years it worked without a single problem. Then came the winter of 1855, which brought one of the most extreme, record-breaking freezes in British history. The ground froze solid to a depth nobody in Birmingham had ever seen. Deep underground, thick ice formed around the collar of the fireplug, forced the stopper loose, and blocked the drainage gap on the surface. Pressurized water had nowhere to go, so it forced its way through the soil under the pavement and flooded the house in Blyth, ruining the furniture inside. The homeowner in Blyth sued the water company for negligence, arguing that they should have cleared away the underground ice. **Does a defendant breach their duty of care when an extreme, unprecedented natural event causes properly built and maintained equipment to fail?**

In Blyth, Baron Alderson gave the classic definition of negligence that every law student in the English-speaking world still learns by heart. **Negligence is failing to do something which a reasonable person, guided by the considerations that ordinarily regulate human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable person would not do.** Notice how Blyth balances Vaughan. The reasonable person is careful and sensible, not a fortune-teller who can predict record-breaking miracles or extreme freaks of nature. Because the fireplug in Blyth had worked safely for twenty-five years and no ordinary engineer could have anticipated the extreme freeze of 1855, the water company had done nothing unreasonable and owed nothing. You should always pair Vaughan and Blyth together. Vaughan proves you cannot drop below the ordinary person's standard by claiming you were clumsy or slow, while Blyth proves you are not required to rise above the ordinary person's standard by predicting unforeseeable disasters.

![Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co (1856): Record-breaking frost freezes the ground 18 inches beneath the iron street cover, blocking the underground fireplug with ice and forcing escaping water into Mr Blyth's house.](../images/01/01_Blyth.jpeg)

### When You Are Completely Innocent of Carelessness, Can Your Land Still Make You Liable?

Now pause and think about the next logical problem. Under Blyth, if you hire skilled engineers and do nothing careless yourself, you are not guilty of negligence. What if, however, you are a wealthy factory owner who brings a huge, dangerous quantity of water or chemicals onto your land to power your business, and that dangerous material escapes and ruins your neighbour's livelihood?

That exact clash happened in Lancashire during the height of the Victorian industrial boom in **Rylands v Fletcher**, in 1868. We'll call it Rylands. In the 1860s, Lancashire was the workshop of the world, packed with two competing industries lying side by side. On the surface stood cotton textile mills, which needed huge amounts of water to power their steam engines. Deep underground lay coal mines, which provided the coal to burn in those engines. In Rylands, a mill owner had a large cotton mill in Ainsworth, while his neighbour leased and worked the coal mines under the land next door. To get more water for his steam mill, the mill owner in Rylands hired experienced, reputable engineers and contractors to dig and build a giant artificial water reservoir on land next to the mines. The mill owner himself took no part in the digging and committed zero personal carelessness. While digging the reservoir bed, however, the contractors uncovered five old vertical coal-mine shafts that had been dug generations earlier, abandoned, and filled in with loose soil and rubbish. Nobody knew that those forgotten shafts connected underground to the neighbour's working coal mine, though the contractors were careless in not testing whether the shafts could hold water pressure. In December 1860, the reservoir in Rylands was filled with millions of gallons of water. The weight of the water burst straight through the loose dirt in the old shafts, rushed through the underground tunnels, and flooded the neighbour's coal mine, forcing him to shut down his business. Under Blyth, the mill owner in Rylands had not been careless at all. So the court faced a major question. **Should a landowner be held strictly liable, meaning legally responsible even without personal fault, when they bring a large, unnatural accumulation onto their land and it escapes to damage a neighbour's property?**

In the Court of Exchequer Chamber in Rylands, Justice Blackburn gave a landmark answer, which was upheld by the House of Lords with an important refinement by Lord Cairns, the Lord Chancellor. The answer was **yes**. Justice Blackburn declared in Rylands that any person who, for their own purposes, brings onto their land and collects and keeps there anything likely to cause harm if it escapes, must keep it in at their own risk. If it escapes, the landowner is **strictly liable** for all the damage that is the natural consequence of its escape, even if the landowner was personally as careful as possible, and they cannot escape responsibility by blaming the independent builders they hired. In the House of Lords in Rylands, Lord Cairns added the famous requirement that the landowner must be making a **non-natural use** of the land, meaning an extraordinary, unusual use of land, like storing a vast artificial lake of water, rather than an ordinary natural use like mining coal or keeping household water pipes. In modern times, the House of Lords in **Transco plc v Stockport Metropolitan Borough Council**, in 2003, which we can call Transco, confirmed that the rule in Rylands is today treated as a special branch of the tort of **private nuisance**, and applies only when a landowner brings something onto the land for an **extraordinary and unusual use** that creates an exceptionally high risk if it escapes.

![Rylands v Fletcher (1868): Water collected in Rylands's artificial mill reservoir bursts downward through forgotten, loosely filled coal shafts and floods Fletcher's neighbouring coal mine.](../images/01/04_Rylands.jpeg)

> **Revision:** In this block we saw how the Industrial Revolution forced the courts to measure carelessness and land hazards across three foundational cases. First, in Vaughan, from 1837, a steaming haystack burned a neighbour's cottages, and the court set the objective standard of the ordinary sensible person, where saying you did your best is no excuse. Second, in Blyth, from 1856, a record-breaking frost burst an underground fireplug, proving that a reasonable person only guards against foreseeable risks, not freaks of nature. Third, in Rylands, from 1868, a giant water reservoir flooded a neighbour's coal mine without the owner's personal fault, creating strict liability for an extraordinary use of land when a gathered hazard escapes, as later confirmed in Transco, from 2003. But while landowners faced these rules, what happened when defective machinery injured a working man who had no contract with the repairer? That brings us to the harsh roadblock in Winterbottom, from 1842.

### The 90-Year Trap of Privity: How a Broken Mail-Coach Axle Locked Injured Victims Out of Court

While judges were working out the rules for landowners in Vaughan, Blyth, and Rylands, a much harsher legal rule was blocking injured workers and ordinary shoppers from winning in court. To understand **Winterbottom v Wright**, decided in 1842, which we'll call Winterbottom, you first need to picture how long-distance travel and mail worked in Britain in the early 1840s, just before railways connected the whole country. At that time, the Royal Mail was carried between cities in heavy, four-wheeled horse-drawn carriages called **mail coaches**, pulled at high speed by teams of four horses along bumpy turnpike roads. The government head of the postal service, the Postmaster General, did not employ every coach builder and driver directly. Instead, business ran through a chain of separate contracts. First, the Postmaster General signed a contract with a carriage specialist to supply mail coaches and keep them safe and in good mechanical repair. Second, the Postmaster General signed a completely separate contract with a coaching firm called Atkinson and Birch to supply the horses and the coachmen, the drivers, to pull those mail coaches between Hartford and Holyhead. In Winterbottom, the injured man was one of the coachmen employed by Atkinson to sit high on the front box and drive the horses.

Now look at the mechanical failure that ruined the driver's life in Winterbottom. Underneath a four-wheeled mail coach sat two heavy iron rods called **axles**. Each axle connected a left wheel to a right wheel and carried the entire weight of the wooden carriage, the passengers, the heavy mailbags, and the driver as the coach bounced over rough stones at ten miles an hour. In Winterbottom, the repair contractor and his mechanics did careless, poor-quality maintenance work on the coach. Inside the coach's iron axle, there was what engineers and lawyers call a **latent defect**, meaning a hidden physical flaw or crack inside the metal that an ordinary driver sitting on the coach could not see from the outside, but that a proper mechanic inspecting the carriage should have found or prevented. Because the contractor in Winterbottom failed to inspect and repair the coach properly, the iron axle suddenly snapped in two while the driver was driving the coach along the road. The heavy carriage collapsed and overturned, throwing the driver violently to the ground and leaving him crippled for the rest of his life. Because his own employer had not built the coach, the driver in Winterbottom sued the maintenance contractor in tort for negligence. Look closely at the contractor's defence in Winterbottom. He argued that his contract to keep the coach safe was signed only with the Postmaster General. Because the driver was not a party to that contract, meaning in legal language there was no **privity of contract** between them, the contractor claimed he owed zero legal duty to the driver who actually sat on the coach. **When a contractor carelessly supplies or maintains a dangerous piece of equipment under a contract with Customer A, can an injured third party, Victim B, who never signed a contract with the contractor, sue the contractor in tort for their physical injuries?**

Today, every instinct tells us that the driver in Winterbottom should have won. In 1842, however, Lord Abinger, the Chief Baron, and the Court of Exchequer looked at the growing world of early factories and trade, imagined thousands of strangers suing coach builders and manufacturers after every accident, and panicked. In Winterbottom, they ruled **no**. Lord Abinger held that if someone who was not a party to a contract could sue in tort for injuries caused by the careless performance of that contract, it would lead to the most absurd and outrageous consequences, because there would be no limit to who could sue whenever a carriage broke down. This disastrous mistake in Winterbottom became known as the **privity fallacy**, the false belief that if one person has a contract with a second person, then a third person cannot sue the first in tort for doing the job carelessly. For the next ninety years, Winterbottom acted as an iron wall protecting careless manufacturers. If a factory made poisoned food or a defective tool and sold it to a shopkeeper, and the shopkeeper sold it to a husband who gave it to his wife or child, the injured wife or child had no contract with the factory and was locked out of court.

![Winterbottom v Wright (1842): Separate contracts leave the mail-coach driver outside privity with the maintenance contractor, so when the cracked iron axle snaps on the road, the Court of Exchequer denies his claim.](../images/01/05_Winterbottom.jpeg)

Forty-one years later, one brilliant judge tried to break down the wall of Winterbottom in the busy shipyards of East London. This came to court in **Heaven v Pender**, in 1883. Let's call it Heaven. To understand Heaven, picture how commercial shipping worked on the River Thames in the 1880s. Iron and wooden steamships carried cargo across the British Empire, and after months at sea, their outer hulls needed to be scraped clean and repainted inside a **dry dock**, which is a narrow basin where the water is pumped out so the ship rests on wooden blocks. Because the sides of a ship are tall and curved, workers could not simply stand on the ground to paint the top of the hull. Instead, the owner of the dry dock supplied and hung a piece of equipment called **staging** around the outside of the ship. The staging was a long, heavy wooden plank where painters stood high in the air, held up by two thick ropes that passed over the top rail of the ship and tied down to the deck. In Heaven, a shipowner brought his ship into the dry dock and paid the dock owner to rent the dock and the rope-hung wooden staging. The shipowner then hired a master painter named Gray to paint the ship, and Gray employed a working ship painter to climb onto the wooden plank and do the painting. Notice the chain of contracts in Heaven, just like the mail coach in Winterbottom. The dock owner had a contract with the shipowner, the shipowner had a contract with Gray, and Gray employed the painter. The painter in Heaven had **no contract at all** with the dock owner. Before the staging was put up, one of the dock owner's thick ropes had been badly scorched and burned by fire, leaving the fibres inside the rope weak and rotten. The moment the painter in Heaven stepped onto the high wooden plank to paint the ship, the burned rope snapped, the wooden stage dropped from under his feet, and he fell into the bottom of the dry dock, suffering severe injuries.

When the injured painter in Heaven sued the dock owner, all three judges in the Court of Appeal agreed that the painter should win his case, yet they disagreed on the legal reason. **The majority of the Court of Appeal, Lord Justice Cotton and Lord Justice Bowen, ruled in favour of the injured painter in Heaven on a narrow property-law point.** They held that because the painter came onto the dock premises to do work that was part of the dock owner's business, the painter counted in law as an **invited visitor**, or invitee, and property owners already owed a legal duty to make sure their premises and equipment were safe for invited visitors. However, the third judge in Heaven, **Brett, the Master of the Rolls, later known as Lord Esher, saw the bigger picture**. He realised that a worker's right to safety should not depend on narrow technical rules about whether he was an invited visitor on land. In a famous minority opinion in Heaven, Brett proposed the first general test for a **duty of care** in English law. Whenever one person is placed in such a position with regard to another person that anyone of ordinary sense would recognise that if they did not use ordinary care and skill, they would cause danger of injury to that other person's body or property, a legal duty arises to use ordinary care and skill to avoid that danger, even without a contract between them. In 1883, that idea in Heaven was fifty years ahead of its time. The majority judges thought his general rule was too wide, so the privity trap of Winterbottom largely survived until a decomposing snail floated out of a dark bottle in a Scottish café in 1928.

![Heaven v Pender (1883): A scorched staging rope supplied by the dry-dock owner snaps beneath the ship painter, and the Court of Appeal awards damages while Brett MR formulates the first general duty of care.](../images/01/06_Heaven.jpeg)

> **Revision:** In this block we saw how contract law blocked injured victims from suing in tort for nearly a century. First, in Winterbottom, from 1842, a hidden crack in an iron mail-coach axle crippled the driver, but the court locked him out because he had no contract with the repairer, creating the ninety-year privity fallacy. Second, in Heaven, from 1883, a burned shipyard rope snapped under a painter who also had no contract with the dock owner. While the majority let him win only as an invited visitor on land, the minority judge proposed a general duty of care whenever ordinary sense shows that carelessness will endanger someone else. How did that minority seed finally grow into the rule that governs modern tort law? That happens in Donoghue, from 1932.

## 4. The Paisley Snail and the Modern Duty of Care: From Neighbourhood to Incrementalism

### How a Decomposing Snail in a Bottle of Ginger Beer Shattered 90 Years of Legal Immunity

By the late 1920s, the way ordinary people bought food and drink had completely changed from the world of 1842. In the nineteenth century, customers usually bought flour, milk, or ale from open barrels in a local shop where both the shopkeeper and the buyer could look inside and see if the product was clean. By 1928, however, factories were mass-producing food and drinks inside **sealed containers, tins, and dark bottles** that were shipped across the country to thousands of small shops and cafés. Neither the café owner nor the customer could see inside a sealed, dark bottle before it was opened and poured. Yet under the old rule of Winterbottom, if someone bought a sealed bottle as a gift for a friend or family member, the person who drank it had no contract with the shopkeeper and no contract with the factory that made it.

That was the exact situation on the warm Sunday evening of 26 August 1928, in the case that became **Donoghue v Stevenson**, decided in 1932. We'll call it Donoghue. On that Sunday evening in Donoghue, a shop assistant travelled from Glasgow to the town of Paisley and walked into the Wellmeadow Café with a friend. Her friend ordered and paid for a pear and ice, which was ice cream with pear, and a bottle of ginger beer for her. The café owner, Francis Minghella, brought a bowl of ice cream and a bottle of ginger beer made by a local manufacturer named David Stevenson. Crucially, the bottle in Donoghue was made of **dark, opaque glass**, glass so dark that nobody could see through it, and sealed at the top with a metal cap. Minghella opened the bottle and poured some of the ginger beer over the ice cream. The woman in Donoghue drank part of her glass. While she was enjoying it, her friend picked up the dark bottle to pour the rest of the ginger beer into her glass. Out of the dark bottle floated the rotting, decomposing body of a dead snail, which had crawled into the empty bottle in the factory yard before the bottle was filled and sealed. Seeing the rotting snail and realising she had already swallowed the dirty liquid caused the woman in Donoghue severe nervous shock and painful gastro-enteritis, meaning terrible stomach inflammation that required hospital treatment. Now look at the legal trap that faced her lawyers in Donoghue. She could not sue the café owner in contract under the Sale of Goods Act because **her friend had bought and paid for the drink**. And under Winterbottom, she could not sue the manufacturer because she had never signed a contract with him either. **Does a manufacturer owe a legal duty of care in tort to the final consumer of a product when there is no contract between them and no reasonable possibility of inspecting the product before it is consumed?**

In Donoghue, by a narrow three to two majority in the House of Lords, Lord Atkin swept away the ninety-year barrier of Winterbottom and created the modern tort of negligence. Building directly on the minority judgment of Brett in Heaven, Lord Atkin asked in Donoghue how the common law could bring all the scattered duties of care under one clear moral and legal principle. Turning to the famous moral rule that you must love your neighbour, Lord Atkin asked the legal question. **Who, in law, is my neighbour?**

His answer in Donoghue is the most famous passage in the history of the common law. **You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour.** And who is your neighbour? In Donoghue, Lord Atkin defined neighbours as **persons who are so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation, meaning in my mind, as being so affected when I am directing my mind to the acts or omissions which are called in question.** Notice that this famous **Neighbour Principle** in Donoghue rests on two ideas working together, which are **reasonable foreseeability** of harm and **proximity**, meaning the closeness and directness of the relationship between the two parties. Alongside this broad principle, Lord Atkin also laid down a specific rule for defective products, often called the **Narrow Ratio** of Donoghue. A manufacturer of products who sells them in a form which shows that they are meant to reach the final consumer in the exact same form in which they left the factory, with no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination, meaning checking the product along the way, owes a duty to the consumer to take reasonable care to prevent injury to their health or property. From the day Donoghue was decided, negligence stood on its own feet, completely independent of contract law.

### When Young Offenders Escape from an Island and Steal a Boat, Can You Sue the Government for What Third Parties Do?

Once Donoghue freed negligence from contract law in 1932, lawyers began testing how far Lord Atkin's Neighbour Principle could reach. It is easy to see why a factory owner is responsible when his own workers leave a dead snail in a bottle. What happens, though, when the defendant does not damage your property with their own hands, but instead carelessly allows a **third party**, another person, to commit a crime that ruins your property?

That question came to court in **Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd**, in 1970. Let's call it Dorset Yacht. To understand Dorset Yacht, we need to understand two things about Britain in the 1960s, which are the youth prison system and the geography of Poole Harbour on the south coast of England. At that time, young offenders between the ages of fifteen and twenty-one who had committed crimes were sent to state detention centres called **Borstals**, named after the village of Borstal in Kent where the first centre opened. Unlike locked adult prisons with high stone walls, open Borstals tried to reform troubled youths through hard outdoor work, discipline, and trust. In September 1962, in Dorset Yacht, three prison officers employed by the Home Office, the government department responsible for prisons, took ten Borstal trainees to **Brownsea Island**, an island sitting right in the middle of Poole Harbour. Poole Harbour is one of the largest natural harbours in the world, packed with hundreds of expensive private sailing boats, or yachts, anchored in the water around the island. In fact, the only realistic way to escape from Brownsea Island at night is by stealing a boat. Every single one of the seven boys involved in Dorset Yacht had a criminal record for breaking and entering or stealing vehicles, and five of them had already escaped from Borstals in the past. Despite written orders to guard the boys carefully at night, the three prison officers in Dorset Yacht simply went to bed and left the trainees completely unsupervised. During the night, seven boys escaped from the building, walked down to the harbour, climbed aboard a motor yacht named the *Silver Mist* that was anchored just off the island, started its engine, and clumsily steered it straight into another luxury yacht belonging to the Dorset Yacht Company, causing heavy financial damage. When the yacht owners in Dorset Yacht sued the Home Office, the government's lawyers argued that under English law, one person is never legally responsible for crimes committed by another free human being, and warned that if courts allowed citizens to sue the prison service after every escape, officers would abandon open training programmes and lock every youth in a cell. **Does a government department owe a legal duty of care to nearby property owners when its officers carelessly allow detainees under their direct supervision to escape and cause obvious, foreseeable damage?**

Lord Reid and the House of Lords in Dorset Yacht ruled **yes**. Normally, the common law does not hold one person liable for a pure **omission**, meaning a failure to act, or for deliberate damage caused by a **third party** hurting someone else. However, Lord Reid explained in Dorset Yacht that an exception applies when two conditions are met. First, the defendant stands in a **special relationship of control** over the wrongdoer, and here the officers had legal custody and control over the Borstal trainees. Second, the third party's harmful act was not just a remote possibility, but the **very kind of thing that was extremely likely to happen** if the officers failed to watch them. Because the yachts in Dorset Yacht were anchored right beside an island with no bridge to the mainland, stealing and crashing a boat was the most obvious natural result of letting escaped car thieves roam free at night. For this reason, the duty of care in Dorset Yacht was strictly limited to **proximate victims**, meaning owners of property right next to the island facing an immediate risk during the escape, not someone robbed by an escapee two hundred miles away a month later. Furthermore, Lord Diplock in Dorset Yacht connected the case directly back to Entick. While courts will not interfere with high-level government policy decisions, such as whether to run open Borstals, once prison officers on the ground act so carelessly that they step completely outside their instructions, the government can be sued in tort for operational negligence just like any private citizen.

> **Revision:** In this chapter we study how the law protects people from harm when there is no contract between them. We saw how the contract wall in Winterbottom, from 1842, and the early reform in Heaven, from 1883, led to the breakthrough in Donoghue, from 1932, where a snail in a dark ginger-beer bottle established the Neighbour Principle based on foreseeability and proximity. Then, in Dorset Yacht, from 1970, we saw that while the law rarely punishes omissions or crimes by third parties, a duty of care arises when officers have direct control over detainees and their escape causes the very kind of damage to nearby property that was plain to foresee. But notice that both Donoghue and Dorset Yacht involved physical harm to a person's stomach or a person's boat. What happens when no body is hurt and no boat is scratched, and someone loses money in their bank account simply because they trusted careless words? That is the question in Hedley Byrne, from 1964.

### What Happens When Someone Loses Money Because of Careless Words Rather Than Careless Hands?

Pause for a moment and look at every negligence case we have heard so far. We had burned cottages in Vaughan, a smashed mail coach in Winterbottom, a flooded coal mine in Rylands, stomach illness in Donoghue, and damaged boats in Dorset Yacht. Every single one of those cases involved **physical damage** to a person's body or physical property. What happens when nobody's body is hurt and no physical object is broken, and instead a defendant writes or speaks a **careless statement** that causes someone to lose money in their bank account, which lawyers call **pure economic loss**?

To hear why the law treats careless words and pure financial loss so carefully, look at how business credit worked in London in the late 1950s in **Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd**, decided in 1964. We'll call it Hedley Byrne. In the commercial advertising world at that time, when a manufacturing company wanted to run advertisements on television and in newspapers, it hired an advertising agency. Under the standard business practice of the day, the advertising agency did not just design the adverts. The agency had to sign contracts in its own name with the television stations and newspapers, making the agency personally responsible for paying the huge advertising bills even if its client later went bankrupt. In Hedley Byrne, a London advertising agency was hired by a company called Easipower, which sold electric household appliances. Before the agency in Hedley Byrne committed itself to paying one hundred thousand pounds for television and newspaper space on Easipower's behalf, it wanted to be certain that Easipower had enough money in the bank to pay its bills. Because a company's bank balance is private, the agency in Hedley Byrne asked its own bank, National Provincial Bank, to send a confidential inquiry to Easipower's merchant bank, Heller & Partners, asking whether Easipower was financially trustworthy. The client's bank wrote back a free letter giving a favourable credit reference, stating that Easipower was a respectably constituted company, considered good for its ordinary business engagements. However, right at the top of that letter in Hedley Byrne, the bank printed a clear warning, called a **disclaimer**, which said, *"For your private use and without responsibility on the part of this bank or its officials."* Relying on the bank's positive words, the agency in Hedley Byrne booked the advertisements. Shortly afterwards, Easipower collapsed into liquidation, meaning it went bankrupt, and the agency in Hedley Byrne lost over seventeen thousand pounds, worth several hundred thousand pounds today. Because the agency had never paid the bank a fee, there was no contract between them, and because the bank had not lied deliberately, there was no fraud. **Can a defendant ever owe a duty of care in tort for a careless statement, a negligent misstatement, that causes pure financial loss when there is no contract between the parties?**

In a landmark ruling in Hedley Byrne, the House of Lords explained why careless words are far more dangerous to the legal system than careless physical actions. If a factory makes one defective bottle of ginger beer, as in Donoghue, it makes only one person sick. Words, however, travel instantaneously and endlessly. If a financial expert makes one careless remark in a newspaper or on the radio, thousands of strangers whom the speaker has never met might read it, invest their money, and lose millions of pounds before the end of the day. To prevent this **floodgates** problem, meaning the danger of exposing a speaker to unlimited lawsuits from an unknown number of people, the House of Lords ruled in Hedley Byrne that simple foreseeability of harm under Donoghue is **never enough** to create a duty of care for pure economic loss. Instead, Hedley Byrne held that a duty of care for a negligent misstatement arises **only where there is a special relationship** between the speaker and the listener, almost as close as a contract. That special relationship in Hedley Byrne rests on two essential pillars. The first pillar is a **voluntary assumption of responsibility** by the advisor, who uses their special skill or knowledge to give advice knowing the other person is counting on them. The second pillar is **reasonable reliance** on that advice by the person who receives it. Why, then, did the advertising agency in Hedley Byrne actually lose the case on the day? Because the bank had stamped *"without responsibility"* right at the top of its letter. You cannot say a bank voluntarily assumed responsibility in Hedley Byrne when the bank explicitly told you from the start that it refused to accept responsibility.

### Corporate Takeovers, Lloyd's Underwriting Crises, and Why Stock-Market Investors Were Stopped in Caparo

Once Hedley Byrne opened the door in 1964 to suing professional advisors for careless statements, commercial lawyers and wealthy investors tried to push that door wide open. During the financial boom of the 1970s and 1980s, especially after a case called **Anns v Merton London Borough Council**, in 1978, which we can call Anns, suggested a broad two-stage test for all negligence claims, investors began treating tort law as a free insurance policy whenever a risky business investment went wrong.

Think about how big-money finance and risk operated in Britain in the 1980s and early 1990s. In two famous arenas, the London Stock Exchange and the **Lloyd's of London insurance market**, wealthy individuals and corporations were making huge fortunes by taking calculated financial risks. At Lloyd's of London, for instance, wealthy private individuals known as **Names** joined insurance syndicates to **underwrite** global risks. That meant they signed their names beneath insurance policies covering ships, aeroplanes, oil rigs, and American factories, promising to use their entire personal wealth to pay out if a disaster happened, in exchange for collecting rich yearly profits when no disasters occurred. For decades, underwriting made those investors very rich. Similarly, on the London Stock Exchange, corporate takeover specialists, often called corporate raiders, made fortunes by spotting struggling companies whose shares were cheap, buying up a majority of the shares to take control of the company, and selling off or reorganising the business for a profit. When a sudden financial crisis struck, whether a wave of asbestos and hurricane claims at Lloyd's, which later produced the famous assumption-of-responsibility lawsuit in **Henderson v Merrett Syndicates Ltd**, in 1995, let's call it Henderson, or a corporate takeover that turned out to be a money-losing disaster, investors who had gladly pocketed profits in the good years immediately looked around for someone with deep pockets, such as the company's **accountants and auditors**, to sue in tort so they could jump off the sinking ship.

That was the exact commercial drama behind **Caparo Industries plc v Dickman**, decided in 1990. We'll call it Caparo. In Caparo, a British company called Fidelity manufactured electrical equipment such as televisions, radios, and tape recorders. By 1984, Fidelity was struggling badly against foreign competition, and its share price dropped on the stock market. Every public company in Britain is required by the Companies Act to hire independent accountants, called **statutory auditors**, to check the company's financial books once a year and publish an audited annual report. In Caparo, Fidelity's auditors, a firm of accountants led by Mr Dickman at Touche Ross, audited Fidelity's 1984 accounts and certified that the company had made a healthy pre-tax profit of 1.3 million pounds. Watching from the outside was the investment group in Caparo, which had started buying shares in Fidelity. After reading the published annual accounts, the investors in Caparo believed Fidelity was a solid bargain, bought more shares on the open stock market, and launched a full takeover of the company for thirteen million pounds. Once the buyer in Caparo took control and opened Fidelity's real books, it discovered a nightmare. Instead of a 1.3 million pound profit, Fidelity had actually suffered a four hundred thousand pound loss. The buyer in Caparo sued the auditors in negligence under Hedley Byrne, arguing that it was completely foreseeable that stock-market investors and takeover bidders would read a company's published annual accounts before buying shares. **Do a company's statutory auditors owe a legal duty of care to potential outside investors, or even to existing individual shareholders, who rely on published annual accounts to buy more shares on the stock market?**

The House of Lords in Caparo unanimously answered **no**. Lord Bridge and Lord Oliver looked at why Parliament requires companies to publish audited annual accounts in the first place. Statutory audits are not prepared to give free investment tips to stock-market speculators or corporate takeover bidders. Instead, the law requires annual audits for one narrow purpose, which is to give the **shareholders as a collective group** truthful information so they can oversee how the directors are running the company at the annual general meeting, for example by voting to replace bad directors. As the court in Caparo saw, if auditors owed a duty of care to every investor on the stock market who read a public report before buying shares, auditors would face limitless liability to the entire world for a single mistake.

In delivering this judgment in Caparo, Lord Bridge permanently buried the overly broad approach of Anns from the 1970s and set out the famous **Caparo Three-Stage Test** for deciding whether a duty of care exists in a **novel, meaning new, situation**:
1. **Reasonable Foreseeability**: Was it reasonably foreseeable that the defendant's carelessness would cause damage to the claimant?
2. **Proximity**: Did a relationship of sufficient closeness and directness, known as proximity or neighbourhood, exist between the claimant and the defendant?
3. **Fair, Just, and Reasonable**: In all the circumstances, is it fair, just, and reasonable for the court to impose a legal duty of care on the defendant?

For cases of **negligent misstatement causing pure economic loss**, Caparo made the proximity rule crystal clear. An advisor owes a duty of care only if they knew, or ought to have known, that their statement would be communicated to the **specific claimant**, either as an individual or as a member of a small, identifiable group, in connection with a **particular transaction**, and that the claimant was very likely to rely on that statement to decide whether to enter into that exact transaction.

### The Supreme Court's 2018 Correction: Why You Must Stop Applying the Caparo Test to Every Case!

Now pay very close attention, because what happened between 1990 and 2018 is the single biggest trap in British tort law. After Caparo was decided in 1990, generations of law students, and even many lawyers and judges, misunderstood what Lord Bridge had actually said. They started treating the three-stage Caparo test of Foreseeability, Proximity, and Fair, Just, and Reasonable as a universal formula that had to be applied to **every single negligence case**, even simple road accidents or physical injuries. Worse still, police forces began using the third stage of Caparo, whether a duty is fair, just, and reasonable, to argue that the police should be immune from being sued whenever someone got hurt during police work.

That misunderstanding came to a head on a busy afternoon in July 2008 on Kirkgate, a crowded shopping street in the centre of Huddersfield, Yorkshire, in **Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police**, decided in 2018. Let's call it Robinson. In Robinson, a detective sergeant named Willan spotted a man named Williams, whom he suspected of dealing crack cocaine and heroin, standing in front of a bookmaker's shop, a betting shop. Knowing that Williams would resist arrest if approached alone, Willan called for backup so four plain-clothes police officers could arrest Williams together on the pavement. While the officers waited, Williams walked out of the bookmakers onto the pavement. Right at that moment in Robinson, a frail seventy-six-year-old lady who stood four feet eleven inches tall walked past Williams along the street. Despite seeing the elderly pedestrian right next to the suspect, two large officers grabbed Williams to arrest him. Williams struggled violently, and within three seconds the two officers and the suspect crashed straight into the pedestrian in Robinson, knocking her to the stone pavement with the three men falling right on top of her. When she sued the police in Robinson for her physical injuries, the Court of Appeal actually threw out her claim. They applied the three-stage Caparo test and ruled that under Stage 3, it was not fair, just, and reasonable to impose a duty of care on police officers while they were trying to arrest a criminal in the street. **Are police officers protected by a special legal immunity when their own positive physical actions carelessly knock over and injure an innocent passer-by in the street, and should courts apply the Caparo three-stage test to established categories of physical injury?**

In 2018, Lord Reed and the UK Supreme Court in Robinson overturned the Court of Appeal, awarded damages to the injured pedestrian, and issued a crucial warning to every lawyer and law student in Britain. First, Robinson made clear that **Caparo never created a universal three-stage test for all negligence claims.** What Caparo actually taught was **incrementalism**, which means developing the law step by step by analogy with existing cases. In the vast majority of negligence cases, such as a driver driving a car, a doctor treating a patient, or **any person, including a police officer, whose positive physical act directly causes foreseeable bodily injury to someone nearby**, previous cases have *already* established that a duty of care exists. As Robinson explained, when a case falls inside an **established category** like that, a court must **not** apply the three-stage Caparo test or ask whether a duty is fair, just, and reasonable. You only ever use the Caparo three-stage analysis when the court is asked to recognise a duty of care in a **genuinely novel situation** that is not covered by existing precedent.

Second, drawing a direct constitutional line all the way back to Entick and Dorset Yacht, Lord Reed in Robinson reaffirmed that under the British common law, public authorities and police officers are subject to the exact same tort laws as ordinary private citizens. There is a vital difference between **omissions**, meaning failing to stop someone else from causing harm, and **positive acts**, meaning causing harm yourself. Because the common law generally does not punish omissions, the police normally owe no duty of care to private citizens if they fail to catch a criminal before the criminal attacks someone, as seen in **Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire**, in 1989, which we call Hill, and **Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales Police**, in 2015, which we call Michael. However, Robinson proved that when police officers carry out a **positive physical act**, like tackling a suspect on a crowded pavement right beside a seventy-six-year-old pensioner, they owe an ordinary duty to take reasonable care not to knock bystanders over, just like any private citizen.

> **Revision:** In this block we saw how the courts control the duty of care when claims move from careless words to everyday physical harm. First, in Hedley Byrne, from 1964, a bank gave a careless credit reference for a client that went bankrupt, and the court held that pure financial loss from careless words requires a special relationship built on voluntary assumption of responsibility and reasonable reliance, though the bank escaped liability because of its disclaimer. Second, in Caparo, from 1990, corporate takeover buyers relied on inaccurate audited accounts, and the court refused a duty because statutory audits are written only for shareholders as a collective group to watch over the company, while setting out the three-stage test of foreseeability, proximity, and what is fair, just, and reasonable for novel situations. Third, in Robinson, from 2018, police officers knocked over a frail passer-by while tackling a suspect, and the Supreme Court made clear that courts must apply established categories of duty first and reserve the three-stage test in Caparo solely for genuinely novel claims. Now that we know when a defendant owes a duty of care, how do we measure whether they broke that duty in everyday life? That is the question we turn to in Nettleship, from 1971.

## 5. Raising and Measuring the Bar: Breach of Duty in the Real World

### Learner Drivers, Flying Cricket Balls, and Electroshock Therapy: How High Is the Standard of Care?

Once the victim proves Stage One of negligence, that the defendant owed them a **Duty of Care**, the battle moves to Stage Two, which is **Breach of Duty**. In simple words, did the defendant act carelessly by falling below the standard of care that the law requires? Remember the two nineteenth-century foundation stones we heard earlier in Vaughan and Blyth. The standard of care is **objective**, measured by what a reasonable, sensible person would do when faced with foreseeable risks. In the twentieth century, three famous cases tested how that objective standard works in everyday life: when the defendant is an inexperienced beginner, when a risk is extremely small, and when the defendant is a highly trained medical professional.

First, let us look at the inexperienced beginner in **Nettleship v Weston**, decided in 1971. We'll call it Nettleship. To appreciate Nettleship, think about Britain in the late 1960s and early 1970s. For the first time in history, millions of ordinary working-class and middle-class families were buying motor cars, and Parliament required every driver on public roads to carry **compulsory third-party motor insurance** so that injured road victims would always have an insurance fund to claim against. In Nettleship, a wife wanted to learn how to drive her husband's car. Rather than paying a professional driving school, she asked a family friend to sit in the passenger seat and teach her. Before agreeing to help her, the friend in Nettleship was very sensible. He asked to see the car's insurance policy to make sure that he would be covered as a passenger if an accident happened, and the couple showed him a comprehensive insurance policy that covered passengers. During her first two lessons in Nettleship, the learner did well. On her third lesson, however, they reached a road junction where she had to turn left. Moving slowly at walking pace, about four miles an hour, she turned the steering wheel to the left while her friend operated the gear stick and handbrake for her. Once the car turned the corner, however, the learner in Nettleship suddenly froze in panic and held the steering wheel tightly in place instead of straightening it out. The car drove slowly onto the left-hand pavement and crashed into a street lamppost, trapping and fracturing the instructor's left kneecap. When he sued her in Nettleship so that her car insurance company would pay for his broken knee, her lawyers made an appealing human argument. They pointed out that her friend *knew* she was a total beginner on her third lesson, and argued that a learner driver should only owe her instructor a lower, personalised duty of care, namely to do the best that an inexperienced learner could do. **Does a learner driver owe a lower, personalised standard of care to a passenger or instructor who knows that the driver is an inexperienced beginner?**

Lord Denning, the Master of the Rolls, and the Court of Appeal in Nettleship ruled **no**. Extending the principle of Vaughan into the modern age of compulsory car insurance, Lord Denning held in Nettleship that every single driver on a public road, even a learner driver on her very first day behind the wheel, must meet the exact same fixed, objective standard of care, which is **the standard of a competent and experienced driver**. In Lord Denning's famous words in Nettleship, *"Her incompetent best is not good enough."* Why did the court adopt such a strict rule? For two practical reasons. First, if the standard of care changed depending on how much each passenger knew about the driver's skill, or how many lessons the driver had taken, or how nervous they were, every car accident trial would turn into an impossible guessing game. Second, Lord Denning candidly pointed to the reality of **compulsory motor insurance**. Parliament forced drivers to buy insurance precisely so that innocent people injured in road accidents would be compensated. Therefore, Nettleship proves that inexperience is never a defence in tort law. A learner driver is judged as an experienced driver, just as a junior hospital doctor on their first shift is judged by the standard of a competent doctor holding that post, as confirmed in **Wilsher v Essex Area Health Authority**, in 1988, which we call Wilsher. And in Nettleship, because the instructor shared control of the car and failed to pull the handbrake quite fast enough, his damages were reduced by fifty percent for contributory negligence, yet the learner driver's breach of duty was firmly established.

Second, what if the defendant is completely experienced and actually realises that a tiny risk of injury exists, yet decides not to spend money stopping it? Travel to a quiet residential suburb of Manchester on a sunny Saturday afternoon in August 1947 in **Bolton v Stone**, decided in 1951. Let's call it Bolton. For over eighty years in Bolton, the Cheetham Cricket Club had played cricket on its ground next to Beckenham Road, a quiet residential side street. A hit in cricket scores six runs when the batter hits the ball all the way over the boundary rope without bouncing. At the Beckenham Road end of the ground in Bolton, the club had already built a seven-foot wooden fence, and because the pitch sat ten feet lower than the top of the bank, the top of the fence stood a full **seventeen feet** above the level of the cricket pitch. On 9 August 1947, in Bolton, a woman who lived at 10 Beckenham Road stepped out of her front garden gate onto the pavement just as a visiting cricketer hit one of the biggest shots ever seen on that ground. The hard leather cricket ball flew nearly one hundred yards, about ninety metres, through the air, sailed right over the top of the seventeen-foot fence, and struck her squarely on the head, injuring her. She sued the cricket club committee in Bolton for negligence. Look closely at the evidence discovered at the trial in Bolton. Over the previous **thirty years**, thousands of balls had been bowled on that pitch, and a cricket ball had only been hit over that fence onto Beckenham Road about **six times in thirty years**, without ever hitting anyone. **Does a defendant breach their duty of care simply because an accident was theoretically foreseeable, even when the probability, the likelihood, of someone actually being injured was extremely small?**

The House of Lords in Bolton unanimously ruled **no**. Notice the crucial distinction the judges in Bolton drew between Stage One, Duty, and Stage Two, Breach. Yes, it was physically possible and foreseeable that once every five years a powerful batter might hit a ball over the fence, so the club owed road users a duty of care. However, deciding whether the club **breached** that duty requires a practical balancing test, which lawyers call the **calculus of risk**. A reasonable person does not spend a fortune or shut down everyday activities to guard against every tiny, remote possibility in human life. Instead, Lord Reid and Lord Radcliffe explained in Bolton that a reasonable person only takes precautions against risks that are sufficiently real and probable that an ordinary, sensible person would not brush them aside as **remote or fantastic**. When deciding whether a defendant took reasonable care, the court weighs four factors together. First is the **likelihood, or probability, of harm** happening, as in Bolton. Second is the **seriousness, or gravity, of the injury** if the accident does happen, as in **Paris v Stepney Borough Council**, in 1951, which we call Paris, where a council had to provide safety goggles to a one-eyed garage mechanic even though flying metal splinters were rare, because losing his only good eye meant total blindness. Third is the **cost and difficulty** of taking precautions to eliminate the risk, as in **Latimer v AEC Ltd**, in 1953, which we call Latimer. Fourth is the **social value** of the activity being carried out. Because the chance in Bolton of a cricket ball clearing a seventeen-foot fence and hitting a pedestrian on a quiet side street was tiny, the cricket club had acted reasonably and did not breach its duty.

Third, how does the law measure the standard of care when the defendant is not an ordinary person driving a car or running a sports club, but a **skilled professional** such as a hospital doctor, surgeon, engineer, or lawyer? That question was answered in **Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee**, in 1957. We'll call it Bolam. To understand Bolam, we need to picture psychiatric medicine in Britain in 1954. At that time, before modern anti-depressant medicines were widely available, patients suffering from severe, life-threatening clinical depression were often treated in hospital with **electroconvulsive therapy**, or ECT. That was a medical treatment in which a doctor placed electrodes on the patient's head and passed a brief electric current through the brain to trigger a controlled seizure, or convulsion, which relieved severe depression in many patients. However, the electric current caused the patient's muscles to contract violently for a few seconds, creating a very small risk, roughly one in ten thousand, that the strength of the patient's own muscle spasms could crack or fracture a bone. In 1954, respected psychiatrists across Britain were deeply divided into two competing medical camps on how to manage that risk:
- **Medical Camp A** believed in injecting the patient beforehand with a newly developed **muscle-relaxant drug**, which paralysed the muscles during the electric shock, or having nurses hold the patient's shoulders and hips down on the bed, known as **manual restraints**.
- **Medical Camp B** strongly disagreed. They pointed out that muscle-relaxant drugs carried a small risk of killing the patient by stopping their breathing under anaesthetic, and they believed that holding a convulsing patient down with physical force actually made bone fractures *more* likely rather than less likely. Moreover, doctors in 1954 generally did not warn depressed patients about the tiny one-in-ten-thousand risk of fractures beforehand, fearing that the warning would terrify the patient into refusing treatment they desperately needed.

In August 1954, in Bolam, a patient voluntarily entered Friern Hospital to receive ECT for his severe depression. His doctor belonged to Medical Camp B. He gave the patient in Bolam no muscle-relaxant drugs, used no manual restraints other than a nurse standing on each side of the couch to stop him falling off, and did not warn him of the tiny risk of bone fractures. During his second treatment in Bolam, the patient suffered violent muscle spasms that drove the tops of his thigh bones straight through both of his hip sockets, causing bilateral acetabular fractures. The patient in Bolam sued the hospital for negligence, calling expert doctors from Camp A who testified that relaxant drugs should have been used. **Is a doctor or other skilled professional guilty of negligence when they follow a medical practice that is accepted as proper by a respected, responsible group of doctors, simply because another respected group of doctors would have used a different method?**

Directing the trial jury in Bolam, Justice McNair laid down the famous **Bolam Test**, which governs professional negligence across the common law world. First, when a task requires a special professional skill, the defendant is not judged by the standard of an ordinary layperson on the street, whom older English judges called the man on the Clapham omnibus, nor are they required to be the most brilliant genius in the country. Instead, under Bolam, they are judged by the standard of the **ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that special skill**, for example an ordinary competent psychiatrist or surgeon. Second, and most importantly, Bolam established that **a doctor is not negligent if they have acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical professionals skilled in that particular art, merely because there is another body of opinion that takes a contrary view.** Because medicine and science are constantly developing, honest professionals often disagree on the best technique, and a judge cannot condemn a doctor as negligent when a respected group of specialists supports what that doctor did.

Two essential modern cases later refined the rule in Bolam. First, in **Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority**, in 1998, which we call Bolitho, the House of Lords ruled that a court is not bound to let a doctor off the hook just because other doctors support them if that medical opinion **cannot withstand logical analysis**. In rare cases where a medical practice has no logical basis at all, Bolitho says the judge has the final say. Second, in **Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board**, in 2015, which we call Montgomery, the Supreme Court ruled that the Bolam test **no longer applies to a doctor's duty to warn patients of treatment risks**. While Bolam still governs diagnosis and physical treatment, Montgomery holds that when it comes to **informed consent and warning a patient of risks**, a doctor must now disclose any material risk to which a **reasonable person in the patient's position** would likely attach significance, or which the doctor knows that particular patient would care about.

> **Revision:** In this block we saw how the courts measure whether a defendant broke their duty of care across three everyday settings. First, in Nettleship, from 1971, a learner driver panicked on her third lesson and broke her instructor's knee, and the court held that every driver must meet the single objective standard of a competent and experienced driver, just as a junior doctor must in Wilsher, from 1988. Second, in Bolton, from 1951, a cricket ball cleared a seventeen-foot fence for only the sixth time in thirty years, showing through the calculus of risk that a reasonable person weighs the likelihood of harm against the gravity of injury in Paris, from 1951, the cost of precautions in Latimer, from 1953, and social value, and need not guard against a tiny risk. Third, in Bolam, from 1957, a patient suffered hip fractures during electroconvulsive therapy, establishing that a skilled professional is not negligent if they follow a practice supported by a responsible body of medical opinion, provided that opinion is logical under Bolitho, from 1998, while warnings of treatment risks now follow the reasonable patient standard in Montgomery, from 2015. But suppose a claimant proves both a duty of care and a serious breach of duty. Do they automatically win, or must they still prove that the carelessness actually caused their harm? That is the question in Barnett, from 1969.

## 6. The Causation Maze: Poisoned Tea, Asbestos Dust, and Harbour Infernos

### Why a Careless Hospital Can Refuse to Examine a Dying Patient and Still Pay Zero Pounds

Suppose someone comes to court and proves Stage One, that the defendant owed them a **Duty of Care** under Donoghue or Robinson, and also proves Stage Two, that the defendant committed a serious **Breach of Duty** under Vaughan, Nettleship, or Bolam. Have they won their case? **No.** They still win zero pounds unless they can cross Stage Three of negligence, which is **Causation and Remoteness of Damage**. Remember why tort law exists. Unlike criminal law, which punishes bad behaviour even when nobody gets hurt, like speeding on an empty motorway, tort law only orders a defendant to pay compensation when the defendant's carelessness **actually caused** the victim's injury.

To hear how harsh and uncompromising this rule can be, travel to West London on the freezing morning of 1 January 1966 in **Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital Management Committee**, decided in 1969. We'll call it Barnett. In Barnett, a night-watchman and two other men worked through the night guarding the Chelsea College of Science and Technology. At five o'clock in the morning on New Year's Day, at the end of their shift, the three watchmen boiled a kettle and drank a cup of tea together. Unknown to them, an unidentified murderer had secretly slipped a massive, lethal dose of **arsenic**, a deadly heavy-metal poison that destroys the stomach and organs, into their tea. Within twenty minutes of drinking the tea in Barnett, all three men began vomiting violently with terrible stomach cramps. Thinking they had severe food poisoning, the three men walked into the casualty department, the emergency room, of St Stephen's Hospital at eight o'clock in the morning. One of the watchmen in Barnett was in such agony that he lay doubled over on two chairs in the waiting room. The hospital nurse on duty immediately picked up the telephone and called the medical casualty officer on duty that morning, Dr Banerjee, who was in his bedroom upstairs feeling unwell and exhausted himself after New Year's Eve. When the nurse described the three men's violent vomiting over the phone, the doctor in Barnett refused to come downstairs to examine them. Instead, he told the nurse, *"Well, I am vomiting myself and I have not been drinking. Tell them to go home and go to bed and call in their own doctors."* The men went back to their college and called their own doctor, who sent the watchman in Barnett back to hospital by ambulance later that morning, yet by half past one in the afternoon he was dead from arsenic poisoning. When his widow sued the hospital in Barnett for negligence, look at the legal question facing the judge. **When a hospital doctor carelessly refuses to examine a dying patient in the emergency room, is the hospital legally liable for his death if medical science proves that the poison would have killed him anyway, even if the doctor had treated him immediately?**

The trial judge in Barnett, Justice Nield, broke the problem down into three clear steps so that everyone could see how negligence works. First, did the hospital owe the poisoned watchman in Barnett a duty of care once he walked through the open doors of the emergency department asking for help? **Yes.** Second, did the doctor breach that duty of care in Barnett by refusing to come downstairs to examine three men who were vomiting violently? **Yes, without any doubt.** Third, did that careless refusal **cause** the watchman's death in Barnett? **No.**

Why not? Because in tort law, the person suing bears the legal burden of proving **factual causation** on the balance of probabilities, meaning a likelihood greater than fifty percent, using the **"But For" Test**. You must ask: **but for, meaning without, the defendant's careless mistake, would the victim have survived?** At the trial in Barnett, expert toxicology doctors explained how arsenic poisoning works in the human body. Even if the doctor in Barnett had rushed downstairs at eight in the morning, admitted the watchman to a hospital bed immediately, and set up an intravenous drip, a doctor does not automatically guess that a patient with stomach pain has been poisoned by an assassin with arsenic. Discovering arsenic poisoning requires chemical tests on the patient's stomach contents and blood, which would have taken several hours. Only after those tests identified arsenic could doctors have injected the specific chemical antidote for heavy-metal poisoning, a drug called BAL, or British Anti-Lewisite. By the time the hospital in Barnett could possibly have identified the poison and given the antidote around noon, the massive dose of arsenic would already have destroyed the watchman's organs and killed him at half past one. Because the victim in Barnett would have died even if the doctor had done everything right, the doctor's carelessness made no difference to the tragic outcome, and the hospital paid nothing.

### When Several Employers Expose a Worker to Deadly Asbestos Dust and Science Cannot Prove Which Fibre Killed Him, Does the "But For" Test Fail Justice?

The "But For" test in Barnett works fairly when medical science can trace exactly how a poison or an injury works. What happens, however, when a worker dies from a terrible industrial disease after working for **two or three careless employers one after another**, and modern medical science is physically unable to prove *which* employer's dust triggered the fatal disease?

That problem reached the House of Lords in **Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services Ltd**, in 2002. Let's call it Fairchild. To understand Fairchild, we first need to understand the industrial history of **asbestos** in twentieth-century Britain and how the fatal cancer called **mesothelioma** works. For decades between the 1930s and the 1970s, asbestos, which is a natural mineral made of microscopic, needle-sharp rock fibres that do not burn, was used everywhere in British shipyards, factories, power stations, and building sites to insulate boilers, pipes, and ceilings against fire. Whenever workers sawed or stripped asbestos boards without protective masks, thick clouds of invisible asbestos fibres filled the air. If a worker inhales asbestos dust, it can cause two very different lung diseases many years later:
1. **Asbestosis**: A scarring of the lungs that is **cumulative**, or divisible, meaning that every single breath of asbestos dust you inhale adds more scar tissue and makes your breathing worse. In asbestosis cases, causation is easy. If you worked for Employer A for five years and Employer B for five years, each employer made your lungs fifty percent worse, as shown in **Bonnington Castings Ltd v Wardlaw**, in 1956, which we call Bonnington.
2. **Mesothelioma**: A terrifying, incurable cancer of the thin lining around the lungs, called the pleura. Mesothelioma is completely different from asbestosis in two ways. First, it is an **indivisible, all-or-nothing disease**. Once the cancer starts, it is fatal, and inhaling more dust later does not make the cancer bigger or worse. Second, medical scientists still do not know the exact moment the cancer begins, except that it can be triggered by **a single microscopic asbestos fibre**, or a few fibres, lodging in the lung lining and causing one cell to mutate into cancer, which then lies silent for thirty or forty years before killing the worker.

In Fairchild, a worker spent the 1960s building and repairing premises where he cut asbestos sheets and swept up clouds of asbestos dust for two different careless employers, one after the other. Thirty years later, in 1996, the worker in Fairchild developed mesothelioma and died at the age of sixty. Now look at the cruel legal trap created by the Barnett "But For" test when his widow sued both employers in Fairchild. The first employer's lawyers stood up in court and said that the widow could not prove with a greater than fifty percent probability that the single fatal asbestos fibre came from their factory rather than the second employer's factory. Then the second employer's lawyers stood up and said the exact same thing, that she could not prove on a fifty-one percent balance of probabilities that the fatal fibre came from their factory rather than the first employer's factory. Because no scientist on earth can look inside a human lung forty years later and read a label on an asbestos fibre saying which employer owned it, the Court of Appeal in Fairchild applied the strict "But For" test and ruled that **both careless employers escaped paying a single penny**. Think about that result. A dying worker whose lungs were filled with asbestos by *one* careless employer won one hundred percent compensation, whereas a dying worker in Fairchild who was poisoned by *two* careless employers won **zero**. **When multiple employers have each carelessly exposed a worker to the same dangerous substance, asbestos, and medical science makes it impossible to prove which employer's fibres triggered a fatal indivisible disease, should the courts modify the strict "but for" test?**

Lord Bingham and the House of Lords in Fairchild refused to tolerate such a shocking injustice and unanimously overturned the Court of Appeal. Drawing on an earlier industrial skin-disease case, **McGhee v National Coal Board**, from 1973, which we call McGhee, the judges in Fairchild held that legal rules exist to serve justice, and when a strict rule produces a result that offends common sense, the rule must bend. So in Fairchild they created the famous **Fairchild Exception** to the "But For" test. Where, first, multiple defendants have each breached their duty of care by exposing the victim to the **same dangerous agent**, such as asbestos dust, second, that agent causes an indivisible disease like mesothelioma, and third, because of the **current limits of human scientific knowledge**, it is impossible for the victim to prove by the "but for" test which exposure triggered the disease, the victim in Fairchild wins by proving that a defendant's breach **materially increased the risk** that they would contract the disease. In those special circumstances, Fairchild treats **materially increasing the risk of the disease** as equivalent to causing the disease itself. And there is an important statutory sequel to Fairchild. After the House of Lords briefly ruled in **Barker v Corus UK Ltd**, in 2006, which we call Barker, that each employer only had to pay their percentage share of the risk, Parliament immediately passed **section 3 of the Compensation Act 2006**, which guarantees that in mesothelioma cases under Fairchild, every negligent employer or defendant who materially increased the risk is **one hundred percent jointly and severally liable** to pay the victim's full compensation.

### A Spark on Oil-Soaked Water: Even If You Factually Caused the Accident, Was the Type of Damage Too Unforeseeable to Be Your Responsibility in Law?

Suppose the victim passes the factual causation test, either the standard "But For" test from Barnett or the special exception in Fairchild. Does that mean the careless defendant must pay for every single freakish chain reaction that flows from their mistake, no matter how strange or unexpected? That brings us to the second half of Stage Three, which is **Remoteness of Damage**, often called **legal causation**, where the court decides where to cut off the chain of legal responsibility.

For forty years before 1961, English law followed a very harsh rule laid down in **Re Polemis and Furness, Withy & Co Ltd**, in 1921, which we call Polemis. In Polemis, workers carelessly dropped a wooden plank into the hold of a ship carrying tins of petrol. While a falling plank would foreseeably cause a small dent in the wood, the plank happened to strike a freak spark that ignited petrol vapour and blew up the entire ship. Under Polemis, the Court of Appeal ruled that once your act was careless, because *some* tiny damage was foreseeable, you were legally liable for **all direct physical consequences** of your act, even if the actual disaster that happened was completely unforeseeable.

That harsh rule in Polemis was finally tested and overthrown after a spectacular harbour fire in Sydney, Australia, in **Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock and Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound No 1)**, decided in 1961 by the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London, composed of the same senior Law Lords who sat in the House of Lords. We'll call it Wagon Mound. To understand what happened in Wagon Mound, picture how commercial shipping and ship repair operated in a busy port in 1951. Large cargo ships were powered by oil-burning steam boilers and took on hundreds of tons of fuel oil at special refuelling piers. This fuel was not explosive car petrol. It was heavy **bunkering furnace oil**, a thick, dark, sticky crude oil product that had to be heated inside a ship's boiler before it would burn. In October 1951, in Wagon Mound, a cargo ship was taking on bunkering furnace oil in Mort's Bay in Sydney Harbour. Because the ship's engineers carelessly failed to close a tank valve on time, a huge quantity of thick furnace oil overflowed from the ship into the harbour water. Driven by the wind and tide, the floating slick of black oil drifted across the bay and gathered underneath a long wooden pier, a **timber wharf**, where skilled workers were using hot **oxy-acetylene welding and cutting torches**, torches that shoot a white-hot flame of oxygen and gas to melt and join steel plates, to repair another ship called the *Corrimal*.

When the manager of the wooden wharf in Wagon Mound arrived on Monday morning and saw the thick black oil floating on the water beneath his wharf, he immediately stopped his men from welding and asked the manager of the oil wharf whether it was safe to use welding torches above furnace oil. Everyone, including the oil experts and the best scientific consensus of 1951, agreed that heavy furnace oil floating in an open bay on cold seawater **could not possibly be set on fire** by sparks falling from above, because the cold water underneath cooled the oil down far below its flashpoint. Reassured, the wharf manager in Wagon Mound ordered his workers to resume welding. Notice what the oil was already doing. It was sticking to the wooden posts and slipways under the wharf, causing minor **fouling and dirt**, which would simply need to be scrubbed clean. For two days in Wagon Mound, fiery sparks fell into the oil-covered water and went straight out, just as everyone expected. On the third day, however, a freak physical chain reaction occurred. A piece of floating **cotton waste**, meaning oily rags or debris, was floating on top of the oil slick beneath the wharf. A drop of red-hot molten metal fell from a welder's torch and landed directly on that floating rag. Instead of sinking into the cold water, the rag smouldered and acted exactly like **the wick inside an oil lamp**, heating a tiny circle of oil around it until the oil vapourised and caught fire. Within minutes in Wagon Mound, the entire oil slick erupted into a giant wall of flame that burned the wooden wharf to the waterline. When the wharf owners sued the shipowners in Wagon Mound, the trial judge made two crucial findings of fact. First, the shipowners could reasonably foresee that spilling oil would cause minor **damage by fouling, meaning muck and dirt**, on the slipways. Second, the shipowners **could not reasonably foresee** that furnace oil floating on cold water could catch fire and cause **damage by fire**. Under the old 1921 rule of Polemis, the shipowners would have lost automatically because the fire was a direct physical consequence of their careless oil spill. **In the tort of negligence, is a defendant legally liable for every direct physical consequence of their careless act, or are they only liable for damage of a kind, or type, that a reasonable person could have foreseen?**

Speaking for the Privy Council in Wagon Mound, Viscount Simonds overturned Polemis and established the modern test for **remoteness of damage** across the common law world. Viscount Simonds explained that it does not match modern ideas of justice or morality to say that because a person was careless in a minor way that could foreseeably cause a tiny smudge of dirt, they should be forced to pay millions of pounds for an utterly unforeseeable catastrophe of a completely different kind. Since **foresight of the reasonable person** is the test for whether a duty of care is breached in the first place, **foresight of the reasonable person must also be the test for remoteness of damage**. Under Wagon Mound, damage is not too remote, and the defendant is liable, **only if the kind, or type, of damage suffered by the claimant was reasonably foreseeable at the time of the breach**. Because damage to a wharf by **fire** is a fundamentally different *kind* of damage from damage by **oil fouling**, and damage by fire was found to be unforeseeable on the evidence in that trial, the shipowners in Wagon Mound were not liable for the burned wharf.

Now keep three famous companion rules alongside Wagon Mound so you can see the whole picture of remoteness:
1. **The manner of occurrence need not be foreseeable**, from **Hughes v Lord Advocate**, in 1963, which we call Hughes. Once the **kind or type of injury**, such as a burn from a paraffin warning lamp left by an open manhole, is reasonably foreseeable, Hughes holds that the defendant is liable even if the **exact way** the accident happened, a boy knocking the lamp into the hole and causing an unexpected vapour explosion, was unforeseeable.
2. **The extent of injury need not be foreseeable, known as the "Eggshell Skull" Rule**, from **Smith v Leech Brain & Co Ltd**, in 1962, which we call Smith. Once a foreseeable type of physical injury occurs, such as a tiny drop of molten metal burning a worker's lip, Smith says you **take your victim as you find them**, even if the victim had a hidden pre-cancerous condition in his lip skin that caused the tiny burn to trigger fatal cancer.
3. **Why the sequel case reached the opposite result**, in **The Wagon Mound (No 2)**, in 1967, which we call Wagon Mound Two. A few years after Wagon Mound, the owners of the *ships*, the *Corrimal* and the *Audrey D*, that were burned alongside the wharf brought their own lawsuit in Wagon Mound Two. Unlike the wharf owners in Wagon Mound, who did not want to admit that furnace oil could catch fire because their own men were welding above it, which would have made them guilty of contributory negligence, the shipowners in Wagon Mound Two had not been welding, so they called top scientists to prove that a skilled ship's engineer *would* have known there was a very rare, tiny possibility that oil on water could catch fire. Because spilling oil into a harbour is illegal and has zero social value, the Privy Council in Wagon Mound Two held that a reasonable engineer would not ignore even a tiny risk of fire when closing a valve costs nothing.

> **Revision:** In this block we saw how a claimant must prove both factual causation and legal remoteness before winning damages. First, in Barnett, from 1969, a hospital doctor carelessly refused to examine a night-watchman poisoned with arsenic, yet the hospital paid nothing under the "but for" test because the poison would have killed him before any antidote could work. Second, in Fairchild, from 2002, where multiple employers exposed a worker to asbestos dust after earlier lung-disease rules in Bonnington, from 1956, and McGhee, from 1973, and science could not tell which single fibre triggered mesothelioma, the court allowed causation to be proved by showing a material increase in risk, with full compensation later guaranteed by Parliament after Barker, from 2006. Third, in Wagon Mound, from 1961, overturning Polemis, from 1921, a harbour oil spill caught fire on cold water, establishing that a defendant is liable only if the kind of damage was reasonably foreseeable, even if the exact manner in Hughes, from 1963, or the victim's fragile condition in Smith, from 1962, was unexpected, while Wagon Mound Two, from 1967, showed that even a tiny foreseeable risk of fire cannot be ignored when avoiding it costs nothing. Having walked through every stage of a physical negligence claim, what happens at the outer frontiers when a disaster causes pure psychiatric shock to onlookers, or ruins life inside a family home? That brings us first to Alcock, from 1992.

## 7. The Outer Frontiers: Psychiatric Shock and the Battle Over Private Land

### Hillsborough, Live Television, and Why Secondary Victims Face Four Strict Legal Hurdles

We have now walked through every step of an ordinary negligence claim for physical injury or property damage. Why, then, does the law suddenly build four strict legal walls when someone comes to court suffering from **pure psychiatric injury**, meaning a diagnosed mental illness such as Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, or PTSD, or severe clinical depression triggered by shock, without any physical blow to their own body? For the exact same policy reason we saw in Hedley Byrne and Caparo. A physical car crash can only break the bones of the people inside the cars, whereas the shock of a single terrible disaster can traumatise thousands of witnesses, relatives, and television viewers across the country.

That agonising problem reached the House of Lords after one of the worst stadium disasters in British history in **Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police**, decided in 1992. Let's call it Alcock. To understand the human and legal story of Alcock, picture how English football stadiums were built in the 1980s before all-seater stadiums were introduced. Behind the goals at many older grounds were large standing terraces divided by tall steel fences into narrow cages called **pens**, designed with metal gates at the front to stop rival fans from running onto the grass pitch. On 15 April 1989, tens of thousands of Liverpool supporters travelled to **Hillsborough Stadium** in Sheffield for the FA Cup semi-final football match between Liverpool and Nottingham Forest, which was being broadcast live on BBC television. Outside the Leppings Lane entrance, a dangerous crowd crush built up at the turnstiles. South Yorkshire Police officers made a catastrophic series of mistakes. They ordered a wide exit gate to be opened to let thousands of fans into the stadium concourse, yet failed to close off the central tunnel that led straight into two already packed central standing pens, Pens 3 and 4. Thousands of fans poured down the tunnel into the back of the two fenced pens, crushing the supporters at the front against the high steel fences with nowhere to escape. Ninety-six innocent men, women, and children were crushed to death, with a ninety-seventh victim dying years later from his injuries, and over seven hundred people were injured as the horror unfolded on live television.

South Yorkshire Police admitted that their careless crowd control caused the deaths and physical injuries of the people trapped inside the pens. However, in Alcock, sixteen bereaved relatives and loved ones, including parents, brothers, sisters, a brother-in-law, a grandfather, and a fiancée of victims killed in the crush, sued the Chief Constable for the severe, medically diagnosed **psychiatric illness**, such as PTSD, that they suffered from witnessing or learning of the deaths of their loved ones. Look carefully at where these sixteen people in Alcock were on the day of the disaster:
- None of the sixteen people in Alcock was trapped inside the central pens themselves, so none was in physical danger of being crushed.
- Some of the people in Alcock were sitting or standing in **other stands inside Hillsborough Stadium**, watching the crush from across the pitch without knowing for certain until later that their specific relative was among the dead.
- Others in Alcock were **at home or nearby watching the disaster on live television** or listening on the radio, knowing their loved ones were at the match.
- One man in Alcock, Brian Harrison, was at the ground and, after searching all evening, **identified his brother's bruised body in the makeshift mortuary** at midnight, eight hours after the crush.

The legal question before the House of Lords in Alcock was heart-wrenching. **When a defendant's carelessness kills or injures a primary victim, what strict legal conditions must a secondary victim, meaning someone who was not in physical danger themselves yet suffered a recognised psychiatric illness from witnessing the tragedy, satisfy in order to claim compensation?**

Building on Lord Wilberforce's earlier speech in **McLoughlin v O'Brian**, in 1983, which we call McLoughlin, and later read alongside **Page v Smith**, in 1996, which we call Page, and **White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police**, in 1999, which we call White, the House of Lords in Alcock drew a sharp boundary between two types of psychiatric claimants:
1. **Primary Victims**: People who were directly involved as participants in the physical zone of danger, meaning they were either physically injured or reasonably feared for their own physical safety. Under Page, a Primary Victim can recover for psychiatric injury simply by proving that *some* physical injury to them was reasonably foreseeable.
2. **Secondary Victims**: People who were **never in any physical danger themselves**, yet suffered a medically recognised psychiatric illness, not mere normal grief, sorrow, or tears, which tort law never compensates, as a result of witnessing the death, injury, or peril of another person.

To stop the floodgates of unlimited claims from thousands of spectators and television viewers, the House of Lords in Alcock held that a Secondary Victim must first prove that it was reasonably foreseeable that a person of **normal fortitude**, meaning ordinary emotional strength, would suffer a psychiatric illness in the circumstances, and must **also** pass **four strict cumulative Control Mechanisms**:
1. **A Close Tie of Love and Affection** with the person killed or injured. To prevent invasive courtroom arguments in every case, Alcock says the law **presumes** this close tie exists *only* between **parents and children** and between **spouses or engaged fiancés**. Every other relative or friend, including **brothers, sisters, grandparents, uncles, and close friends**, is *not* presumed to have a sufficiently close tie and must bring special factual evidence proving that their relationship was just as exceptionally close as that of a parent or spouse. For example, two men in Alcock lost because they did not bring special evidence proving their adult relationship with their brothers was closer than ordinary brotherhood.
2. **Proximity in Time and Space, meaning physical presence at the accident or its immediate aftermath.** Under Alcock, the person suing must have been physically present at the scene of the horrifying accident itself, or must have arrived at its **immediate aftermath** very shortly afterwards while the victim was still in the raw state of the disaster, as in McLoughlin, where a mother rushed to the hospital within roughly an hour and saw her family covered in oil, dirt, and blood. In Alcock, identifying a body at the mortuary **eight or nine hours later** was held to be too late to count as the immediate aftermath.
3. **Direct Perception through the Claimant's Own Unaided Senses.** In Alcock, the court ruled that you must see or hear the horrifying event, or its immediate aftermath, directly **with your own eyes and ears**. Being told the tragic news by a third party on the telephone or in person never qualifies, and **watching the disaster on live television** did not qualify in Alcock because television broadcasting rules forbade cameras from zooming in on the faces of identifiable dying individuals, so the trauma came from knowing one's relative was at the stadium, rather than seeing their actual death with one's own eyes.
4. **Induced by a Sudden Shock.** Finally, Alcock requires that the psychiatric illness must be caused by a **sudden, unexpected, horrifying shock to the nervous system**, a violent assault on the senses, rather than the gradual build-up of stress or sorrow over days or weeks. Every single one of the sixteen people in Alcock failed at least one of these four strict hurdles and recovered nothing. And in a blockbuster modern sequel, the UK Supreme Court in **Paul v Royal Wolverhampton NHS Trust**, in 2024, which we call Paul, confirmed that Alcock remains strict law. When a hospital doctor carelessly fails to diagnose a patient's heart disease, and fourteen months later the patient's young children watch him collapse and die of a heart attack in the street, Paul holds that the children cannot sue as secondary victims because a medical crisis caused by an earlier untreated illness is not an external **accident**.

### From Entick's Front Door to a London Skyscraper: Who Has the Right to Sue When Your Home Life Is Ruined?

Look at how our twenty-case story now comes full circle back to where we started in 1765. We began this chapter in Entick with the tort of **trespass to land**, which protects your home against someone stepping directly across your boundary line. Later, we met Rylands, where a reservoir of water burst underground and flooded a neighbour's mine. That brings us to the tort of **private nuisance**, which deals with **indirect interference with a person's use or enjoyment of their land**, such as a factory next door pumping thick black smoke, terrible smells, or deafening noise across your garden fence every day, and to the famous modern case of **Hunter v Canary Wharf Ltd**, decided in 1997. We'll call it Hunter.

To understand the historical and social story behind Hunter, picture East London in the 1980s and early 1990s. For nearly two hundred years, the **Isle of Dogs** and **Limehouse** on the River Thames were the working heart of London's shipping docks, surrounded by tight-knit working-class neighbourhoods of small houses and council flats. By 1980, however, modern container ships had moved downriver, the old docks had closed down, and thousands of acres of dockland lay empty and derelict. To rebuild the area and turn East London into a global banking centre to rival the City of London, the government set up the London Docklands Development Corporation, the LDDC, and gave special fast-track permission for a giant commercial building project called **Canary Wharf**. Between 1988 and 1991, developers built Britain's tallest skyscraper, **One Canada Square**, a massive tower standing **two hundred and fifty metres, over eight hundred feet, high** and covered from top to bottom in stainless steel cladding, along with a huge new road tunnel called the **Limehouse Link Road**.

For the thousands of ordinary families in Hunter already living in the houses and flats around Poplar and Limehouse, the mega-project created two years of daily misery:
1. **The Blocked Television Signals**: Because the two-hundred-and-fifty-metre tower in Hunter was wrapped in stainless steel and stood directly in the path between the BBC's Crystal Palace broadcasting transmitter in South London and the homes of Poplar, the skyscraper acted like a giant metal mirror that **completely blocked television signals** for thousands of residents for nearly two years, from 1989 until a new relay transmitter was built in 1991.
2. **The Clouds of Construction Dust**: While blasting and digging the Limehouse Link Road right beside local streets, contractors threw thick clouds of **construction dust** into the air. Every time residents in Hunter opened a window or hung washing out to dry, a thick layer of grey dust settled over their furniture, carpets, and clothes.

Hundreds of local residents in Hunter joined together to sue Canary Wharf for blocking their television reception and the LDDC for the clouds of road dust in the tort of **private nuisance**. Now look closely at *who* the hundreds of people suing in Hunter were. They included not only the people whose names were written on the property deeds as **homeowners**, or freeholders, or on the rental agreements as **tenants**, or leaseholders, but also their **husbands, wives, adult children, and lodgers** who lived in those exact same houses as their permanent family homes, breathed the exact same dust, and stared at the exact same blank television screens, yet did not legally own or rent the house. Just a few years earlier, the Court of Appeal in **Khorasandjian v Bush**, in 1993, which we call Khorasandjian, had tried to modernise private nuisance for the twentieth century by ruling that a young woman who lived in her mother's house, without owning or renting it, could use private nuisance to stop an ex-boyfriend from making harassing phone calls to the home. Relying on Khorasandjian, the Court of Appeal in Hunter agreed that anyone who lived in a house as their home had a right to sue in nuisance for the dust. When Hunter reached the House of Lords, the judges had to decide two fundamental questions about property law. **First, can a family member who lives in a home without owning or renting the property sue in private nuisance? Second, can a landowner be sued in private nuisance simply for building a tall building on their own land that blocks television signals from reaching their neighbours' homes?**

The House of Lords in Hunter answered **no to both questions**, overruled Khorasandjian, and restored the traditional boundaries of property law:
1. **Who Can Sue in Private Nuisance? Only Someone with a Property Right in the Land.** Lord Goff and the majority in Hunter explained that we must never confuse the tort of **negligence**, which protects *people* against personal injury, with the tort of **private nuisance**, which is strictly a **tort against land**, designed to protect the value and utility of a piece of real property. Because private nuisance is a tort to land, Hunter holds that you only have legal standing to sue if you hold a **proprietary interest in the land** or a **right to exclusive possession** of it, meaning you are the **owner, or freeholder**, the **tenant on the lease**, or someone in exclusive physical possession. Family members who merely live in the house with the owner's permission, such as **a husband or wife whose name is not on the deeds or lease, children living with their parents, or lodgers**, are merely licensees on the land and **cannot sue in private nuisance** under Hunter. If they suffer personal injury from toxic dust, they must sue in **negligence**, and if they suffer harassment like the daughter in Khorasandjian, Parliament created a separate statutory remedy in the **Protection from Harassment Act 1997**.
2. **Why Blocking TV Signals with a Tall Building Is Not a Nuisance, known as the Emanation Rule.** The judges in Hunter also unanimously threw out the television claim, even for the homeowners. For hundreds of years, English common law has held that a landowner is free to build on their own land, and unless a neighbour holds a special legal right called an ancient easement, **the mere presence of a building on your neighbour's land is never an actionable nuisance**, even if that building blocks your view, stops the wind from reaching your chimney, or blocks radio and television waves passing through the air. Normally, as Hunter explained, an actionable private nuisance requires something noxious, such as **smoke, noise, vibrations, smells, dust, or invasive tree roots**, to physically **emanate, meaning come out**, from the defendant's land and cross into the neighbour's land. And in a brilliant recent update to Hunter, the UK Supreme Court in **Fearn v Board of Trustees of the Tate Gallery**, in 2023, which we call Fearn, clarified that while simply building an ordinary flat or tower next door is never a nuisance, when the Tate Modern art gallery opened a special outdoor public **viewing platform** inviting half a million tourists a year to stand with binoculars and cameras staring straight through the glass walls of the neighbours' living rooms next door, that **intense visual intrusion** from an abnormal use of land *did* constitute an actionable private nuisance.

> **Revision:** In this final block we saw how the law guards its outer frontiers against unlimited claims in psychiatric shock and private nuisance. First, in Alcock, from 1992, building on McLoughlin, from 1983, and read alongside Page, from 1996, White, from 1999, and Paul, from 2024, relatives of the Hillsborough disaster victims lost their claims because secondary victims who were outside the physical zone of danger must pass four strict hurdles: a close tie of love and affection, presence at the accident or its immediate aftermath, direct perception with their own unaided senses rather than television, and a sudden shock. Second, in Hunter, from 1997, overruling Khorasandjian, from 1993, residents living beside the Canary Wharf tower and roadworks lost when they had no property right in their home or complained of blocked television reception, because private nuisance is strictly a tort to land requiring exclusive possession and a harmful emanation crossing the boundary, though intense overlooking from a public viewing gallery can now be a nuisance under Fearn, from 2023. Having traced all twenty foundational cases from Entick in 1765 to Robinson in 2018, how do all five gates of tort law fit together on a single mental map? That is what we review next in the Master Blueprint.

## 9. The 20 Foundational Cases in Logical Summary Format

Now that you hold the full historical and legal story connecting every rule from 1765 to 2018, study each of the twenty foundational cases below. Read the **Facts** to anchor the physical and commercial story, the **Conflict Equation** to spot the issue under exam pressure, the **Decision** and **Liability** verdicts to lock in the outcome, and the **Principles** to master the exact legal reasoning.

## ❒ Entick v Carrington (1765)

> **Facts:** John Entick wrote political articles criticising King George III's government in an opposition newspaper. Acting under a "general warrant" signed by a government minister rather than a judge, four royal officers broke into Entick's house, searched his private rooms for four hours, and carried away his private papers. When Entick sued them for trespassing, the government claimed it had the power to enter any citizen's home in the name of state security.

![Entick v Carrington (1765): A government minister signs a general warrant, royal officers raid Entick's home and seize his papers, and Lord Camden CJ holds them liable in trespass.](../images/01/07_Entick.jpeg)

- **ENTICK** (political writer, private home, seized papers) + **CARRINGTON** (King's messengers, minister's warrant): **Can government officials enter private property and seize a citizen's papers without a clear Act of Parliament or common law rule giving them that authority?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — The government has no automatic power to invade private property; every entry onto private land by a state official is a tort unless authorised by a specific law.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The King's officers were liable in trespass for breaking into Entick's home and taking his papers under an unlawful warrant.
- **Principle**: Lord Camden CJ established the constitutional rule that while private citizens may do anything the law does not forbid, public officials may do nothing unless positive law expressly authorises it.
- **State Necessity Rejected**: "State necessity" is never a valid legal defence to trespass in the common law, making the Secretary of State's general warrant completely invalid.
- **Strict Property Protection**: Stepping onto another person's land without permission—even without causing physical damage—is immediately actionable as trespass to land (*trespass quare clausum fregit*).

## ❒ Vaughan v Menlove (1837)

> **Facts:** Menlove built a large stack of damp hay right next to his neighbour Vaughan's wooden country cottages. Even though damp hay ferments and builds up intense heat, Menlove ignored five weeks of warnings from neighbours, said he would "chance it," and cut an air hole through the stack that let oxygen feed the heat like a chimney. After the hay caught fire and burned down Vaughan's cottages, Menlove argued he should not be blamed because he had low intelligence and had acted to the best of his own limited judgment.

![Vaughan v Menlove (1837): Menlove's fermenting damp haystack catches fire and spreads across the fence to Vaughan's cottages.](../images/01/02_vaughan.jpeg)

- **VAUGHAN** (burned neighbouring cottages) + **MENLOVE** (fermenting damp haystack, "I'll chance it"): **Is negligence judged by an objective standard of ordinary carefulness rather than whether the defendant did their personal best?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — Negligence is measured against the objective external standard of a person of ordinary prudence (ordinary carefulness and common sense), not the defendant's personal intelligence.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Menlove was liable for the fire even though he claimed he was not clever enough to understand the full danger and had acted honestly.
- **Principle**: Chief Justice Tindal rejected a subjective test because judging care by each person's individual intelligence would be "as variable as the length of the foot of each individual."
- **Reckless Disregard**: Warned repeatedly for five weeks that his steaming haystack would catch fire, Menlove fell far below the standard of an ordinary sensible neighbour.

## ❒ Winterbottom v Wright (1842)

> **Facts:** The Postmaster General signed a contract with Wright to supply horse-drawn mail coaches and keep them in safe mechanical repair, and signed a separate contract with another firm to supply drivers. Because Wright did careless maintenance work, a hidden crack inside the coach's iron axle (the heavy metal rod connecting the wheels beneath the carriage) snapped while Winterbottom was driving the horses, throwing him off and crippling him for life. Wright argued he owed zero duty to the driver because his contract was only with the Postmaster General.

![Winterbottom v Wright (1842): Separate contracts, the snapped iron axle throwing the mail-coach driver, and the court barring his claim for lack of privity.](../images/01/05_Winterbottom.jpeg)

- **WINTERBOTTOM** (crippled mail-coach driver, no contract with repairer) + **WRIGHT** (careless coach maintenance contractor): **Can a third party injured by a defective piece of equipment sue the careless contractor in tort when there is no contract between them?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Under nineteenth-century law, a person who was not a party to a contract could not sue in tort for injuries caused by the careless performance of that contract.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Even though the driver was crippled by the hidden defect in the iron axle, the maintenance contractor owed no tort duty outside his contract with the Postmaster General.
- **Principle**: Lord Abinger CB blocked non-parties from suing in tort out of fear that allowing strangers to a contract to sue would cause "the most absurd and outrageous consequences" of unlimited lawsuits.
- **Historical Impact**: Known as the "privity fallacy," this rule shielded careless manufacturers and repairers from injured workers and consumers for 90 years until *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932).

## ❒ Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co (1856)

> **Facts:** A water company installed cast-iron water pipes beneath the street outside Blyth's house, fitted with an underground fireplug (a stopper beneath a street cover that firefighters pulled out to draw water for fire engines). The fireplug worked safely without leaking for 25 years. During a record-breaking winter freeze in 1855, thick ice formed deep underground around the stopper, blocked the drainage gap, and forced water into Blyth's cellar. Blyth sued the company for negligence.

![Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co (1856): Underground cross-section showing the frozen ground, the ice-blocked fireplug 18 inches below the street cover, and water escaping into Mr Blyth's house.](../images/01/01_Blyth.jpeg)

- **BLYTH** (flooded homeowner) + **BIRMINGHAM WATERWORKS** (underground fireplug, extreme 1855 freeze): **Does a defendant breach their duty of care when an extreme, unprecedented natural event causes properly maintained equipment to fail?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A defendant is only required to take precautions against reasonably foreseeable risks, not extraordinary natural events beyond ordinary human foresight.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Because the fireplug was properly built and had worked safely for 25 years, the water company was not at fault when an unprecedented freeze forced hidden ice around the underground plug.
- **Principle**: Baron Alderson gave the classic definition of negligence: failing to do something which a reasonable person would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable person would not do.
- **Foreseeability Limit**: The reasonable person is sensible and careful, not a fortune-teller required to predict record-breaking freaks of nature.

## ❒ Rylands v Fletcher (1868)

> **Facts:** Rylands hired skilled independent builders to dig and build a large artificial water reservoir on his land to power his cotton mill. Beneath the soil lay five old, forgotten coal-mine shafts filled with loose dirt that connected underground to his neighbour Fletcher's active coal mine. When the reservoir was filled, millions of gallons of water burst through the old shafts and flooded Fletcher's mine, even though Rylands personally had not been careless at all.

![Rylands v Fletcher (1868): Cross-section of Rylands's mill reservoir bursting through old underground coal shafts into Fletcher's active coal mine.](../images/01/04_Rylands.jpeg)

- **FLETCHER** (flooded neighbouring coal mine) + **RYLANDS** (mill water reservoir, forgotten underground mine shafts): **Is a landowner strictly liable without personal fault when an extraordinary, dangerous accumulation on their land escapes and damages a neighbour's property?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — A landowner who brings onto their land and collects anything likely to cause harm if it escapes keeps it at their own risk and is strictly liable for all natural consequences if it escapes.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Even though Rylands committed no personal carelessness, he was strictly liable when the water from his new reservoir burst through the old shafts and flooded Fletcher's mine.
- **Principle**: Under Blackburn J's rule (as refined by Lord Cairns LC in the House of Lords), making a **"non-natural (extraordinary) use"** of land triggers strict liability when the accumulated hazard escapes and causes damage.
- **No Defence of Independent Contractors**: Rylands could not escape liability by arguing that the mistake was made by the independent builders he hired rather than by himself.

## ❒ Heaven v Pender (1883)

> **Facts:** A dry-dock owner (Pender) rented out his dock and hung a wooden painting stage (a high wooden plank held up by thick ropes looped over the ship) around a ship so workers could paint the outside hull. One of the ropes had been burned and weakened beforehand. When Heaven, a ship painter employed by an independent painting contractor, stepped onto the wooden plank, the burned rope snapped and he fell into the dock. Pender argued he owed Heaven no duty because he had no contract with him.

![Heaven v Pender (1883): The scorched staging rope snapping beneath the ship painter in dry dock and the Court of Appeal ruling in his favour.](../images/01/06_Heaven.jpeg)

- **HEAVEN** (injured ship painter, no contract with dock owner) + **PENDER** (dry-dock owner, burned staging rope): **Does a supplier of commercial equipment owe a duty of care to a worker injured by defective gear even though there is no contract between them?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — The dock owner owed a legal duty to take reasonable care that the rope-hung wooden staging was safe for workers coming onto the dock to use it.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Pender was liable to pay damages to the injured painter for supplying a burned, defective rope that snapped under his weight.
- **Principle (Majority vs Minority Split)**: While the **majority of the Court of Appeal (Cotton and Bowen LJJ)** ruled in the painter's favour on the narrow property-law ground that the painter was an **invited visitor** on the dock owner's premises, **Brett MR (in the minority)** saw the bigger picture and proposed the first general duty of care based on foreseeable danger to others.
- **Direct Seed of Donoghue**: Brett MR's famous minority rule—that a duty of care arises whenever any person of ordinary sense would recognise that failing to use ordinary care will endanger another person's body or property—laid the direct foundation for Lord Atkin's Neighbour Principle in 1932.

## ❒ Wilkinson v Downton (1897)

> **Facts:** As a cruel practical joke, Downton walked into a London pub run by Mrs Wilkinson and told her a completely false story that her husband had just been thrown from a wagon on his way back from the horse races, that both of his legs were broken, and that she needed to take two pillows in a cab to bring him home. Although Downton never touched her, the violent mental shock turned Mrs Wilkinson's hair white and caused weeks of vomiting and a severe nervous breakdown.

![Wilkinson v Downton (1897): Downton's false story at the pub bar, Mrs Wilkinson's immediate shock, and her subsequent severe illness in bed.](../images/01/03_Wilkinson.jpeg)

- **WILKINSON** (terrified wife, severe nervous shock and physical illness) + **DOWNTON** (cruel prankster, false accident story): **Is a defendant liable in tort for deliberately making a false statement that is plainly likely to cause severe physical or psychiatric harm?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — A person who deliberately (wilfully) does an act calculated (plainly likely) to cause physical harm to another person commits a tort when physical or recognised psychiatric harm actually results.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Downton was liable for the severe nervous shock and physical illness caused to Mrs Wilkinson by his deliberate lie.
- **Principle**: Wright J created a separate intentional tort for **indirect harm**, holding that when a defendant deliberately does an act that is plainly likely to cause severe harm without lawful justification, the law treats them as intending that result.
- **Filling the Gap in Trespass**: Because Downton never physically touched Mrs Wilkinson (so battery did not apply) and did not trick her out of money (so fraud did not apply), this new rule filled a vital gap between direct trespass and modern negligence.

## ❒ Donoghue v Stevenson (1932)

> **Facts:** Mrs Donoghue went to a café in Paisley, Scotland, where her friend bought and paid for a bottle of ginger beer made of dark, opaque glass that nobody could see through. After Mrs Donoghue drank part of her glass, her friend poured out the rest of the bottle, and the rotting body of a dead snail floated out into her glass, causing her severe shock and stomach illness. Because her friend had bought the drink, Mrs Donoghue had no contract with either the café owner or the manufacturer, David Stevenson.

![Donoghue v Stevenson (1932): Mrs Donoghue's friend pours ginger beer from an opaque dark-glass bottle in the Paisley café as a decomposing snail floats out into the glass.](../images/01/08_Donoghue.jpeg)

- **DONOGHUE** (sick café customer, no contract of sale) + **STEVENSON** (ginger-beer manufacturer, rotting snail in dark sealed bottle): **Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care in tort to the final consumer when a defective product causes physical injury, even without a contract between them?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — A manufacturer owes a duty of care to the final consumer when products are sold in a sealed form that prevents anyone from inspecting the contents before they are used or consumed.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — By a 3–2 majority, the House of Lords ruled that Mrs Donoghue had a valid claim in negligence against the manufacturer despite having no contract with him.
- **Principle**: Lord Atkin established the **Neighbour Principle**: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your **neighbour**—defined as persons who are so **closely and directly affected** by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in mind.
- **End of the Privity Barrier**: By separating the tort of negligence from contract law, the House of Lords demolished the 90-year roadblock of *Winterbottom v Wright* (1842) and created modern consumer protection.

## ❒ Bolton v Stone (1951)

> **Facts:** While Miss Stone was standing on a quiet suburban side street outside her garden gate in Manchester, a batter at the nearby cricket ground hit a huge shot that flew nearly 100 yards through the air, cleared a fence standing 17 feet above the pitch, and struck her on the head. Over the previous 30 years, a cricket ball had only been hit over that high fence about six times, and nobody had ever been hurt before.

- **STONE** (pedestrian hit on the head outside her garden) + **BOLTON** (cricket club committee, 17-foot fence, only six balls hit over in 30 years): **Does a defendant breach their duty of care simply because an accident was theoretically foreseeable, even when the probability of injury was extremely small?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A reasonable person is only required to take precautions against risks that are sufficiently likely to happen, not tiny, remote possibilities that an ordinary sensible person would brush aside.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Even though it was physically possible for a ball to clear the 17-foot fence once every few years, the chance of hitting a passer-by on a quiet street was so tiny that the cricket club did not breach its duty.
- **Principle**: Lord Reid and Lord Radcliffe established the **calculus of risk** for Breach of Duty: courts weigh the **likelihood of injury** and the **seriousness of the harm** against the cost of precautions and the social value of the activity.
- **Reasonable Care, Not Perfection**: Tort law demands reasonable care, not an absolute guarantee that no accident will ever happen.

## ❒ Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee (1957)

> **Facts:** Mr Bolam was a hospital patient suffering from severe depression who agreed to receive electroconvulsive therapy (ECT, passing a brief electric current through the brain to relieve depression, which causes strong muscle spasms). His doctor followed the practice of one respected group of psychiatrists by giving him no muscle-relaxant drugs and using no physical straps to hold him down, even though another respected group of psychiatrists always used them. During the spasms, both of Mr Bolam's hip sockets were fractured.

- **BOLAM** (hospital patient, fractured hip sockets) + **FRIERN HOSPITAL** (ECT given without muscle-relaxant drugs or straps, divided medical experts): **Is a doctor negligent when they follow a medical practice accepted as proper by a respected group of doctors, simply because another group of medical experts disagrees?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A skilled professional is not negligent if they act in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a **responsible body of skilled professionals** in that field.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — The hospital was not liable because a respected group of psychiatrists at the time opposed using muscle-relaxant drugs (which carried a risk of death) and physical restraints (which could make fractures worse).
- **Principle**: McNair J laid down the **Bolam Test**: a professional is judged not by the standard of an ordinary person on the street, but by the standard of the **ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that special skill**.
- **Modern Qualifications**: Today, under *Bolitho* (1998), the medical opinion must be **logical**, and under *Montgomery* (2015), *Bolam* no longer applies to **warning patients of treatment risks** (where doctors must warn of any risk a reasonable patient would consider significant).

## ❒ Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock and Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound No 1) (1961)

> **Facts:** While refuelling in Sydney Harbour, the crew of the cargo ship *Wagon Mound* carelessly spilled a large slick of heavy bunkering furnace oil onto the water, which drifted under the claimant's wooden ship-repair wharf. Everyone—backed by the science of the day—believed thick furnace oil floating on cold seawater could not possibly catch fire from sparks above, so the wharf manager told his welders to keep working. Two days later, red-hot metal fell onto a floating cotton rag in the oil, which acted like a candle wick, ignited the oil, and burned the wooden wharf down.

- **MORTS DOCK** (burned wooden wharf, welding sparks) + **OVERSEAS TANKSHIP** (spilled furnace oil on cold harbour water, unforeseeable fire): **Is a careless defendant liable for every direct physical consequence of their act, or only for damage of a kind/type that a reasonable person could have foreseen?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A defendant in negligence is only liable for damage of a **kind (or type)** that was reasonably foreseeable to a reasonable person at the time of the careless act.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Although the shipowners could foresee that spilling oil would cause minor **fouling (dirt and muck)** on the wooden posts, they were not liable for the destruction of the wharf by **fire** because fire on cold water was unforeseeable on the evidence in that case.
- **Principle**: Viscount Simonds and the Privy Council overruled the harsh 1921 rule in *Re Polemis* (which held defendants liable for all direct consequences) and established **reasonable foreseeability of the kind of damage** as the modern test for remoteness.
- **Kind vs Extent of Damage**: Because damage by fouling and damage by fire are two completely different *kinds* of damage, foreseeability of minor dirt did not make the shipowners responsible for an unforeseeable fire.

## ❒ Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd (1964)

> **Facts:** Before an advertising agency (Hedley Byrne) signed contracts making itself personally responsible for £100,000 of television and newspaper adverts for a client called Easipower, it asked its bank to check whether Easipower was financially sound. Easipower's bank, Heller & Partners, wrote back a free credit reference saying Easipower was trustworthy for its business commitments, yet printed a clear disclaimer at the top: "without responsibility on the part of this bank." When Easipower went bankrupt and the agency lost £17,000, the agency sued the bank in tort for its careless statement.

![Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd (1964): Heller & Partners signs a credit reference stamped "without responsibility," protecting the bank when Easipower goes bankrupt and Hedley Byrne suffers £17,000 in pure financial loss.](../images/01/10_Hedley Byrne.jpeg)

- **HEDLEY BYRNE** (advertising agency, £17,000 pure financial loss) + **HELLER & PARTNERS** (client's bank, careless credit reference, "without responsibility" disclaimer): **Can a defendant owe a duty of care in tort for a careless statement (negligent misstatement) that causes pure financial loss when there is no contract between the parties?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES (in legal principle) / ✖️ NO (on the facts because of the disclaimer) — A duty of care for a careless statement causing pure financial loss arises where there is a **special relationship** based on an **assumption of responsibility** and **reasonable reliance**, unless the speaker clearly disclaims responsibility.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Even though the House of Lords created the rule allowing claims for careless financial statements, the bank won because its express disclaimer ("without responsibility") prevented any assumption of responsibility from arising.
- **Principle**: Because spoken or written words can travel endlessly to thousands of strangers, simple foreseeability of financial loss under *Donoghue* is not enough; there must be a special relationship almost as close as a contract.
- **The Twin Pillars**: The claimant must prove that the advisor **voluntarily assumed responsibility** for the accuracy of their advice and that the claimant **reasonably relied** on their special skill and judgment.

## ❒ Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital Management Committee (1969)

> **Facts:** Three night-watchmen drank tea at work on New Year's morning that had been secretly poisoned with deadly arsenic by a murderer, and within twenty minutes they were vomiting violently. When they walked into the local hospital's emergency room at 8:00 a.m., the casualty doctor on duty felt unwell himself, refused to come downstairs to examine them, and told the nurse over the phone to send them home to call their own doctors. William Barnett died at 1:30 p.m., and his widow sued the hospital.

- **BARNETT** (poisoned night-watchman, lethal arsenic dose) + **CHELSEA & KENSINGTON HOSPITAL** (doctor refused to examine patient, chemical antidote would have arrived too late): **Is a hospital legally liable for a patient's death after a doctor carelessly refuses to examine him, if medical science proves the poison would have killed him anyway even with immediate treatment?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Factual causation fails under the **"But For" Test** if the victim's death or injury would still have happened on the balance of probabilities even if the defendant had acted carefully.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Although the hospital owed a duty of care and the doctor flagrantly breached that duty by refusing to examine Mr Barnett, the hospital paid zero damages because the massive dose of arsenic would have killed him before hospital tests could identify the poison and allow the antidote to work.
- **Principle**: Nield J held that the claimant must prove on a greater than 50% probability that, **but for (without)** the defendant's careless mistake, the harm would not have occurred.
- **Breach Without Causation**: Tort law only compensates damage actually caused by the breach; even terrible carelessness leads to zero liability if it made no difference to the outcome.

## ❒ Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd (1970)

> **Facts:** Three government prison officers took seven young offenders (Borstal trainees with criminal records for stealing vehicles and escaping) to Brownsea Island in the middle of Poole Harbour for an outdoor training exercise. Disobeying their orders, the officers went to sleep at night and left the boys unguarded. Seven boys escaped, climbed onto a yacht anchored right beside the island, and crashed it into the claimant's luxury yacht. The government argued it could never be sued in tort for crimes committed by third parties.

![Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd (1970): Sleeping Borstal officers on Brownsea Island leave seven trainees unguarded at night, who board the Silver Mist and crash into the Dorset Yacht Company's boat.](../images/01/09_Dorset Yacht.jpeg)

- **DORSET YACHT** (damaged yacht anchored beside the island) + **HOME OFFICE** (sleeping prison officers, escaped young offenders): **Does a government authority owe a duty of care to nearby property owners when its officers carelessly allow detainees under their direct control to escape and cause obvious, foreseeable damage?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — Where a defendant has a **special relationship of control** over dangerous detainees, they owe a duty of care to nearby victims who face an obvious, immediate risk if the detainees escape.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The Home Office was liable for the yacht damage caused by the escaped Borstal trainees while the officers slept in breach of their instructions.
- **Principle**: Lord Reid held that while a person is not normally liable for deliberate crimes committed by a third party, a duty of care arises where the defendant has control over the wrongdoer and the third party's harmful act is the **very kind of thing** that is extremely likely to happen if supervision fails.
- **Accountability of Public Authorities**: Lord Diplock confirmed that when government employees on the ground act so carelessly that they step outside their instructions, the state can be sued in tort for operational negligence just like a private citizen.

## ❒ Nettleship v Weston (1971)

> **Facts:** Mrs Weston asked a family friend, Mr Nettleship, to teach her how to drive her husband's car after showing him that the car had comprehensive insurance covering passengers. During her third lesson, while turning a corner slowly at walking pace, Mrs Weston froze in panic, failed to straighten the steering wheel, and drove onto the pavement into a lamppost, breaking Mr Nettleship's kneecap. She argued that because he knew she was a beginner, she should only be judged by the lower standard of an inexperienced learner doing her best.

- **NETTLESHIP** (injured driving instructor, fractured kneecap) + **WESTON** (panicked learner driver on her third lesson): **Is a learner driver judged by a lower, personalised standard of care towards an instructor or passenger who knows of their inexperience?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Every driver on a public road, including a beginner on their very first lesson, is held to the single objective standard of a **competent and experienced driver**.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Mrs Weston was liable in negligence for failing to straighten the wheel (with Mr Nettleship's damages reduced by 50% for contributory negligence because he shared control and failed to pull the handbrake in time).
- **Principle**: Lord Denning MR ruled that *"her incompetent best is not good enough"*: in the age of compulsory motor insurance, tort law applies one fixed, objective standard of care on the roads regardless of personal skill or experience.
- **Why Variable Standards Are Rejected**: Trying to measure a different standard of care for every driver based on how much each passenger knew about their skill would make courtroom trials unworkable and leave road victims uncompensated.

## ❒ Caparo Industries plc v Dickman (1990)

> **Facts:** Fidelity plc was a struggling electronics manufacturer whose annual accounts were audited under the Companies Act by a firm of accountants (Dickman). The audited accounts mistakenly reported a £1.3 million profit when Fidelity had actually suffered a £400,000 loss. Relying on those published accounts, an investment company called Caparo bought shares on the stock market and paid £13 million to take over Fidelity, then sued the auditors in tort for its financial losses.

![Caparo Industries plc v Dickman (1990): Caparo relies on Fidelity plc's audited annual report showing a £1.3M profit to launch a takeover, then discovers a £400,000 loss in the real accounts.](../images/01/11_Caparo.jpeg)

- **CAPARO** (corporate takeover investor, pure financial loss) + **DICKMAN** (company's statutory auditors, inaccurate published annual accounts): **Do a company's statutory auditors owe a duty of care to outside investors or individual shareholders who rely on published annual accounts to buy shares on the stock market?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Statutory auditors owe their legal duty solely to the company and its **shareholders as a collective group** for the purpose of overseeing how the company is run, not to individual investors deciding whether to buy shares.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — The auditors were not liable to Caparo because annual statutory audits are not prepared for the purpose of guiding stock-market purchases or corporate takeovers.
- **Principle**: Lord Bridge set out the famous **Caparo Three-Stage Test** for deciding whether a duty of care exists in a **novel situation**: (1) **reasonable foreseeability** of damage, (2) a relationship of sufficient **proximity** (closeness), and (3) that it is **fair, just, and reasonable** to impose a duty.
- **Transaction-Specific Proximity**: For careless statements causing financial loss, proximity exists only if the advisor knew their statement would be communicated to an identifiable claimant for a **specific transaction** and relied upon for that exact purpose.

## ❒ Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police (1992)

> **Facts:** At the 1989 Hillsborough Stadium football disaster, careless police crowd control allowed thousands of fans down a central tunnel into two fenced standing pens, crushing 96 supporters to death. Sixteen relatives and loved ones—who were either sitting in other parts of the stadium, watching the tragedy unfold on live television, or identifying their relatives' bodies at the mortuary at midnight—developed medically diagnosed psychiatric illnesses (such as PTSD) and sued the police as secondary victims.

- **ALCOCK** (bereaved relatives and fiancée, diagnosed psychiatric illness) + **SOUTH YORKSHIRE POLICE** (careless crowd control at Hillsborough, live TV broadcasts & midnight mortuary identification): **Can secondary victims recover compensation for pure psychiatric injury without passing strict legal tests of family closeness, physical presence, and direct unaided perception?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Secondary victims (people who were never in physical danger themselves) can recover for psychiatric illness only if they satisfy **four strict Control Mechanisms**.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — All sixteen claimants lost because they either failed to prove an exceptionally close tie of love and affection (such as adult brothers who are not presumed close without special proof), watched the event on television rather than with their own unaided eyes at the scene, or arrived at the mortuary eight hours later outside the "immediate aftermath."
- **Principle**: The House of Lords divided psychiatric claimants into **Primary Victims** (people physically inside the zone of danger) and **Secondary Victims** (onlookers who witness injury to someone else) and placed tight limits on Secondary Victims to prevent thousands of claims from a single disaster.
- **The Four Alcock Control Mechanisms**: A Secondary Victim must prove (1) a **close tie of love and affection** (presumed *only* for parents/children and spouses/fiancés; siblings and others must prove exceptional closeness), (2) **proximity in time and space** to the accident or its immediate aftermath, (3) **direct perception through their own unaided senses** (not television or being told by a third party), and (4) a recognised psychiatric illness caused by a **sudden shock**.

## ❒ Hunter v Canary Wharf Ltd (1997)

> **Facts:** During the regeneration of London's derelict East End docks, developers built the 250-metre stainless-steel Canary Wharf skyscraper (which blocked television signals for thousands of local homes for two years) and blasted a new link road (which covered local houses in thick clouds of construction dust). Hundreds of local residents—including husbands, wives, adult children, and lodgers who lived in the houses as their family homes without owning or renting them—sued in private nuisance.

- **HUNTER** (East London residents, including family members who did not own or rent the homes) + **CANARY WHARF** (250-metre steel skyscraper blocking TV reception & roadworks dust): **Can a family member who lives in a home without owning or renting the land sue in private nuisance, and is blocking television signals by building a tall building on your own land an actionable nuisance?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Only a person with a **property right (proprietary interest) or exclusive possession** of the land can sue in private nuisance, and the mere presence of a tall building on a neighbour's land is not a nuisance.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Spouses, children, and lodgers whose names were not on the property deeds or rental lease had no right to sue in nuisance for the dust, and nobody at all could sue in nuisance for the blocked television signals.
- **Principle**: Overruling *Khorasandjian v Bush* (1993), the House of Lords reaffirmed that private nuisance is strictly a **tort against land**, not a tort protecting personal comfort; family members without a property right in the land are mere "licensees" who must use negligence or the Protection from Harassment Act 1997 instead.
- **The Emanation Rule**: Lawfully building a tall structure on your own land is not a nuisance simply because it blocks your neighbour's view, wind, or television waves; nuisance normally requires something harmful (such as smoke, noise, smells, or dust) to physically **emanate (come out)** from the defendant's land onto the claimant's land.

## ❒ Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services Ltd (2002)

> **Facts:** During the 1960s, Arthur Fairchild worked for two different employers who each carelessly exposed him to thick clouds of asbestos dust while cutting insulation boards. Decades later, he died of mesothelioma, a fatal cancer of the lung lining that can be triggered by a single microscopic asbestos fibre. Because medical science cannot prove which employer's single fibre triggered the cancer, both careless employers argued that his widow must lose under the strict "but for" test.

- **FAIRCHILD** (deceased worker, indivisible mesothelioma lung cancer) + **GLENHAVEN FUNERAL SERVICES** (multiple careless asbestos employers, scientific impossibility of proving which fibre caused the cancer): **When multiple employers have each carelessly exposed a worker to asbestos dust and medical science cannot prove which employer's fibre triggered mesothelioma, can the claimant prove causation by showing a material increase in risk?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — Where the limits of human medical science make it impossible to prove by the "but for" test which of several careless asbestos exposures triggered mesothelioma, proving that a defendant **materially increased the risk** of contracting the disease is sufficient to establish causation.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The House of Lords held both careless employers liable, refusing to let wrongdoers escape paying compensation simply because two employers had poisoned the worker instead of one.
- **Principle**: Lord Bingham created the **Fairchild Exception** to the *Barnett* "but for" test: in the special case of mesothelioma and single-agent scientific impossibility, fairness and justice for dying workers outweigh strict causal proof.
- **100% Compensation Under the 2006 Act**: Under **section 3 of the Compensation Act 2006** (passed after *Barker v Corus*), every defendant who materially increases a victim's risk of mesothelioma is **100% jointly and severally liable** for the victim's full damages.

## ❒ Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police (2018)

> **Facts:** Two plain-clothes police officers tried to arrest a suspected drug dealer on a busy shopping pavement in Huddersfield right as Mrs Robinson, a frail 76-year-old passer-by, walked past. During the struggle, the two officers and the suspect crashed straight into Mrs Robinson, knocking her to the ground and injuring her with the men falling on top of her. The police argued that under Stage 3 of *Caparo*, it was not "fair, just, and reasonable" to hold police officers liable in negligence when catching criminals.

- **ROBINSON** (76-year-old pedestrian knocked over on the pavement) + **WEST YORKSHIRE POLICE** (officers tackling suspect, positive physical act): **Do police officers owe an ordinary duty of care when their own positive physical actions foreseeably injure an innocent passer-by in the street, without applying the Caparo three-stage test?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — Public authorities and police officers owe an ordinary duty of care not to cause foreseeable physical injury by their **positive acts**, governed by **established categories** of negligence rather than the *Caparo* three-stage test.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The Chief Constable was liable to pay damages to Mrs Robinson because the officers failed to take reasonable care for the safety of an elderly pedestrian standing right beside the arrest.
- **Principle**: Lord Reed and the UK Supreme Court clarified that **Caparo v Dickman (1990) did not create a universal three-stage test** for all negligence claims; courts must apply **established categories** of duty first (such as positive acts causing physical injury) and use the *Caparo* test **only in genuinely novel situations**.
- **Positive Acts vs Omissions**: While the police generally owe no duty of care for **omissions** (failing to stop a third-party criminal from harming a citizen), they have zero special immunity when their own **positive physical actions** directly injure a member of the public.
