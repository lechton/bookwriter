# Chapter 1: The Architecture of Civil Wrongs: 250 Years of UK Tort Law in One Unbroken Chain

## 1. The Blood, the Brick, and the Bank Balance: What Tort Law Is Actually Doing

### Forget the Latin and Ask One Question: When Life Breaks, Who Pays for the Pieces?

Walk out of this lecture hall, step onto the pavement, and look around you. A delivery van mounts the kerb because the driver is checking a text message. A crane swings a steel girder over a crowded street. A surgeon holds a scalpel three millimetres from a patient's vocal cords. An auditor signs off on a set of company accounts that are pure fiction. Every single second, millions of strangers are packed together in a modern society, rubbing shoulders, wielding machines, giving advice, and building fires next to their neighbours' fences. Most of the time, nobody gets hurt. When things go wrong, however, a life is shattered, a house burns down, or a fortune vanishes overnight.

Criminal law asks whether the state should lock the wrongdoer in a cage. Contract law asks whether two people made a private deal before the disaster happened. Tort law asks a far more brutal, urgent question: **between two strangers who never signed a contract, should the courts force the person who caused the harm to reach into their pocket and pay the victim, or must the victim swallow the loss alone as a cruel twist of fate?**

That is the entire game of tort law. The word *tort* comes from the Norman French for a "wrong" or a "twisted act," derived from the Latin *tortum*. If you treat tort law as a random list of twenty or thirty cases to memorise before May, you will drown in the exam. If you see it for what it truly is, you will never forget a single case. UK tort law is a single, unbroken 250-year argument about where to draw the boundary between your freedom to act and your neighbour's right to be safe. Every case on our list was born because an earlier rule broke down under the pressure of real human conflict. Every judge you will meet in this chapter is trying to solve the same terrifying dilemma: **how do we give justice to injured victims without paralysing society under a mountain of crushing, endless lawsuits?**

> **Streetwise Exam Advice:** Examiners can spot a student who memorised isolated flashcards in thirty seconds. Top-First answers treat the cases like a relay race: you show how a judge in 1932 was answering a problem created in 1842, and how the Supreme Court in 2018 finally killed off a mistake made in 1990. Connect the dots, and you own the room.

## 2. The Sacred Fortress: Trespass, State Power, and the Old World of Direct Wrongs

### Before Negligence Existed, the Common Law Guarded Two Things Above All Else: Your Body and Your Land

Let us rewind the clock to the eighteenth century, before factories, railways, or motor cars existed. Early English tort law did not care much about carelessness; it cared about **boundaries**. Your body and your land were sacred fortresses, protected by the ancient writ of **trespass**. If someone stepped across your boundary line without permission, the law did not ask whether they meant well or whether they broke anything. The crossing of the line *was* the wrong.

Suppose four government officers kick down your front door at dawn, ransack your study for four hours, and carry off your private notebooks because you wrote pamphlets criticising the King's ministers. When you sue them, the government shrugs and says, "We are the state, and we acted out of state necessity to protect national security." **Can the government invade a citizen's private property and seize their papers without an explicit statute or common law rule giving them that power?**

In **Entick v Carrington (1765)**, Lord Camden CJ delivered one of the greatest knock-out punches in legal history: **no**. A private citizen is free to do anything the law does not forbid, whereas a public official may do *nothing* unless positive law expressly authorises it. "State necessity" is not a defence known to the common law. Even if the King's messengers had not scratched a single floorboards, the moment their boots crossed John Entick's threshold without a valid warrant, they committed the tort of trespass to land (*trespass quare clausum fregit*). Tort law is not merely about broken bones and dented bumpers; it is the steel backbone of British constitutional liberty.

### What Happens When Someone Hurts You Without Laying a Finger on You or Stepping on Your Land?

Trespass is magnificent when someone kicks down your door or punches you on the nose. It is actionable *per se*, meaning you win without proving a single penny of physical damage. Yet trespass had a fatal blind spot: the injury had to be **direct** and physical.

What if a cruel prankster walks into your kitchen and tells you a malicious lie that your husband has just been smashed in a horrific traffic accident, with both legs broken, and that you must take two pillows in a cab to fetch him home? Nobody touches you. Nobody steps on your land uninvited. Yet the shock is so violent that your hair turns white and you suffer weeks of vomiting and permanent nervous collapse. **Can the law touch a defendant who inflicts devastating psychiatric and physical harm purely through spoken words?**

In **Wilkinson v Downton (1897)**, Wright J refused to let the prankster hide behind the gaps in the old forms of action. Because the harm was indirect, battery did not fit; because fraud only covered financial loss, deceit did not fit. Wright J therefore forged a brand-new intentional tort: where a defendant **wilfully does an act calculated to cause physical harm** to the claimant, and actually causes physical or recognised psychiatric damage, the law imputes an intention to produce that natural result. Words alone can be weapons, and tort law will not let a calculated sadist walk away free simply because no fist was thrown.

> **Streetwise Exam Advice:** Never confuse **Entick v Carrington (1765)** with **Wilkinson v Downton (1897)**. *Entick* is direct trespass: no damage needed, strict boundary protection, and the birth of the Rule of Law. *Wilkinson* is indirect intentional harm: damage is essential, and it bridges the gap between ancient trespass and modern psychiatric injury (which we will meet again in *Alcock* and recently saw refined by the Supreme Court in *Rhodes v OPO (2015)*).

## 3. The Industrial Crucible: Forging the "Reasonable Person" and the Trap of Contract

### Why "I Did My Best" Is the Worst Defence in a Courtroom

When the Industrial Revolution hit Britain, the old world of direct trespass could no longer cope. Steam engines exploded, stagecoaches overturned at high speed, and crowded properties caught fire. Most of these disasters were not deliberate attacks; they were the result of **carelessness**. As negligence began to emerge as an independent tort in the 1830s, the courts immediately hit a philosophical wall.

Imagine a farmer named Menlove piles up a massive stack of damp hay right against his neighbour Vaughan's cottages. Everyone in the village knows damp hay ferments and bursts into flames. For five weeks, neighbours beg him to take it down. Menlove replies that he will "chance it," and even cuts a ventilation hole through the stack that acts like a blast furnace. The hay explodes into fire and burns Vaughan's cottages to the ground. In court, Menlove's barrister makes a clever plea: "My client is a man of exceptionally low intelligence. He acted honestly, to the very best of his own weak judgment. How can you blame a man for not being cleverer than God made him?" **Is negligence judged by the defendant's personal best efforts, or by an objective external standard of ordinary prudence?**

In **Vaughan v Menlove (1837)**, Tindal CJ slammed the door on subjective excuses forever. If liability depended on how smart each defendant happened to be, the law would be "as variable as the length of the foot of each individual." Tort law is not a morality play judging the purity of your soul; it is a system of social insurance that guarantees a minimum baseline of safety for everyone around you. The moment you step into society, you are measured against the **objective standard of the person of ordinary prudence**.

### Does the "Reasonable Person" Have to Be a Prophet Who Predicts Record-Breaking Disasters?

Once **Vaughan v Menlove (1837)** locked in the objective standard, defendants panicked. Does ordinary prudence mean you are liable every time your equipment fails?

Twenty years later, in **Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co (1856)**, a water company maintained an underground fireplug outside Mr Blyth's house. It worked flawlessly for twenty-five years. Then came the brutal winter of 1855, bringing a frost of unheard-of severity. Ice formed deep underground, encrusted the stopper, and forced water into Blyth's home. **Does a defendant breach their duty of care when an unprecedented natural freak of nature causes properly maintained equipment to fail?**

Baron Alderson gave us the classic definition of negligence that every law student must know by heart: *negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided upon those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do.* The reasonable person is prudent, not clairvoyant. Because the 1855 frost was beyond human foresight, the water company breached no duty. Pair *Vaughan* and *Blyth* together in your mind: *Vaughan* tells you that you cannot drop below the ordinary person's standard by pleading stupidity, while *Blyth* tells you that you are not required to rise above the ordinary person's foresight by predicting miracles or freaks of nature.

### When You Are Completely Innocent of Carelessness, Can Your Land Still Bankrupt You?

Hold on: what if you are just as careful as the water company in *Blyth*, yet you bring a massive, unnatural hazard onto your own land to run your business?

In **Rylands v Fletcher (1868)**, a mill owner named Rylands hired competent engineers to build a giant water reservoir on his land. Neither Rylands nor the engineers knew (though the engineers should have spotted) that beneath the soil lay five ancient, filled-in coal shafts leading straight into his neighbour Fletcher's active coal mines. When the reservoir was filled, millions of gallons burst through the old shafts and drowned Fletcher's mine. Under *Blyth*, Rylands personally did nothing careless. **Should a landowner be strictly liable without personal fault when a dangerous accumulation on their land escapes and ruins a neighbour?**

Blackburn J (affirmed with Lord Cairns's famous gloss in the House of Lords) said **yes**. When you make a **non-natural use** of your land by accumulating something on it that is likely to do mischief if it escapes, you keep it at your peril. If it escapes, you are **strictly liable** for all the natural consequences, no matter how careful you were, and you cannot hide behind the mistakes of your independent contractors. (Keep a mental note for later: in *Transco plc v Stockport MBC (2003)*, the House of Lords confirmed that this rule is now a specialised sub-species of private nuisance, triggered only by an extraordinary, unusual use of land.)

### The 90-Year Prison of Privity: Why Injured Victims Were Locked Out of Court

While landowners were fighting over reservoirs and haystacks, a far darker rule was strangling injured workers and consumers. In **Winterbottom v Wright (1842)**, Wright had a contract with the Postmaster General to keep mail coaches safe. Wright did a shoddy job, a latent defect in an axle gave way, and the coach collapsed, crippling the driver, Winterbottom, for life. Winterbottom had no contract with Wright; he only worked for the company that supplied the horses. **Can a third party crippled by a defective product sue the negligent contractor in tort when there is no contract between them?**

Lord Abinger CB looked into the future, saw millions of consumers suing manufacturers, and panicked. He ruled **no**: if strangers to a contract could sue in tort for its negligent performance, it would lead to "the most absurd and outrageous consequences." For the next ninety years, this **privity fallacy** acted as an iron shield for negligent manufacturers. If you bought a poisoned pie or a defective carriage and gave it to your friend, your friend could not sue the seller in contract (no privity) and could not sue the maker in tort (blocked by *Winterbottom*).

One brave judge tried to pick the lock early. In **Heaven v Pender (1883)**, a dock owner supplied a rotten staging rope to a shipowner, and a painter hired by a contractor fell and was injured. Most of the Court of Appeal let the painter win on a narrow point targeting invited visitors, yet Brett MR (later Lord Esher) saw the bigger picture. In a visionary minority judgment, Brett MR declared that whenever one person is placed in such a position with regard to another that everyone of ordinary sense would recognise that failure to use ordinary care would cause danger to their person or property, a **duty of care** arises even without a contract. In 1883, Brett MR was fifty years ahead of his time. The law was waiting for a decomposing snail in a Scottish café.

> **Streetwise Exam Advice:** When an essay question asks you about the evolution of the Duty of Care, do not start in 1932! Start with the villain of the story, **Winterbottom v Wright (1842)**, show how **Heaven v Pender (1883)** planted the seed of rebellion, and then bring in **Donoghue v Stevenson (1932)** as the liberating hero. That three-step arc turns an average answer into a masterclass.

## 4. The Paisley Snail and the Modern Duty of Care: From Neighbourhood to Incrementalism

### How a Decomposing Snail in a Bottle of Ginger Beer Shattered 90 Years of Legal Immunity

On a warm August evening in 1928, Mrs May Donoghue walked into Wellmeadow Café in Paisley, Scotland. Her friend bought her a pear and ice, poured over with ginger beer from an opaque dark glass bottle manufactured by David Stevenson. After Mrs Donoghue drank the first glass, her friend topped it up, and out floated the decomposing remains of a dead snail. Mrs Donoghue suffered severe shock and gastro-enteritis. Look at her legal trap under **Winterbottom v Wright (1842)**: she could not sue the café owner in contract because **her friend bought the drink**, and she could not sue the manufacturer Stevenson because she had no contract with him either! **Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care in tort to the ultimate consumer of a product when there is no contract between them and no chance to inspect the product before consumption?**

In **Donoghue v Stevenson (1932)**, by a nail-biting 3-2 majority in the House of Lords, Lord Atkin demolished *Winterbottom v Wright* and gave birth to modern negligence. Picking up the torch from Brett MR in **Heaven v Pender (1883)**, Lord Atkin transformed the biblical command "love thy neighbour" into the legal question: **who, in law, is my neighbour?**

His answer is the most famous passage in the common law world: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your **neighbour**, defined as persons who are so **closely and directly affected** by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation when directing my mind to the acts or omissions called in question. Alongside this grand **Neighbour Principle**, Lord Atkin laid down the **Narrow Ratio** for defective products: a manufacturer who sells products in a form that shows they are meant to reach the ultimate consumer without intermediate examination owes a duty of care to that consumer. Overnight, tort law broke free from the chains of contract.

### When Prisoner Trainees Steal a Yacht, Can You Sue the Government for What Third Parties Do?

Once **Donoghue v Stevenson (1932)** opened the gates, claimants pushed the Neighbour Principle into uncharted waters. It is one thing to say a manufacturer is liable for his own snail. What if the defendant did not smash your property themselves, but carelessly allowed *someone else* to do it?

In **Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd (1970)**, prison officers took seven Borstal boys (young offenders with criminal records and histories of escaping) to Brownsea Island in Poole Harbour for a training exercise. Instead of watching the boys at night, the officers went to bed and left them unsupervised. Five boys escaped, boarded a nearby yacht, and rammed it into the claimant's yacht. The government argued that under English law, no person owes a duty to stop one adult or youth from committing a crime against another, and that holding public authorities liable would wreck the prison system. **Does a public authority owe a duty of care to nearby property owners when its officers negligently allow detainees under their direct control to escape and cause foreseeable damage?**

Lord Reid and the House of Lords held **yes**. Ordinarily, the law imposes no liability for a pure **omission** or for the deliberate criminal acts of a third party. However, where the defendant stands in a **special relationship of control** over the third party (the Borstal officers' custody over the boys) and a special relationship of **proximity** to the victim (owners of boats moored right next to the island, who faced a uniquely high, obvious risk during an escape), a duty of care arises. Moreover, Lord Diplock made a vital constitutional link back to **Entick v Carrington (1765)**: unless a public authority acts reasonably within its statutory discretion, it can be sued in tort just like any private citizen.

### What Happens When Someone Loses Money Because of Careless Words Rather Than Careless Hands?

Wait: so far, every negligence case we have looked at involved **physical damage**—burned cottages in *Vaughan*, flooded mines in *Rylands*, gastro-enteritis in *Donoghue*, smashed yachts in *Dorset Yacht*. What if nobody's body or property is touched, and instead a defendant utters a **careless statement** that causes the claimant to lose a fortune in **pure economic loss**?

Suppose an advertising agency places advertisements on credit for a company called Easipower and asks its own bank to check whether Easipower is financially solid. Easipower's bank, Heller & Partners, replies (under a header saying "without responsibility on the part of this bank") that Easipower is "respectably constituted and considered good for its ordinary business engagements." Easipower goes bust, and the agency loses £17,000. **Can a defendant ever owe a duty of care for a negligent misstatement that causes pure financial loss without any contract or fiduciary relationship?**

In **Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd (1964)**, the House of Lords recognised why careless words are far more dangerous to the legal system than careless driving. If a lorry crashes, the physical damage stops at the bottom of the hill. If an analyst utters a careless financial opinion on television or in a newspaper, thousands of strangers across the country might lose millions before lunchtime. To stop this **floodgates** catastrophe, their Lordships ruled that simple foreseeability of harm (the *Donoghue* test) is **never enough** for pure economic loss. Instead, a duty of care for negligent misstatement arises only where there is a **special relationship** between the parties, built on two twin pillars: a **voluntary assumption of responsibility** by the speaker, and **reasonable reliance** by the recipient. (On the actual facts of *Hedley Byrne*, the bank escaped liability only because its express **disclaimer** prevented any assumption of responsibility from arising in the first place!)

### Why Buying Shares on the Stock Market Using Public Accounts Leaves You Empty-Handed

If **Hedley Byrne (1964)** opened the door to negligent misstatements, corporate investors soon tried to kick it off its hinges. In the late 1970s and 1980s (especially after Lord Wilberforce's expansive two-stage test in *Anns v Merton London Borough Council (1978)*), claimants argued that whenever harm was foreseeable, a duty existed unless policy said otherwise. The House of Lords decided it was time to slam on the brakes.

In **Caparo Industries plc v Dickman (1990)**, Caparo bought shares in a struggling company called Fidelity and launched a takeover after reading Fidelity's annual accounts, which had been audited by Dickman. Those accounts showed a healthy profit when Fidelity had actually suffered a massive loss. Caparo sued the auditors under *Hedley Byrne*. **Do statutory company auditors owe a duty of care to potential investors or existing shareholders who rely on published annual accounts to buy more shares on the open market?**

The House of Lords unanimously said **no**. Statutory audits are prepared for one narrow purpose: to let the **body of shareholders as a whole** exercise their collective governance rights at the company's general meeting, not to give free investment advice to individual speculators deciding whether to buy shares. In reaching that result, Lord Bridge buried the expansive approach of the 1970s and articulated the famous **Caparo Three-Stage Test** for novel duty of care situations:
1. **Reasonable foreseeability** of damage to the claimant;
2. A relationship of sufficient **proximity** (closeness and directness) between claimant and defendant; and
3. Whether it is **fair, just, and reasonable** to impose a duty of care.

For a negligent misstatement under *Caparo*, proximity exists only if the advisor knew that their statement would be communicated to the **claimant** (individually or as an identifiable class), in connection with a **particular transaction**, and that the claimant was very likely to rely on it for that exact purpose.

### The Supreme Court's 2018 Plot Twist: Stop Applying Caparo to Everything!

Here is a trap that catches 80% of law students every single year. After 1990, thousands of students (and quite a few judges and police barristers) started treating the **Caparo (1990)** three-stage test as a magic universal formula that must be applied to *every* negligence problem, asking in every case whether it is "fair, just, and reasonable" to let the claimant win.

Fast-forward to a busy shopping street in Huddersfield in **Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police (2018)**. Two plain-clothes police officers spotted a suspected drug dealer emerging from a bookmakers and decided to arrest him on the pavement. During the struggle, the two officers and the suspect barged straight into Mrs Elizabeth Robinson, a frail 76-year-old passer-by, knocking her to the ground and injuring her while they tumbled on top of her. The police argued that under the third stage of *Caparo*, it was not "fair, just, and reasonable" to impose a duty of care on police officers catching criminals, because fear of lawsuits would lead to defensive policing! **Are the police immune from ordinary negligence when their positive physical actions injure an innocent passer-by in the street?**

Lord Reed and the Supreme Court dismantled the police's argument and delivered the single most important modern clarification in UK tort law. First, **Caparo (1990) did not create a universal three-stage test for all negligence cases!** On the contrary, *Caparo* actually preached **incrementalism**: in the vast majority of cases, courts simply apply **established categories** of duty (like drivers on a road, doctors treating patients, or anyone whose positive act directly causes foreseeable physical injury to a person nearby). You only ever reach for the *Caparo* three-stage assessment in a **genuinely novel situation** where no precedent exists! Second, drawing a straight line all the way back to **Entick v Carrington (1765)** and **Dorset Yacht (1970)**, Lord Reed held that public authorities are subject to the exact same liabilities in tort as private individuals. While the police generally owe no duty to protect citizens from **omissions** or harm caused by third-party criminals (as in *Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire (1989)* and *Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales Police (2015)*), when police officers commit a **positive act** of knocking over an elderly lady in the street, they owe an ordinary duty of care just like anyone else.

> **Streetwise Exam Advice:** Write this in big red letters at the top of your exam notepad: **Never jump straight to the Caparo three-stage test if the defendant's positive act caused direct physical injury!** Because of **Robinson (2018)**, you must first ask whether the case falls within an **established category** of duty (positive physical injury = duty established under *Donoghue* and *Robinson*). Only pull out the *Caparo* three-stage test if the scenario is genuinely **novel**, or involves an **omission**, a **third party**, **psychiatric harm**, or **pure economic loss**. Getting *Robinson* right immediately puts you in the top 5% of candidates.

## 5. Raising and Measuring the Bar: Breach of Duty in the Real World

### Learner Drivers, Flying Cricket Balls, and Electroconvulsive Therapy: How High Is the Standard of Care?

Once you prove that the defendant owed the claimant a duty of care, you enter Stage Two of negligence: **Breach of Duty**. Remember our foundation stones from **Vaughan v Menlove (1837)** and **Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks (1856)**: the standard of care is **objective**, measured by what the reasonable person would do against foreseeable risks. In the twentieth century, three brilliant cases tested that objective standard to its absolute limits.

First, what happens when the defendant is a **total beginner** who is literally doing the activity for the third time in their life? In **Nettleship v Weston (1971)**, Mrs Weston wanted to learn to drive. Her husband's friend, Mr Nettleship, agreed to give her lessons after checking that she was covered by comprehensive insurance. On Lesson Three, while moving at walking pace, Mrs Weston panicked while turning left, failed to straighten the steering wheel, mounted the kerb, and hit a lamppost, fracturing Mr Nettleship's kneecap. She argued that because Mr Nettleship *knew* she was a learner, she only owed him the lower standard of care of a learner doing her best. **Does a learner driver owe a lower, personalised standard of care to an instructor who knows of their inexperience?**

Lord Denning MR and the Court of Appeal said an emphatic **no**. Echoing **Vaughan v Menlove (1837)** across a century of compulsory motor insurance, Lord Denning held that the learner driver must meet the exact same standard as a **competent, experienced driver**: "Her incompetent best is not good enough." Why? Because if the standard varied with every passenger's knowledge of the driver's skill, or with how nervous the driver felt that morning, courts would face an impossible inquiry and injured victims would go uncompensated despite compulsory insurance. In tort law, **inexperience is never an excuse**.

Second, what if the defendant is not inexperienced, and actually foresees that a tiny risk exists, yet decides to do nothing more about it? In **Bolton v Stone (1951)**, Miss Bessie Stone was standing quietly outside her garden gate on a suburban road when she was struck on the head by a cricket ball hit right out of the Cheetham Cricket Ground. It was a colossal hit: the ball travelled 100 yards and cleared a fence that stood 17 feet above the pitch. Over the previous thirty years, balls had only been hit over that fence about six times, and nobody had ever been hurt. **Does a defendant breach their duty of care merely because an accident was theoretically foreseeable, even when the probability of injury was infinitesimally small?**

The House of Lords held **no**. Foreseeability alone proves a duty exists, yet whether that duty was **breached** is a practical calculus of risk. A reasonable person does not take precautions against every microscopic possibility in life; they only guard against risks that are sufficiently substantial that a reasonable person would not brush them aside as **fanciful or remote**. By weighing the tiny **likelihood of harm** against the cost and social reality of shutting down village cricket, their Lordships showed that tort law demands **reasonable care**, not absolute perfection. (Contrast that in exams with *Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951)*, where a low likelihood of a metal splinter flying still required goggles because the **gravity of the harm** to a one-eyed mechanic was total blindness!)

Third, what happens when the defendant is not an ordinary citizen on a road or a cricket pitch, but a **skilled professional** like a hospital doctor, an architect, or a lawyer? In **Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee (1957)**, Mr John Hector Bolam underwent electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) for severe clinical depression. The hospital did not give him muscle-relaxant drugs, did not strap him down manually, and did not warn him of the tiny (1 in 10,000) risk of fractures. During the seizure, he suffered devastating fractures of both hip sockets. At trial, eminent psychiatrists disagreed sharply: one respected school of medical thought used relaxant drugs and restraints, while an equally respected school believed relaxant drugs increased the risk of death and that physical restraints actually *increased* the risk of bone fractures! **Is a doctor negligent when they act in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion, merely because another group of experts would have done the opposite?**

McNair J directed the jury with the immortal **Bolam Test**: a professional is not judged by the man on the Clapham omnibus, nor by the highest genius in the profession, but by the standard of the **ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that special skill**.Crucially, a doctor **is not negligent if they have acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical men skilled in that particular art**, even if another body of opinion takes a contrary view.

> **Streetwise Exam Advice:** Whenever you cite **Bolam (1957)** in an exam, earn instant bonus marks by adding two modern upgrades in two sentences: first, under *Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority (1998)*, the body of medical opinion must withstand **logical analysis** (judges, not doctors, have the final say if a medical practice defies logic); second, under *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board (2015)*, the *Bolam* test **no longer applies to warning patients of risks** before consent—for disclosure of risks, the test is now what a **reasonable patient** would attach significance to!

## 6. The Causation Maze: Poisoned Tea, Asbestos Dust, and Harbour Infernos

### Why a Negligent Hospital Can Let a Patient Die and Still Pay Zero Pounds

Suppose the claimant proves a Duty of Care (*Donoghue*, *Robinson*) and proves a flagrant Breach of Duty (*Vaughan*, *Nettleship*, *Bolam*). They have still won nothing unless they can cross the bridge of **Causation and Remoteness**. Tort law does not punish bad behaviour in a vacuum; it compensates **damage caused by the breach**.

Consider one of the most cold-blooded, unforgettable cases in the canon: **Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital Management Committee (1969)**. At 5:00 a.m. on New Year's Day, three night-watchmen drank tea at work and began vomiting violently within twenty minutes. At 8:00 a.m., they staggered into the casualty department of the local hospital. One watchman lay doubled over on a bench in agony. The nurse telephoned the on-duty medical casualty officer, Dr Banerjee, who was feeling unwell himself after New Year's Eve. Without coming down to examine the men, Dr Banerjee told the nurse over the phone: "Tell them to go home to bed and call in their own doctors." By 1:30 p.m. that afternoon, William Barnett was dead. The coroner discovered that a murderer had laced their tea with fatal doses of **arsenic**. **If a hospital doctor negligently refuses to examine a dying man, is the hospital liable for his death when medical science proves he would have died from arsenic poisoning even if treated immediately?**

Nield J carefully separated the steps. Did the hospital owe Mr Barnett a duty of care once he walked into casualty? **Yes.** Did the doctor breach that duty by failing to examine a man vomiting violently? **Yes, flagrantly.** Was the hospital liable? **No!** To establish **factual causation**, the claimant must pass the **"But For" Test**: *but for* the defendant's breach of duty, would the injury have occurred on the balance of probabilities (greater than 50%)? The expert medical evidence showed that even if Dr Banerjee had rushed downstairs at 8:00 a.m., diagnosed arsenic poisoning (which takes hours of tests), set up an intravenous drip, and administered the only known antidote (BAL), Mr Barnett would already have absorbed a lethal dose and **would have died anyway** before the antidote could save him. Because the tragedy would have happened even without the negligence, the doctor's breach caused zero legal loss.

### When Five Employers Expose a Worker to Lethal Asbestos and Science Cannot Prove Which Fibre Killed Him, Does the "But For" Test Fail Justice?

The "But For" test in **Barnett (1969)** works cleanly when science can give us a clear answer. What happens when a claimant contracts a horrific, fatal industrial disease after working for **several negligent employers in succession**, and human medical science is physically incapable of proving *which* employer's dust triggered the fatal cell mutation?

In **Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services Ltd (2002)**, Arthur Fairchild (along with two other claimants) died of **mesothelioma**, an agonising, incurable cancer of the lining of the lungs caused by inhaling asbestos fibres. During his working life, Mr Fairchild had been negligently exposed to clouds of asbestos dust by more than one employer. Here was the scientific nightmare that delighted the employers' insurance companies: unlike asbestosis (which gets worse with every breath of dust you inhale), mesothelioma can be triggered by a **single asbestos fibre** (or a few fibres) mutating a single cell. Once that cell mutates, later exposures do not make the cancer worse. Because science cannot look inside a tumour forty years later and read a barcode on an asbestos fibre saying "Employer A" or "Employer B," the widows could never prove on a 51% balance of probabilities *which* employer caused the fatal mutation under the **Barnett (1969)** "but for" test! Under strict orthodoxy, **every negligent employer would walk away paying nothing**, leaving dying workers destitute because two employers had sinned instead of one. **When multiple defendants have each exposed a claimant to the same lethal agency and medical science cannot identify which exposure triggered an indivisible disease, should the law relax the strict "but for" test?**

Lord Bingham and the House of Lords looked at that result and declared that the policy of helping injured workers decisively outweighed the ritual of the "but for" test. Relying on and refining *McGhee v National Coal Board (1973)*, their Lordships created the **Fairchild Exception**: in the tightly bounded scenario where multiple tortfeasors have each breached their duty by exposing the claimant to the same noxious agent (asbestos), and it is **scientifically impossible** to prove which exposure caused the disease, proving that a defendant **materially increased the risk** of contracting mesothelioma is treated in law as proving that they caused the disease. Justice triumphed over scientific uncertainty. (And after the House of Lords briefly restricted this to proportionate liability in *Barker v Corus (2006)*, Parliament immediately stepped in with **section 3 of the Compensation Act 2006** to make every such asbestos defendant 100% jointly and severally liable!)

### A Spark on Oil-Soaked Water: Even If You Caused the Disaster in Fact, Was the Type of Damage Too Bizarre to Be Your Fault in Law?

Suppose you pass the "But For" test of **Barnett (1969)**. You factually caused the disaster. Does that mean you have to pay for every freakish chain reaction that follows until the end of time? That question takes us to **Remoteness of Damage** (also called legal causation).

For forty years after *Re Polemis (1921)*, the rule in England was harsh: once your careless act could foreseeably cause *any* tiny scratch of damage, you were liable for **all direct physical consequences**, no matter how bizarre or unforeseeable they were. Then came a ship called the *SS Wagon Mound* moored in Sydney Harbour.

In **Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock and Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound No 1) (1961)**, the crew of the *Wagon Mound* carelessly allowed a huge slick of bunkering furnace oil to spill into the bay, where it drifted beneath the claimant's timber wharf. The wharf owners were using oxy-acetylene welding torches to repair another ship. When they asked whether it was safe to keep welding above the oil slick, everyone—backed by the best scientific belief of the day—agreed that heavy furnace oil floating on cold seawater **could not possibly catch fire**. Two days later, a drop of molten metal fell from the wharf onto a piece of floating cotton waste floating in the oil; the cotton acted like a candle wick, ignited the oil, and burned the timber wharf to ashes. The trial judge found that the oil spill foreseeably caused minor **fouling and muck** on the slipways, yet the **conflagration by fire** was completely unforeseeable. **Is a negligent defendant liable for all direct physical consequences of their act, or only for damage of a kind that was reasonably foreseeable?**

Viscount Simonds and the Privy Council buried *Re Polemis* forever and established the modern law of remoteness: it ignores civilised justice to hold a defendant liable for every freakish direct consequence just because some minor, completely different harm was foreseeable. The true test of remoteness is whether the **kind (or type) of damage** suffered by the claimant was **reasonably foreseeable** by the reasonable person at the time of the breach. Because damage by **fouling** is a fundamentally different *kind* of damage from destruction by **fire**, and fire was unforeseeable, the shipowners escaped liability for the burned wharf.

> **Streetwise Exam Advice:** Once you state the rule in **The Wagon Mound No 1 (1961)** that the **type of damage** must be reasonably foreseeable, always pair it with its three famous companions so the examiner knows you see the whole picture: (1) under *Hughes v Lord Advocate (1963)*, the **precise manner** in which the accident happens need not be foreseeable as long as the *type* of harm (e.g., a burn) is foreseeable; (2) under *Smith v Leech Brain & Co Ltd (1962)*, the **Eggshell Skull Rule** means you take your victim as you find them once the initial type of physical injury is foreseeable; and (3) in *The Wagon Mound (No 2) (1967)*, when the owners of the *ships* moored at the wharf sued with better scientific evidence showing a tiny, real risk of fire that cost nothing to avoid, they actually won!

## 7. The Outer Frontiers: Psychiatric Shock and the Battle Over Private Land

### Why Watching Your Loved Ones Die on Live Television Is Not Enough to Win in Court

We have now walked through Duty, Breach, Causation, and Remoteness for ordinary physical injuries. Why, then, does the law suddenly throw up giant barbed-wire fences when the injury is **pure psychiatric harm** (what older judges called "nervous shock")? For exactly the same reason we saw in **Hedley Byrne (1964)**: a single catastrophe can traumatise tens of thousands of people who never suffered a physical scratch.

On 15 April 1989, one of the worst tragedies in British history unfolded at Hillsborough Stadium in Sheffield. Because South Yorkshire Police negligently allowed an overwhelmingly large crowd of Liverpool supporters into the fenced pens at the Leppings Lane end, ninety-six fans were crushed to death and hundreds more were injured as the disaster was broadcast on live television. The police admitted negligence towards those crushed in the pens. However, in **Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police (1992)**, sixteen claimants—parents, brothers, sisters, a brother-in-law, a grandfather, and a fiancée—sued for devastating psychiatric illness (such as PTSD) suffered after seeing or hearing about their loved ones' deaths. Some were in other parts of the stadium; some watched the horror unfold on live TV; one identified his brother's body in the makeshift mortuary at midnight. **What strict control mechanisms must a secondary victim satisfy to recover damages for pure psychiatric harm caused by witnessing the death or injury of another person?**

Building on *McLoughlin v O'Brian (1983)* and later crystallised alongside *Page v Smith (1996)* and *White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police (1999)*, the House of Lords divided psychiatric victims into two sharp categories:
- **Primary Victims**: People who were physically inside the zone of danger (or reasonably believed they were in physical danger). Under *Page v Smith*, they recover if any physical injury was foreseeable.
- **Secondary Victims**: People who were never in physical danger themselves, yet suffered a recognised psychiatric illness from witnessing the shock of injury to someone else.

To prevent unlimited liability, **Alcock (1992)** ruled that a Secondary Victim must prove not only that psychiatric illness was reasonably foreseeable in a person of customary phlegm, but also satisfy four cumulative **Alcock Control Mechanisms**:
1. **A Close Tie of Love and Affection** with the immediate physical victim (presumed only for **spouses/fiancés** and **parents/children**; brothers, sisters, grandfathers, and friends must prove exceptional closeness with hard evidence);
2. **Proximity in Time and Space** to the accident or its **immediate aftermath** (turning up at a mortuary eight or nine hours later was held to be outside the immediate aftermath);
3. **Perception through Unaided Senses** (you must see or hear the event with your own eyes and ears at the scene; watching live television broadcasts did not count because camera rules prohibited showing recognisable suffering individuals, and hearing the news from a third party never suffices); and
4. **Sudden Shock**: The psychiatric illness must be induced by a sudden, horrifying assault on the nervous system, not the gradual accumulation of grief or stress. Every single claimant in *Alcock* failed at least one of these merciless hurdles. (And in 2024, the Supreme Court in *Paul v Royal Wolverhampton NHS Trust (2024)* doubled down on *Alcock*, holding that relatives who witness a loved one collapse months after a doctor's negligent failure to diagnose an illness cannot claim because there was no external "accident"!)

### From Entick's Front Door to a London Skyscraper: Who Owns the Right to Sue When Your Home Is Made Unbearable?

Notice how our twenty-case journey comes full circle. We opened in **Entick v Carrington (1765)** with the sanctity of land against direct physical invasion (trespass), and visited **Rylands v Fletcher (1868)** where an unnatural accumulation escaped underground to flood a neighbour's mine. That brings us to the tort of **private nuisance**—unlawful, indirect interference with a person's use or enjoyment of their land—and to the gleaming glass tower of One Canada Square in London's Docklands.

In **Hunter v Canary Wharf Ltd (1997)**, hundreds of residents living in East London brought two claims against the developers of the 250-metre Canary Wharf tower and the London Docklands Development Corporation. First, the giant metallic skyscraper blocked their **television reception** for years until a relay transmitter was built. Second, construction of the Limehouse Link road threw clouds of **thick dust** onto their homes. Look closely at who the claimants were: they included not only homeowners and tenants, but also **husbands, wives, children, and lodgers** who lived in the houses as their family homes without holding any legal title or lease in the property. Earlier Court of Appeal decisions (like *Khorasandjian v Bush (1993)*) had tried to modernise nuisance by allowing anyone who lived in a home to sue, turning nuisance into a branch of personal negligence. **Can a family member without a proprietary interest in land sue in private nuisance, and is blocking television signals by simply building a tall building on your own land an actionable nuisance?**

The House of Lords answered **no to both questions** and restored the ancient purity of property law:
1. **Standing to Sue Requires a Proprietary Interest**: Private nuisance is a **tort against land**, not a tort protecting personal comfort. Only a claimant with a **right to exclusive possession** of the land (a freeholder, a tenant under a lease, or a person in exclusive de facto possession) can sue in private nuisance. Mere licensees—spouses not on the deeds, children, and lodgers—have no standing in nuisance and must look to negligence or the Protection from Harassment Act 1997 instead.
2. **Mere Presence of a Building Is Not an Actionable Nuisance**: Absent an ancient easement, English law has never given a landowner a right to an uninterrupted view, a breeze, or television waves passing across a neighbour's plot. Generally, **something must emanate** from the defendant's land (like smoke, noise, smells, dust, or Japanese knotweed) to constitute a nuisance, though the Supreme Court in *Fearn v Board of Trustees of the Tate Gallery (2023)* recently clarified that intense visual intrusion from a public viewing gallery staring straight into someone's living room can qualify when it comes from an abnormal use of land!

## 9. The 20 Foundational Cases in Logical Summary Format

Now that you hold the narrative thread connecting every rule from 1765 to 2018, study each of the twenty foundational cases below. Read the **Facts** to anchor the human story, the **Conflict Equation** to spot the issue under exam pressure, the **Decision** and **Liability** verdicts to lock in the outcome, and the **Principles** to master the exact legal reasoning.

## ❒ Entick v Carrington (1765)

> **Facts:** John Entick wrote articles that criticised the King's government. One day, four royal officers broke into his house, searched his rooms for four hours, and took all his private papers. When Entick sued them for trespassing, the government claimed it had the power to enter any home in the name of state security.

- **ENTICK** (pamphleteer, private home, seized papers) + **CARRINGTON** (King's messengers, executive warrant): **Can the state enter private property and seize goods without explicit statutory or common law authority?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — The executive holds no inherent prerogative to invade private property; every state intrusion is a tort unless justified by positive law.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The King's messengers were liable in trespass for breaking into Entick's home and seizing his papers under an invalid warrant.
- **Principle**: Lord Camden CJ established that while private citizens may do anything not forbidden, public officials may do nothing without express legal authority.
- **State Necessity**: "State necessity" is unknown to the common law as a defense to trespass, rendering a Secretary of State's general warrant wholly void.
- **Strict Property Protection**: Stepping onto another's land without license—even without physical damage—constitutes an actionable trespass (*trespass quare clausum fregit*).

## ❒ Vaughan v Menlove (1837)

> **Facts:** Menlove built a large pile of damp hay right next to his neighbour Vaughan's cottages. People warned him for five weeks that the hay would catch fire, but he refused to move it and said he would "chance it." After the hay caught fire and burned down Vaughan's cottages, Menlove argued he was not to blame because he was not smart enough to understand the danger.

- **VAUGHAN** (burned boundary cottages) + **MENLOVE** (damp haystack, "I'll chance it"): **Is negligence judged by an objective standard of ordinary prudence rather than the defendant's personal best judgment?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — Negligence is measured against an objective external standard of a person of ordinary prudence, not the defendant's individual intellect.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Menlove was liable for the fire even though he claimed to have acted honestly to the best of his own weak judgment.
- **Principle**: Tindal CJ rejected a subjective test because measuring care by personal judgment would be "as variable as the length of the foot of each individual."
- **Reckless Disregard**: Warned repeatedly for five weeks that his fermenting hayrick would ignite, Menlove refused to dismantle it and cut a chimney that accelerated combustion.

## ❒ Winterbottom v Wright (1842)

> **Facts:** The Post Office hired Wright to keep its mail coaches safe and in good repair. Because Wright failed to check the coach properly, a faulty axle snapped while Winterbottom was driving it, crippling him for life. When Winterbottom sued for his injuries, Wright argued he owed no duty to the driver because his contract was only with the Post Office.

- **WINTERBOTTOM** (crippled mail-coach driver) + **WRIGHT** (negligent maintenance contractor): **Can a third party injured by a defective product sue the contractor in tort without a direct contract between them?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A stranger to a contract cannot bring an action in tort for injuries caused by the negligent performance of that contract.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Although the injured driver needed protection from a latent axle defect, the contractor owed no tortious duty outside the Postmaster General's contract.
- **Principle**: Lord Abinger CB barred non-parties from suing in tort to prevent "the most absurd and outrageous consequences" of unlimited liability.
- **Historical Impact**: This privity barrier insulated negligent manufacturers and repairers from injured consumers for 90 years until *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932).

## ❒ Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co (1856)

> **Facts:** A water company installed a fireplug outside Blyth's house that worked safely for 25 years. During a record-breaking winter freeze in 1855, thick ice formed underground, blocked the plug, and flooded Blyth's home. Blyth sued the company for the damage, claiming they should have prevented the pipe from freezing.

- **BLYTH** (flooded householder) + **BIRMINGHAM WATERWORKS** (encrusted fireplug, extreme 1855 frost): **Does a defendant breach their duty of care when an unprecedented natural event causes properly maintained equipment to fail?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A defendant is only required to guard against reasonably foreseeable risks, not extraordinary natural events beyond human foresight.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — While the water company owed a duty to maintain its pipes safely, it was not liable when an unprecedented frost forced hidden ice into a sound fireplug.
- **Principle**: Baron Alderson defined negligence as omitting to do what a reasonable man would do, or doing something a prudent and reasonable man would not do.
- **Foreseeability**: Because the fireplug had operated safely for 25 years, the company could not be faulted for failing to anticipate a record-breaking freeze.

## ❒ Rylands v Fletcher (1868)

> **Facts:** Rylands hired builders to construct a large water reservoir on his land to power his mill. Unknown to Rylands, the ground underneath had old, forgotten coal shafts that led straight into his neighbour Fletcher's active coal mine. When the reservoir was filled, the water burst through the old shafts and flooded Fletcher's mine, even though Rylands had done nothing careless himself.

- **FLETCHER** (flooded neighbouring coal mine) + **RYLANDS** (mill reservoir, ancient mine shafts): **Is a landowner strictly liable without personal fault when a dangerous accumulation on their land escapes and damages a neighbour?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — A landowner who accumulates something likely to do mischief if it escapes is strictly answerable for all natural consequences of its escape.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Although Rylands committed no personal negligence, he was strictly liable when his newly built reservoir burst through old shafts into Fletcher's mine.
- **Principle**: Under Blackburn J's rule (refined by Lord Cairns), a "non-natural use" of land triggers strict liability when an accumulated hazard escapes and causes damage.
- **Non-Delegable Duty**: Rylands could not escape liability by blaming the independent contractors who negligently failed to seal the disused mine shafts.

## ❒ Heaven v Pender (1883)

> **Facts:** A dock owner put up a wooden stage hung by ropes around a ship so workers could paint the hull. Because one of the ropes had been burned and weakened beforehand, it snapped while Heaven, a ship painter hired by a contractor, was standing on the stage. Heaven fell and was badly injured, even though he had no contract with the dock owner.

- **HEAVEN** (injured ship painter, no contract with dock) + **PENDER** (dock owner, scorched staging rope): **Does a supplier of equipment owe a duty of care to a foreseeable user injured by defective gear despite the absence of a direct contract?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — The dock owner owed a duty to take reasonable care that the staging was safe for workers invited onto the premises to use it immediately.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Pender was liable for supplying a defective, scorched rope that collapsed beneath the painter.
- **Principle**: While the majority decided the case on the narrower ground of an occupier's duty to invitees, Brett MR formulated the first general duty of care based on foreseeable danger to others.
- **Precursor to Donoghue**: Brett MR's minority proposition—that a duty arises whenever ordinary sense recognises that carelessness will endanger another's person or property—laid the direct foundation for Lord Atkin's Neighbour Principle in 1932.

## ❒ Wilkinson v Downton (1897)

> **Facts:** As a cruel practical joke, Downton walked into Mrs Wilkinson's pub and told her that her husband had just been smashed in a terrible traffic accident with both legs broken. The story was a complete lie, but the shock was so severe that Mrs Wilkinson suffered weeks of violent vomiting, her hair turned white, and her nervous system collapsed.

- **WILKINSON** (terrified wife, severe nervous shock) + **DOWNTON** (malicious prankster, fabricated accident): **Is a defendant liable in tort for intentionally making a false statement calculated to cause severe physical or psychiatric harm?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — A person who wilfully does an act calculated to cause physical harm to another commits an actionable tort when such harm ensues.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Downton was liable for the severe nervous shock and physical illness inflicted on Mrs Wilkinson by his fabricated story.
- **Principle**: Wright J created a standalone intentional tort for indirect harm, holding that where an act is plainly calculated to produce physical or psychiatric injury, the law imputes an intention to cause it.
- **Bridging the Gap**: Because no physical contact occurred (ruling out battery) and no financial reliance was involved (ruling out deceit), this rule filled a vital gap for intentional infliction of harm.

## ❒ Donoghue v Stevenson (1932)

> **Facts:** Mrs Donoghue went to a café in Paisley where her friend bought her a bottle of ginger beer made of dark, opaque glass. After Mrs Donoghue drank half of it, her friend poured out the rest, and a rotting dead snail floated into her glass. Because her friend had bought the drink, Mrs Donoghue had no contract with the café or the manufacturer, David Stevenson.

- **DONOGHUE** (poisoned café patron, no contract of sale) + **STEVENSON** (ginger-beer manufacturer, decomposing snail in opaque bottle): **Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care in tort to the ultimate consumer when a defective product causes physical injury without any contract between them?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — A manufacturer owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer where goods are sold in a form that prevents intermediate inspection before consumption.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The House of Lords held that Mrs Donoghue had a valid cause of action in negligence against Stevenson despite the total absence of contractual privity.
- **Principle**: Lord Atkin formulated the **Neighbour Principle**: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought to have them in contemplation.
- **Demolition of Privity**: By separating tortious negligence from contract law, the 3-2 majority shattered the 90-year barrier of *Winterbottom v Wright* (1842) and created the modern tort of negligence.

## ❒ Bolton v Stone (1951)

> **Facts:** While Miss Stone was standing on a quiet road outside her garden gate, a batsman at the nearby cricket ground hit a massive six that flew 100 yards and cleared a 17-foot-high fence, striking her on the head. Over the previous 30 years, a ball had only been hit over that fence about six times, and nobody had ever been hurt before.

- **STONE** (pedestrian struck outside her garden) + **BOLTON** (cricket club committee, 17-foot fence, six hits in 30 years): **Does a defendant breach their duty of care when a foreseeable accident occurs, even though the probability of injury was infinitesimally small?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A reasonable person is only required to take precautions against risks that are sufficiently probable, not remote or fantastic possibilities.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Although it was foreseeable that a ball could occasionally clear the fence, the likelihood of hitting a passer-by on a quiet road was so small that the club did not breach its duty.
- **Principle**: Lord Reid and Lord Radcliffe established that breach of duty depends on weighing the **likelihood of injury** and the **seriousness of the consequence** against the burden of precautions.
- **Reasonable Care vs Insurance**: Tort law demands reasonable care, not a guarantee of absolute safety; where a risk is so small that a reasonable person would brush it aside, no breach occurs.

## ❒ Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee (1957)

> **Facts:** Mr Bolam agreed to undergo electroconvulsive therapy at the defendant's hospital to treat his severe depression. The doctor followed the practice of a respected group of psychiatrists by giving him no muscle-relaxant drugs and using no physical restraints, even though another respected group of doctors always used them. During the treatment, violent spasms fractured both of Bolam's hips.

- **BOLAM** (psychiatric patient, fractured hip sockets) + **FRIERN HOSPITAL** (ECT without relaxants or restraints, divided medical opinion): **Is a doctor negligent when they follow a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion, even though other experts disagree?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A professional is not negligent if they act in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of skilled professionals in that field.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — The hospital was not liable because a respected body of medical opinion opposed using relaxants and restraints during ECT at the time.
- **Principle**: McNair J formulated the **Bolam Test**: a professional is judged not by the ordinary layperson on the Clapham omnibus, but by the standard of the ordinary skilled person exercising and professing to have that special skill.
- **Room for Genuine Disagreement**: Medicine and science evolve through competing schools of thought; a doctor is not in breach merely because another expert would have adopted a different treatment.

## ❒ Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock and Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound No 1) (1961)

> **Facts:** The crew of the *Wagon Mound* carelessly spilled a large amount of furnace oil into Sydney Harbour, where it drifted under the claimant's timber wharf. Everyone believed furnace oil floating on cold water could not possibly catch fire, so the wharf owners kept welding until molten metal ignited a floating rag and burned the wharf down.

- **MORTS DOCK** (burned timber wharf, welding sparks) + **OVERSEAS TANKSHIP** (spilled furnace oil on seawater, unforeseeable ignition): **Is a negligent defendant liable for all direct physical consequences of their act, or only for damage of a type that was reasonably foreseeable?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — A defendant is only liable in negligence for damage of a **kind or type** that a reasonable person would have foreseen at the time of the breach.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Although the shipowners foreseeably caused minor oil fouling to the slipways, they were not liable for the destruction of the wharf by fire because ignition of furnace oil on water was unforeseeable.
- **Principle**: Viscount Simonds (in the Privy Council) overruled *Re Polemis* (1921), replacing the harsh "direct consequence" rule with the modern remoteness test of **reasonable foreseeability of the kind of damage**.
- **Kind vs Extent**: Liability turns on the specific *type* of harm (fouling vs fire); foreseeability of one type of minor damage does not make the defendant an insurer for an unforeseeable catastrophe of a different kind.

## ❒ Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd (1964)

> **Facts:** An advertising agency asked its bank to check whether a client called Easipower was financially safe before placing expensive ads on its behalf. Easipower's bank, Heller & Partners, wrote back saying Easipower was financially sound, but headed the letter "without responsibility on the part of this bank." When Easipower went bankrupt and the agency lost £17,000, the agency sued the bank for its careless reference.

- **HEDLEY BYRNE** (advertising agency, £17,000 pure financial loss) + **HELLER & PARTNERS** (client's bank, careless credit reference, express disclaimer): **Can a defendant owe a duty of care in tort for a negligent misstatement that causes pure economic loss without a contract?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES (in principle) / ✖️ NO (on the facts) — A duty of care for negligent misstatement arises where there is a special relationship of **assumption of responsibility** and **reasonable reliance**, unless excluded by an effective disclaimer.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Although the House of Lords recognised liability for negligent misstatements for the first time, the bank defeated any assumption of responsibility by heading its reference "without responsibility."
- **Principle**: Simple foreseeability of financial harm is insufficient because words travel endlessly; a duty for pure economic loss requires a **special relationship** akin to contract.
- **Twin Pillars**: The claimant must show that the advisor voluntarily assumed responsibility for the accuracy of the statement and that the claimant reasonably relied upon their special skill and judgment.

## ❒ Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital Management Committee (1969)

> **Facts:** Three night-watchmen drank tea at work that had been secretly laced with arsenic by a murderer and began vomiting violently. When they walked into the local hospital's casualty department at 8:00 a.m., the on-duty doctor refused to come downstairs to examine them and told the nurse by phone to send them home. William Barnett died five hours later, and his widow sued the hospital.

- **BARNETT** (poisoned night-watchman, fatal arsenic dose) + **CHELSEA & KENSINGTON HOSPITAL** (doctor refused to examine, antidote would have arrived too late): **Is a hospital liable for a patient's death after a negligent refusal to examine him when medical evidence proves he would have died even with proper treatment?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Factual causation fails under the **"But For" Test** if the claimant's injury or death would have occurred on the balance of probabilities even without the defendant's negligence.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Although the hospital owed a duty of care and the doctor breached it by failing to examine Mr Barnett, the hospital was not liable because the arsenic would have killed him before any antidote could work.
- **Principle**: Nield J held that the claimant bears the burden of proving that, *but for* the defendant's breach of duty, the harm would not have occurred.
- **Separation of Breach and Causation**: Even flagrant professional carelessness results in zero damages if the breach made no causal difference to the tragic outcome.

## ❒ Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd (1970)

> **Facts:** Prison officers took seven young offenders with histories of escaping to an island in Poole Harbour for a training exercise. Instead of supervising them at night, the officers went to sleep, allowing five boys to escape, board a nearby yacht, and crash it into the claimant's yacht. The government argued it could not be sued for crimes committed by third parties.

- **DORSET YACHT** (smashed yacht moored in harbour) + **HOME OFFICE** (sleeping Borstal officers, escaped trainees): **Does a public authority owe a duty of care to nearby property owners when its officers negligently allow detainees under their direct control to escape and cause foreseeable damage?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — Where a defendant holds a special relationship of control over dangerous detainees, they owe a duty of care to proximate victims who face a distinct, foreseeable risk from an escape.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The Home Office was liable for the damage caused by the escaped Borstal boys while the officers slept in breach of their instructions.
- **Principle**: Lord Reid held that while the law generally imposes no liability for the deliberate acts of third parties, a duty arises where the third party's action is the **very kind of thing** likely to happen if the defendant fails in their duty of supervision.
- **Public Authority Accountability**: Lord Diplock confirmed that once a public body acts wholly outside the reasonable exercise of its statutory discretion, its operational negligence is actionable in tort.

## ❒ Nettleship v Weston (1971)

> **Facts:** Mrs Weston asked her husband's friend, Mr Nettleship, to teach her how to drive after showing him that the car had full insurance covering passengers. During her third lesson, Mrs Weston panicked while turning a corner at walking pace, failed to straighten the wheel, and crashed into a lamppost, breaking Mr Nettleship's kneecap. She argued she should only be judged as a beginner doing her best.

- **NETTLESHIP** (injured driving instructor, fractured kneecap) + **WESTON** (panicked learner driver on third lesson): **Is a learner driver judged by a lower, personalised standard of care towards an instructor who knows of their inexperience?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Every driver on the road, including a complete beginner, is held to the single objective standard of a **competent and experienced driver**.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — Mrs Weston was liable in negligence for failing to straighten the steering wheel, subject to a 50% reduction for Mr Nettleship's contributory negligence in not grabbing the handbrake in time.
- **Principle**: Lord Denning MR declared that "her incompetent best is not good enough"; tort law measures conduct by a fixed objective yardstick backed by compulsory motor insurance, not by personal skill levels.
- **Rejection of Variable Standards**: Adjusting the duty of care according to how much each passenger knows about the driver's experience would produce endless uncertainty in the courts.

## ❒ Caparo Industries plc v Dickman (1990)

> **Facts:** Caparo bought shares in a struggling company called Fidelity and launched a corporate takeover after reading Fidelity's annual accounts, which had been audited by Dickman. When Caparo discovered the accounts were badly wrong and the company was worth far less than reported, Caparo sued the auditors for its financial losses.

- **CAPARO** (corporate takeover investor, pure financial loss) + **DICKMAN** (statutory company auditors, inaccurate annual accounts): **Do statutory company auditors owe a duty of care to potential investors or individual shareholders who rely on published accounts to buy shares on the market?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Statutory auditors owe their duty solely to the governance body of shareholders as a whole, not to individual investors making market decisions.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — The auditors were not liable to Caparo because the annual accounts were prepared for collective corporate oversight at the general meeting, not to guide share purchases.
- **Principle**: Lord Bridge formulated the **Caparo Three-Stage Test** for novel duty situations: (1) **reasonable foreseeability** of harm, (2) a relationship of **proximity**, and (3) that it is **fair, just, and reasonable** to impose a duty.
- **Purpose-Specific Proximity**: For negligent misstatements, proximity requires that the advisor knew their advice would be communicated to an identifiable claimant for a **specific transaction** and relied upon for that exact purpose.

## ❒ Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police (1992)

> **Facts:** During the 1989 Hillsborough Stadium disaster, police negligence allowed severe overcrowding in the terraced pens that crushed 96 football supporters to death. Sixteen relatives and friends who were either in other parts of the stadium, watching live television, or identifying bodies at the mortuary hours later developed psychiatric illness and sued the police.

- **ALCOCK** (bereaved relatives and friends, psychiatric illness / PTSD) + **SOUTH YORKSHIRE POLICE** (negligent crowd control at Hillsborough, TV broadcasts & mortuary identification): **Can secondary victims recover damages for pure psychiatric harm without satisfying strict proximity requirements of relationship, time, space, and unaided perception?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Secondary victims who were never in physical danger themselves can recover for psychiatric illness only if they pass four strict **control mechanisms**.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — All sixteen claimants failed because they either lacked proof of a close tie of love and affection (such as siblings or relatives without special proof), watched the disaster on television rather than with unaided senses, or arrived at the mortuary outside the immediate aftermath.
- **Principle**: The House of Lords distinguished **Primary Victims** (in the zone of physical danger) from **Secondary Victims** (witnesses to another's peril) and imposed strict legal filters to prevent unlimited liability.
- **The Four Control Mechanisms**: A Secondary Victim must prove (1) a **close tie of love and affection** (presumed only for spouses/fiancés and parents/children), (2) **proximity in time and space** to the event or its immediate aftermath, (3) **direct perception with unaided senses**, and (4) psychiatric illness caused by a **sudden shock**.

## ❒ Hunter v Canary Wharf Ltd (1997)

> **Facts:** Developers built the 250-metre Canary Wharf skyscraper and a major link road in East London. Hundreds of local residents—including spouses, children, and lodgers who did not own or rent the homes themselves—sued in private nuisance because the giant tower blocked their television reception for years and the roadworks covered their homes in dust.

- **HUNTER** (East London residents, non-owning family members, blocked TV signals & dust) + **CANARY WHARF** (250-metre skyscraper & road construction): **Can a resident without a legal interest in the land sue in private nuisance, and is blocking television reception by erecting a tall building an actionable nuisance?**
- **Decision**: ✖️ NO — Only a person with a **proprietary interest or exclusive possession** of land can sue in private nuisance, and the mere presence of a neighbour's building does not constitute an actionable nuisance.
- **Liability**: ✖️ NO — Claimants without a property right had no standing to sue for the dust, and no resident could sue for blocked television signals caused simply by the height of the tower.
- **Principle**: The House of Lords reaffirmed that private nuisance is a **tort to land**, not a tort protecting personal discomfort; mere licensees (spouses not on the deeds, children, lodgers) must rely on negligence or statutory remedies instead.
- **Emanation Rule**: Building lawfully on one's own land does not give rise to nuisance merely because it blocks light, wind, a view, or TV waves; nuisance almost always requires something noxious to **emanate** from the defendant's land onto the claimant's land.

## ❒ Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services Ltd (2002)

> **Facts:** Arthur Fairchild worked for several different employers over his career, and each one negligently exposed him to clouds of asbestos dust. Decades later, he died of mesothelioma, a fatal lung cancer that can be triggered by a single asbestos fibre. Because medical science cannot prove which employer's fibre triggered the cancer, all the employers argued they should pay nothing under the "but for" test.

- **FAIRCHILD** (deceased worker, indivisible mesothelioma cancer) + **GLENHAVEN FUNERAL SERVICES** (multiple negligent asbestos employers, scientific rock of uncertainty): **When multiple employers have each negligently exposed a worker to asbestos and science cannot prove which exposure triggered mesothelioma, can the claimant establish causation by proving a material increase in risk?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — Where scientific impossibility prevents a claimant from proving which of several negligent asbestos exposures caused mesothelioma, proving that a defendant **materially increased the risk** of the disease satisfies causation.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The House of Lords held the negligent employers liable, refusing to let multiple wrongdoers escape merely because science cannot trace a single fatal fibre.
- **Principle**: Lord Bingham carved out the **Fairchild Exception** to the strict *Barnett* "but for" test: policy and justice demand that an injured worker should not go uncompensated when the very nature of the disease makes strict scientific proof impossible.
- **Statutory Reinforcement**: Following *Barker v Corus* (2006), Parliament enacted **section 3 of the Compensation Act 2006**, ensuring that every defendant who materially increases the risk of mesothelioma is **100% jointly and severally liable** to the victim.

## ❒ Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police (2018)

> **Facts:** Two plain-clothes police officers tried to arrest a suspected drug dealer on a busy shopping street in Huddersfield. During the struggle, the officers and the suspect crashed into Mrs Robinson, a frail 76-year-old passer-by, knocking her to the ground and injuring her. The police argued it was not "fair, just, and reasonable" under *Caparo* to hold officers liable while catching criminals.

- **ROBINSON** (76-year-old pedestrian knocked down in the street) + **WEST YORKSHIRE POLICE** (officers tackling suspect, positive physical act): **Do police officers owe an ordinary duty of care when their positive physical actions foreseeably injure a passer-by during an arrest, without applying the Caparo three-stage test?**
- **Decision**: ✔️ YES — Public authorities and police officers owe an ordinary duty of care not to cause foreseeable physical injury by their **positive acts**, governed by established categories of negligence rather than the *Caparo* test.
- **Liability**: ✔️ YES — The Chief Constable was liable for Mrs Robinson's injuries because the officers failed to take reasonable care for the safety of an elderly pedestrian standing right beside the arrest.
- **Principle**: Lord Reed held that **Caparo v Dickman (1990) did not lay down a universal three-stage test** for all negligence claims; courts must apply **established categories** of duty first and use *Caparo* only in **genuinely novel** situations.
- **Positive Acts vs Omissions**: While the police generally owe no duty to protect citizens from harm caused by third-party criminals (an omission), they enjoy no blanket policy immunity when their own positive physical conduct injures an innocent bystander.
