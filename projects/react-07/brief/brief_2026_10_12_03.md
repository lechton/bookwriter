# Brief 2026-10-12-03: The Exact Term and Its Plain Meaning Together

## Why

Lecture 1's two-ways box read "(1) Change the page with **commands** (plain JavaScript)… (2) Change the page with a **description** (this chapter)…". The owner had already given the pattern: "(1) Manipulate DOM with **imperative programming**…", the technical term together with a plain explanation. The writer kept the plain half and dropped the term. Earlier lessons showed the opposite fault: the term with no plain meaning. This book prepares the reader for interviews, so the reader needs both: the exact term the interviewer expects (imperative and declarative, HTML and the DOM, props and `children`) and plain words that show they understand it.

The instructions caused it. Four places asked for plain words with no exception for the terms a lesson teaches: `lecture-voice` Section 03 ("every word the reader sees ... plain B2"), Section 05 ("rare or esoteric terms replaced with everyday vocabulary"), and Section 26 (the 8-Year-Old Reality Test, excluding words "from props, variables, and explanations"). No skill described the `[!WAYS]` box, and the map asked only for an option's "name", so map 01 carried the writer's own words "commands" and "a description". The rule that interviewers grade exact vocabulary sat only in the prompt-writing checklist, which the lecture writer does not read. Brief 2026-10-11-04 had also removed the etymology dimension along with the heavy term ritual; the owner wants one sentence of origin back for difficult terms (imperative: Latin *imperare*, "to command", the root of *emperor*).

The remedy prunes before it adds: the plain-word rules are narrowed to the names we choose, and one positive standard says what to do with a term.

## Group 1: The exact term and its plain meaning together (#2026_10_12_03_group_1)

- `lecture-voice` Section 22: a paragraph and two checks. Every idea an interviewer can ask about is named by its exact term, bold at first use, with its plain meaning in the same or the next sentence, and the same term everywhere after. One sentence of origin when it explains the term (imperative, declarative), none when it explains nothing (`children`, `className`). Proper example: the owner's imperative sentence with the emperor. Counter-examples: Lecture 1's commands/description box (plain words only) and "React uses **declarative** programming" (term only).
- `lecture-voice` Sections 03, 05, and 26: each now says it covers the names we choose (props, variables, titles, component names), and that the terms the lesson teaches follow Section 22. Nothing else in them changes.
- `lecture-structure` Section 12: standard 2's "proper name" is the exact term with its plain meaning. A new check describes the `[!WAYS]` box: one numbered paragraph per option, the same frame on each line, the exact term in bold, where it comes from, and its plain meaning. Proper example: the owner's Lecture 1 frame ("Update the DOM with **imperative programming** ..."). Counter-example: the commands/description box.
- `lecture-structure/intro-examples.md`, Example 1: a note that a rewrite can add one origin sentence to each term.
- `map-principles` Section 05B and the template: each Option starts with its exact technical term, never a word the writer made up.
