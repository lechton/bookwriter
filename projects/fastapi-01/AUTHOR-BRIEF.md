# AUTHOR BRIEF — writing one lecture for react-lecture-03

Paste this brief plus the question row into the writing model. The full rules live in `instructions.md`; this page is the working subset that fits a small context. When the build log is clean, the lecture is mechanically done.

## Operational Directives: `ppp` and `nnn`
- **`ppp` (Please Proceed)**: Execution trigger. Immediately proceed to execute the approved plan, author or edit files, and run builds without requesting further confirmation.
- **`nnn` (Chat-Only Constraint)**: Chat-only constraint. When prompt contains `nnn`, do NOT make any edits or create any files in the workspace, and do not run modifying commands. Strictly discuss, brainstorm, analyze, and refine in chat until explicitly ordered to proceed.
- **Cautious Visual Verification Gate (Strict Anti-Screenshot-Waste Law)**: Be cautious and do NOT scan screenshots or render pages (`pdftoppm`) of the build result during routine prose authoring, text revisions, or copy editing. Render screenshots ONLY if you made an actual change to the visual design, layout CSS, or figure geometry of the page, OR if there is a serious grounded suspicion of an actual visual layout error.

## Workflow Phases
- **Phase 1: Pre-Lecture / Prelecture Blueprint (`md-pre-lectures/{nn}.md`)**: First iteration of the material. Directly mirrors final lecture headings, mapping each block into atomic pre-element bullets (`[p]`, `[code]`, etc.) with in-situ pedagogical qualifications attached via `◼`. Locks in technical vocabulary. Follows `PRE-LECTURE-INSTRUCTIONS.md`.
- **Phase 2: Production Lecture (`md-lectures/{n}.md`)**: Full long-form lecture written from the approved pre-lecture / prelecture blueprint following the skeleton below.

## Input you receive
- One row from `./questions/questions.md`: number, tier (`❱ CORE` / `❱❱ MORE` / `❱❱❱ ADVANCED`, each possibly suffixed ` (Server)` for Q101–Q180), topic tag, question text, hook. The hook is the seed of your opening scenario; grow it, do not replace it.

## Write `md-lectures/{n}.md` with this skeleton, in this order
1. Line 1, exactly: `# Lecture {n}: {Short Title}`
2. Line 2, exactly: `> INTERVIEW QUESTION | {tier} | {question text copied verbatim}` (tier includes ` (Server)` when the row carries it)
3. Blank line, then the Opening Ladder: 5–7 numbered beats that open the body, in this order: the scene, the code moment, the question (a clear, direct sentence stating the visible conflict without cryptic brevity), the danger, the mystery, the promise. No sentence over 20 words, no em-dashes, and never name the lecture's mechanism term in the ladder (the term is earned later, in the body). The ladder is always numbered; bullets are for unordered lists. (The pattern is called the Opening Ladder here, not "Hook Ladder", because in a React course the word hook belongs to React.) The Interview Question box shows the question only: never write a leading scene line before the ladder, and never write the "Imagine this scenario:" lead yourself — the build generates the lead and wraps the ladder in its own styled section. Scenario clarity is absolute: write the beats for a tired B2 English reader with a headache, because the ladder is the reader's first contact with the topic and must be the clearest part of the whole lecture. Every noun in a beat is exactly one thing — a person (named with an unambiguous human role, e.g. "a journalist", "a subscriber", "a reader" — never add unnatural phrases like "a real person"), a thing visible on the screen, a thing in the code, or a machine event — never a bare overloaded word: "editor" (say "the journalist" for the person, "your code editor, the program" for VS Code), "live" (say "the site is open in the reader's browser right now"), "script", "log" (say "print to the console"), and the React mechanism words in disguise: "state", "props", "hook", "render", "mount", "hydrate", "trigger" — write the visible behavior instead ("the page keeps showing the old headline"). Each beat must make sense read alone and out of order. The ladder also carries the logic, never just the scene: every value that becomes the problem (a total, a count, a price) has its business rule stated in an explanatory sentence when introduced and then repeated attached to the on-screen value in the next beat (for example "The paper pays by the word." then "That total counts the words of every story, because the paper pays by the word.") — a value whose computation was never given is a cipher, and the reader cannot fear its loss; the change event (a new story lands) is shown before the stale screen, so the "why" beat points at an input the reader just watched move; and no technical shorthand ("needs a loop over every story, plus a safety check for broken records") may stand in for logic — unpack it into operations the reader can picture ("visit every story, add its words, skip any story with no word count"), re-define taught terms like "a computed value" in plain words in the same beat, and earn any "does not fit one line" wall by first classifying the value as one that needs steps, not one formula. Critical mandates with worked failing and passing ladders live in the "Opening Ladder" section of `instructions.md` (the three case studies: role rotation and phantom routines, the jargon and inside-out engine traps, and the Mismatch Law). Never force the question into a cryptic 4-word riddle — write a natural, complete question that explicitly states the visible conflict (e.g. "Why did London stay as the registered location, even after the update?"). Obey the universal laws: single actor throughout (never rotate reporter/correspondent/journalist), no phantom actors ("background routines"), no metaphorical questions ("Why did nothing move?"), no niche jargon ("bureau", "portal", "wire", "bulletin", "dispatch desk"), and the Mismatch Law (the beat that sets up the scenario must directly expose the contradiction between what the tool was told to do vs what the screen actually needs).
4. Right after the ladder comes the first `### ` section: a catchy title that names the chapter's idea, then the paragraphs that open the teaching and lead into the first code. Never leave a bare paragraph between the ladder and the first heading.
5. Body sections with `### ` headings only (`## ` forces a PDF page break; save it for `## Beyond the basics` audit sections). EVERY lecture includes one ` ```components title="... — Component Explorer" ` panel, mandatory with no exceptions: place it after the prose that names the visual arrangement and before the first code fence that implements the mechanism. The tree must contain EVERY file the lecture shows or imports (fence titles and import paths) and nothing else. Canonical scaffold: top-level folders, files one level deep — `components/` holds every `.jsx` (and `.tsx`) file, `state/` holds plain `.js`/`.ts` logic modules and appears only when one exists; write bare filenames in the block (never path prefixes; the build groups by file type); imports reflect the structure (`./Name.jsx` within `components/`, `../state/cart.js` from a component into a module). Module entries appear in the left file tree ONLY — the canvas shows rendered components only, and the builder filters modules out of it automatically, so indent a module under its importing root without worrying about the canvas. Only exception: React Server questions (Q101+) whose subject is the module structure itself (`'use client'` split, app routes). The third field (visual kind) must be one of: `shell`, `header`, `navigation`, `profile`, `badge`, `counter`, `card`, `form`, `table`, `button`, `branch`, `instances`, `generic`. The fourth field is the component's real rendered UI, lines separated by `;;`, with the values COMPUTED by hand from the lecture's actual code — `<p>Total payout tonight: {totalPayout}</p>` over three stories of 800, 1200, and 950 words at rate 0.5 becomes `Total payout tonight: **1475**`. Every END component gets the field (a literal string is computable too); a container whose children render inside it stays unfilled on purpose (the children are its UI); a logic module never renders; and every word in a fill must trace to the code block's JSX, the code block's starting values, or the prose's description of the screen — filled beats empty, but traced beats invented. The build warns on any lecture with zero panels and on any shown or imported file missing from the tree. EVERY lecture also embeds at least one standalone HTML figure via a ` ```html-figure src="figures/{nn}-{seq}-{slug}.html" caption="..." ` fence: author it as a self-contained HTML file in `md-lectures/figures/` with locally scoped styles, cloned from the closest of the 7 proven archetypes in `md-lectures/figures/templates/` and obeying `docs/figure-design-system.md` (never text-only, no raw `<h2>`/`<h3>` tags inside figures, keep the ~380px to 420px printable height budget); the build warns on any lecture with zero figures.
6. Code fences: ` ```jsx title="RealName.jsx" `. Real, runnable examples. Every line inside a fence renders as a numbered row, blank lines included, so keep fences dense. Keep every line under about 80 characters: break long JSX and object literals across rows yourself, one attribute or property per row — the built output wraps overflow as a safety net only.
7. Comments: `code; // annotation` at the END of the code line, never on their own line. Lead verdicts with `**RIGHT:**` / `**WRONG:**` and put one caps load-bearing word in `**bold**`. Never write `✔️`/`✖️`: the build strips them. Only `**bold**` works inside comments.
8. At least one `> [!TIP]` callout with a `**To impress the interviewer:**` lead, placed at the moment the tip matters. Use `> [!KEY]` for one-line insider takeaways. Include exactly one `> [!WILD]` card per major technical section (with eyebrow `⚡ IN THE WILD`) to isolate the student's single most common counter-intuitive confusion point (including any friction or confusion around classic vs modern framework nomenclature) and discharge it with an immediate "aha moment". Grounding cards MUST strictly obey **The Knife Paradigm**: open with a **short, catchy, bold leading question** (6–9 words, zero syntax clutter), speak with tactile everyday familiarity (*"Can you imagine...", "Remember the blinking cursor...?"*), take the reader by the hand with intentional repetition, **always bold all clarified key terms** (`**imperative**`, `**declarative**`, `**physical DOM**`, `**virtual DOM**`), avoid technical trivia or method names in the body, and deliver up-to-the-point arguments cutting like a knife (matching the cardinal benchmark in `PRE-LECTURE-INSTRUCTIONS.md`). Full markdown works inside callout bodies.
9. Right before the Glossary, a `### Where you will meet this` section: 3 to 5 one-line uses of today's concept in real apps the reader knows; the first line may be tonight's own case; each line is one pictureable moment plus what the concept does there — a concrete situation, never an abstract category. This list is the one place the lecture widens beyond its own world.
10. A `### Glossary` section positioned directly after `### Where you will meet this` and immediately before `### Summary`: 4 to 6 core terms introduced or reinforced in the lecture, each formatted on a single continuous line as `- **Term**: Plain-English definition and concrete engineering role without em-dashes.` The Glossary renders on its own dedicated standalone page in the PDF.
11. Close with `### Summary` written as a Streetwise Review from an experienced developer's daily perspective ("when will you write this?", "why does it matter in daily practice?"):
    - Lead the summary body with an authoritative **Technical Title** in bold (`**Technical Title**`) on its own line, followed by an empty line before the opening paragraph.
    - Use `❒ {Subtitle}` section headers.
    - Under each subtitle, use numbered points (`1.`, `2.`) with indented sub-bullets formatted with `<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(a) ...` so they stay on one continuous line without hard-wrapping.
    - Highlight core principles with `➔ NEVER / ALWAYS / IF ... THEN ...`.
    - Bold load-bearing words or phrases in every bullet.
    - Comparative right/wrong code fences with verdict in fence tag (` ```jsx right ` / ` ```jsx wrong `) under flush-left `**DO THIS:**` / `**DO NOT DO THIS:**` headers.
    - NEVER open the summary with conversational filler like "Look," or "Look, in practice...". Open directly with the developer frequency, architectural role, or consequence.
    - End with the comparison table as the LAST block of the file.

## The closing table (exact shape)
```
| | **COLUMN B**<br>(subtitle) | **COLUMN C**<br>(subtitle) |
| ---: | :--- | :--- |
| **Dimension** | value | value |
```
First header cell always empty; header titles are `**CAPS**` plus `<br>` plus a one-word subtitle; the divider row is exactly `| ---: | :--- | :--- |`; every body row starts with a bold dimension; table columns in PDF are narrow (~240px), so ALWAYS separate prose from code with `<br>` (e.g. `Imported from<br>`react-dom/client``) and manually wrap multi-part code across separate backticks with `<br>` (e.g. `createRoot(node)`<br>`.render(<App />)`) to prevent ugly PDF padding breaks. Never put `<br>` inside backticks.

## Hard format rules
- One continuous line per paragraph, bullet, and table row. Never hard-wrap.
- No em-dashes anywhere in the lecture.
- **Bold Key Terms Law**: Key terms in any paragraph MUST be in **bold** (e.g. `**imperative DOM scripting**`, `**state drift**`, `**component**`). Every concept introduced must be visually anchored with bold formatting.
- **The Complete Nomenclature Law (Classic & Modern Dual-Canon)**: Whenever you dedicate a paragraph or section to describing a runtime phenomenon, architectural pattern, or data structure, withholding its formal name is strictly prohibited. You must provide the **Dual-Canon**: (1) the **Classic Industry Term** (what 95% of senior interviewers call it across the table, e.g. `**virtual DOM**`), (2) the **Modern Official Term** (what modern specifications and docs call it, e.g. `**UI descriptor tree**`), and (3) its concrete physical reality (e.g. *lightweight JavaScript objects in memory*). Never describe a mechanism while dodging its formal name. If the terminology transition or concept creates common student confusion, dedicate an `⚡ IN THE WILD` grounding card to discharge that confusion.
- **Zero-Unexplained-Syntax Law**: Every parameter, keyword, attribute, method, or symbol appearing in a code snippet MUST be explicitly audited and line-by-line deconstructed in the surrounding prose. Consecutive back-to-back code blocks without intervening deconstructive prose are strictly forbidden.
- **Dual-Form CLI Command & Scaffolding Anatomy Law**: When introducing terminal scaffolding commands (e.g. `npm create vite`), present two distinct forms: (1) the **Generic Formula** in prose (e.g. `npm create vite@latest <app_name> -- --template react`), explicitly explaining that `<app_name>` defines the target directory on disk and the package name in `package.json` (requiring lowercase letters and hyphens without literal angle brackets), why `npm create` runs `create-vite` in memory, why the double-dash `--` is required to forward options to the generator rather than npm, and what `--template react` selects; and (2) the **Concrete Runnable Execution** in a terminal code fence with the chapter's actual project name (e.g. `national-weather`), ensuring copy-paste safety without shell redirection errors. Furthermore, thoroughly deconstruct what happens on disk across all generated files and folders: `package.json` (manifest & scripts), root `index.html` (entry shell with `<div id="root">` and `<script type="module">`), `vite.config.js` (registering `@vitejs/plugin-react` for HMR and compilation), `src/` (`main.jsx` mounting to `#root`, `App.jsx` root component, styles), and `node_modules/` with `package-lock.json` created by `npm install`.
- **The Bullet Decompression & Anti-Jargon Smuggling Law (The Tired Reader Standard for Lists)**: Bullet points are high-risk compression traps. Authors must never treat list items as an excuse to abbreviate explanations or smuggle unbaptized jargon. Every bullet point introducing a file, configuration, or dependency must be written for a tired developer with a splitting headache using CEFR B2 vocabulary. Never chain multiple unexplained systems (e.g. *"JSX compilation, Fast Refresh, and Rollup bundling"*) into a single sentence. For every tool or compiler mentioned, explain: (1) what it is, (2) what it physically transforms from $A$ to $B$, and (3) its tangible developer or browser benefit.
- **The Problem-Condition Heading Law ("When" Over "Why" for Failure States)**: Never use "Why" for headings introducing bugs, blank screens, performance delays, or runtime failure symptoms (e.g., never write *"Why does client-side rendering show a white screen?"*). "Why" falsely implies that the failure is an intentional design choice or a permanent reality. Always use **"When"** (e.g., *"When does client-side rendering show a white screen?"*), which correctly frames the problem as a situational condition, timing delay, or operational boundary.
- **The Anti-Tail-End Name-Dropping Law (Zero Orphan Buzzwords at Paragraph Ends)**: Never conclude a paragraph, section, or callout card by dropping a new technical term or proper noun into the final sentence without immediately unpacking its mechanics. If a named system (such as **Hot Module Replacement**) represents the climax or resolution of an explanation, you must physically deconstruct it in that exact place: state how the server connects to the browser (e.g. a live connection), what payload moves (e.g. only the changed module), and how the browser swaps it in memory without refreshing or clearing form state.
- **The Standalone Glossary Page Law**: Every lecture must include a `### Glossary` section positioned directly after `### Where you will meet this` and immediately before `### Summary`. The Glossary must render on its own dedicated standalone page in the PDF (`page-break-before: always;`). It defines 4 to 6 core terms introduced or reinforced in the lecture. Each term is formatted on a single continuous line as `- **Term**: Plain-English definition and concrete engineering role without em-dashes.`, rendering as unbulleted cards with `border-left: 3.5px solid var(--accent);` and bold Slate-900 titles.
- **The Right/Wrong Code Indicator Law (Summary & Anti-Pattern Comparisons Only)**: For right and wrong code comparisons, DO NOT put comments inside the code block explaining what is right or wrong. Put the instruction on the line directly above the fence using flush-left text without emojis, where **ONLY the uppercase directive is bold**: `**DO THIS:** [instruction in normal weight]` or `**DO NOT DO THIS:** [instruction in normal weight]` (never wrap the following explanatory text in bold). Add `right` or `wrong` directly to the code fence language tag (e.g. ` ```jsx right ` or ` ```jsx wrong `). This automatically generates the side-panel SVG indicator (emerald checkmark or rose cross). Scope of application is strictly restricted to: (1) closing `### Summary` blocks to summarize key points of thinking and error avoidance, and (2) direct in-body **anti-pattern comparisons** where a broken or legacy architectural approach is contrasted with a modern React 19 pattern (e.g. manual DOM queries vs declarative JSX). NEVER apply `DO NOT DO THIS` to terminal scaffolding commands, documentation placeholders, CLI flags, or standard setup steps.
- **Bespoke Accent Bullet Styling**: Body unordered lists use custom, enlarged geometric bullets (`● ` at `1.25em`) colored in `var(--accent)` (`#0e7490`), with nested sub-bullets styled as matching open rings (`○ ` at `1.1em`), establishing a cohesive visual brand with card borders and grounding callouts.
- **Marginalia Section Headings & Summary Typography Law**:
  1. **Strong Modern Typeface (Avenir Next)**: All headings (`h1, h2, h3, h4`), marginalia titles, summary subtitles, and bold keywords (`p strong, p b`) explicitly utilize **Avenir Next** (with system sans fallbacks) with heavy weights (`font-weight: 800; letter-spacing: -0.015em;` for titles; `font-weight: 800;` for paragraph bold terms). This completely prevents Prince from falling back to thin serif fonts and guarantees bold keywords in the signature teal accent (`#0e7490`) deliver high contrast, punchy clarity, and crisp geometric legibility against the justified body copy.
  2. **Main Lecture Title & Left-Margin Headings**: The main lecture title (`h1`) and section headings in the left marginalia column (`main h3, main h4`) use the signature teal accent color (`var(--accent)` = `#0e7490`), creating an instantly distinguishable visual navigation hierarchy alongside the black body copy.
  3. **Accent Bold Terms in Paragraphs**: All bold key terms inside narrative paragraphs (`p strong, p b`) render in the signature teal accent color (`var(--accent)` = `#0e7490`), providing vibrant visual landmarks for newly introduced concepts and core mechanisms.
  4. **Tight Box Justification**: Narrative paragraphs are fully justified (`text-align: justify; text-align-last: left; hyphens: auto;`), creating clean, tight, book-like rectangular columns without ragged right edges. Dedicated headers, interview questions, summary lead titles, principles (`➔`), and code lead-ins explicitly preserve left alignment (`text-align: left; hyphens: none;`).
  5. **Summary `❒` In-Column Subheadings**: Lines beginning with `❒` in `### Summary` automatically render as bold in-column subheadings (`.summary-subtitle`) with a bold title (`font-weight: 800`) and the `❒` glyph in signature teal (`var(--accent)`). They remain cleanly within the body column, never colliding with the left-margin "Summary" title.
  6. **Thick Accent Summary Numbered Lists**: Top-level numbered list markers (`1.`, `2.`) in `### Summary` render with thicker weight (`font-weight: 800; font-size: 1.1em;`) and matching teal accent color (`color: var(--accent);`), giving high visual prominence to streetwise takeaways.
  7. **Modern Inline Code Pills**: All inline `<code>` terms (e.g. `Masthead`, `<Masthead />`, `package.json`, `npm create vite@latest`) render as refined developer badges/pills with a soft ice-cyan tint (`background: #eef7f9;`), crisp accent border (`border: 1px solid rgba(14, 116, 144, 0.25);`), rounded corners (`border-radius: 5px;`), comfortable breathing room (`padding: 0.12em 0.38em; margin: 0 0.08em;`), `color: #0e7490;`, and `white-space: nowrap; hyphens: none;` so code tokens and multi-word CLI flags never split or hyphenate mid-token across lines.
  8. **Multi-Theme Publishing Law**: Every compilation (`node src/build-lectures.mjs`) automatically publishes the entire course reader in three distinct visual themes, once per theme, under the pipeline's reader name:
     - `React 19 Q01-Q12-teal.pdf` (**Teal Theme**): Signature Deep Teal / Cyan-700 (`#0e7490`) applied to the main title `h1`, left-margin headings (`main h3, main h4`), narrative bold keywords (`p strong, p b`), list bullets, code pills, and summary markers.
     - `React 19 Q01-Q12-black.pdf` (**Monochrome Black Theme**): Solid black (`#000000`) applied to the main title `h1`, left-margin headings (`main h3, main h4`), summary subtitles, and all bold keywords (`p strong, p b`) inside narrative paragraphs, paired with neutral slate inline code pills and slate borders.
     - `React 19 Q01-Q12-old.pdf` (**Old Theme**, the react-lecture-01 look): The same teal accent (`#0e7490`) on callout borders, but the -01 typography: system font stack, weight-700 black `h1` and left-margin headings, plain black bold keywords, default-size bullets, plain grey code chips, left-aligned paragraphs, and roomier comparison tables.
     The untagged `React 19 Q01-Q12.pdf` is the teal alias; no duplicate `deck-*` reader files are produced.
- Keep every technical term, and immediately define it in plain English in the same sentence.
- **Define through experience, never only through other words.** Start every new concept from what the reader has already done or seen in this course: a line they wrote, a prop they used, a file ending they know. Define the concept as that known thing plus one change, and teach only the change. If the concept is visible in code, define it by its shape: files so far contained A, this file adds one extra B there; without it the problem you already showed, with it the fix. After each definition the reader must be able to point at something and say what it does.
- **Build the lecture around the one thing.** Every concept reduces to one visible change — one line written differently, one prop added, one file created; even an invisible concept is created by one line or kept in one file. Find it, show it early and alone, and center the lecture on it. Then move from that concrete thing toward the abstract only through the reader's own question, asked out loud at the moment it arises: Why this? We already know a way. Answer it; every abstraction must answer a live question.
- **If the topic is a new part of the file, that growth is the headline.** Say plainly in the first section: your file grows a new part today. The story demonstrates the part; it is not the headline. The ladder's last beat promises the new part, not only the story's outcome.
- **Teach structural surprises before the fence that shows them.** If a construct breaks what the reader believes a React file can hold so far (a hook inside a conditional flow, a `'use client'` line at the top of a file, an `async` component awaiting data, JSX passed between a component's tags for the first time, a fragment), teach the surprise in prose first: name the model the reader holds, tell the reader the construct is allowed and normal, and say what marks the difference — before the first fence that shows it. Never reuse wording from these instructions in the lecture.
- **Raise the strongest naive alternative first.** The first "how else could we do this?" must be the one the course equipped the reader to think of (why not just pass it as a prop? why not a plain variable? why not an effect?); answer it before any weaker strawman. A tool whose real competitor goes unasked never becomes necessary.
- **Search the deck before baptizing a term.** Grep `md-lectures/` for the hook or concept first. If an earlier lecture taught it, re-anchor with its one-line reminder plus `(see Lecture N)` and deepen — never re-teach from zero with a fresh metaphor.
- **No invisible owners.** Any prose actor that owns the mechanism (the page that stamps out the copies) must appear in a fence or the components panel, or be cut from the prose; ground compound names at first mention (one sentence saying what each noun of the name means).
- **The ladder's last beat promises, never explains.** Promise what today brings, in plain words; the answer to the ladder's own question belongs to the body's first section.
- Length: 800–1,200 words (about 1,000). ADVANCED questions may reach 1,500. Do not pad; do not rush the mechanism either.
