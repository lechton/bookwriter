# Derived Values, Part 02: The Wiring

Part 01 explained what a derived value is, and why it is lazy. A derived value is a stored answer, a dirty flag, and a function, sealed together. The function defines the answer. The dirty flag says whether the answer can be trusted. The stored answer is the last value the function produced. On the first read, the function runs. On later writes to its inputs, the dirty flag flips. On later reads, the function runs only if the flag is up.

That explanation left one thing out on purpose. It never said how the derived value finds out that one of its inputs changed. The dirty flag does not flip by magic. Some wire has to connect the input to the derived value, and some signal has to travel along that wire when the input changes, and that signal has to be the thing that flips the flag. This part is about that wire. It is the hardest part of the whole mechanism to hold in your head, because the wire is a connection between two named things, and connections between named things are exactly where explanations usually lose the reader. So this part will go slowly. Uncomfortably slowly, by the standards of a writer who already understands it. That slowness is the point.

## The two things we are about to connect

Before any wire exists, we need two things for the wire to connect. We will build the wire between them one step at a time, and we will not introduce a third thing until the wire between these two is solid.

The first thing is a signal, and we will call it price. A signal, in this vocabulary, is a reactive box holding one value, plus its own private list. The private list belongs to price. No other signal shares it. No derived value shares it. It is price's list, sealed inside price, and only price's own read and write functions can reach it. Price starts holding the number 3, and price's private list starts empty.

The second thing is a derived value, and we will call it total. A derived value, in this vocabulary, is a stored answer, a dirty flag, a function, and its own private list. The function is the rule that produces total's answer, and for this example the rule is price times 100. Total's stored answer starts empty. Total's dirty flag starts true, meaning total has never computed and therefore its stored answer cannot be trusted yet. Total's own private list starts empty.

Two things, named. Price, a signal. Total, a derived value. Each has its own private list, and the two lists have nothing to do with each other yet. The lists are not connected. Price's list is empty. Total's list is empty. Nothing is wired to anything.

## What "wired" is going to mean

We are about to make a connection. Before we make it, we have to say precisely what the connection is, because the word wired gets used loosely and the looseness is where the confusion lives.

The connection we are about to build is a one-way relationship. The relationship is: the signal price will, at some future moment, need to tell the derived value total that price changed. For price to be able to tell total anything, price has to be able to reach total. The way price reaches total is by holding a reference to a piece of total inside price's own private list. When that reference is sitting in price's list, we say price is wired to total. The wire is not a cable and not a metaphorical pipe. The wire is an entry in a list. One entry. Sitting in one list. That is the entire physical reality of the wire.

Hold that. The wire is an entry in a list. Specifically, the wire is an entry in price's private list, and that entry points at a piece of total. When the entry is there, price can reach total. When the entry is not there, price cannot. There is no other wire. There is no other channel. The list is the only wire that exists.

## The piece of total that sits in price's list

Here is the part that is almost always glossed over in one phrase, and that we are going to slow down for, because the phrase-version loses everybody.

When price's list holds an entry that points at total, what exactly is the entry? It is not total itself. Total is a bundle of a stored answer, a dirty flag, a function, and a list. Price's list does not hold that whole bundle. Price's list holds one specific piece of total.

The specific piece is a small function that lives inside total, and whose entire job is to flip total's dirty flag and nothing else. We are going to give this small function its own name, because it needs one, and we are going to call it markDirty. So: total contains a small function called markDirty. MarkDirty belongs to total. MarkDirty is sealed inside total's closure, alongside total's stored answer and total's dirty flag. MarkDirty is part of total. And the entry that sits in price's list is markDirty. Not total. Not total's function. Not total's stored answer. MarkDirty, the small flag-flipping function that lives inside total.

Restate the same fact from the other side, because the connection is the kind of thing that has to be approached twice. From price's side: price's list contains markDirty. From total's side: total's markDirty is sitting in price's list. Same fact, two orientations. Price reaches total by holding markDirty in its list. Total is reached by price because total's markDirty is what price is holding. The wire is the entry, the entry is markDirty, markDirty is part of total. One connection, stated twice, named every time.

## Why the entry is markDirty and not something else

There are other candidates for what price's list could hold, and the reason the real mechanism chooses markDirty is worth a moment, because the choice is the whole architecture.

Suppose price's list held total's main function, the one that does the arithmetic. Then, when price changed, price would call total's main function. That would mean total recomputes the moment price changes. That is the opposite of lazy. That is eager. We spent all of part 01 refusing exactly that behavior. So total's main function is not the right thing for price to hold.

Suppose instead price's list held the function that originally asked for total's value, some outer piece of code that read total once and would like to be told if total changes. Then, when price changed, price would call that outer function. But that outer function knows nothing about total's internal arithmetic. It cannot recompute total. It is the wrong shape. So that is not the right thing for price to hold either.

The right thing for price to hold is a function whose entire body is: flip total's dirty flag, and then pass the news along to whoever is sitting in total's own private list. That function is markDirty. When price changes and calls markDirty, two things happen, and only two things. Total's dirty flag flips to true. And markDirty, having flipped the flag, looks at total's own private list, and tells anyone sitting there that total is now stale. No arithmetic. No recomputation. Just a flag flip and a notification. That is the smallest possible unit of work that still connects a change in price to a consequence in total, and that is why markDirty is the thing that sits in price's list.

## How the entry actually gets into price's list

We have said what the wire is, and we have said what the entry is. We have not yet said how the entry gets into the list in the first place. This is the third connection in the mechanism, and because it is the third we are going to slow down for it the same way we slowed down for the first two.

The entry gets into price's list during total's first read. Recall from part 01 that the first read is special. On the first read, total's function runs, because total's dirty flag is true and the rule is recompute when dirty. Now, total's function is the rule price times 100. To compute price times 100, the function has to read price. Reading price means calling price's read function.

Here is the moment to slow all the way down, because this single moment is where the wire is built. Total's function calls price's read function. While that call is happening, total is the active derived value, the one currently running. Price's read function, the moment it is called, checks who is currently running. The currently running thing is total, or more precisely a piece of total, the same markDirty we have been talking about. Price's read function takes that currently running thing, markDirty, and drops it into price's private list.

State the sequence as a sequence, because it is a sequence and the order matters. Total is read. Total's dirty flag is up, so total's function runs. Total's function needs price, so total's function calls price's read function. Price's read function sees that total, in the form of markDirty, is the thing currently running. Price's read function adds markDirty to price's private list. Price's read function returns the value of price. Total's function multiplies and finishes. Total stores the answer and clears its dirty flag. The read is over.

At the end of that sequence, price's private list contains one entry, and that entry is markDirty. The wire has been built. Price now holds a reference to a piece of total, and that reference is the wire. It got there because, while total's function was running and reading price, price's read function took the opportunity to record who was reading it. The recording is the wiring.

## What the wire does, the first time price changes

Now that the wire exists, we can watch it carry a signal. This is the fourth connection, and we will slow down for it too.

Price changes. Specifically, price is written to, with a new value. What does it mean for price to change. It means price's write function is called with a new value. Price's write function does three things, in order. It updates the stored value of price. It looks at its own private list, the list that now contains markDirty. It calls, one by one, every function sitting in that list.

The list contains exactly one entry. That entry is markDirty. So price's write function calls markDirty.

State that again, because it is the wire in action and it has to land. Price's write function looks at price's private list. The list contains markDirty. Price's write function calls markDirty. The call is the signal traveling along the wire. The wire was the entry in the list. The signal traveling along the wire is the write function calling the entry. That is the whole physical reality of a signal traveling. A loop, over a list, calling each entry. Nothing more.

What happens when markDirty is called. Two things, and only two things, in order. First, markDirty flips total's dirty flag to true. Total's stored answer is still there, still holding the old value, but it is now flagged as untrustworthy. Second, markDirty looks at total's own private list, and calls every function sitting there. Total's private list is empty in this example, because nothing has been set up to read total yet. So the second step does nothing. The first step, the flag flip, is all that happens.

The whole chain, from price changing to total being marked stale, took: one value update, one list lookup, one function call, one boolean flip. No arithmetic. Total did not recompute. Total's stored answer is still the old value, but it is now flagged as needing recomputation the next time somebody reads total.

## The proof, watched from inside the lists

We can watch every one of those steps by inspecting the lists directly. Here is a derived value total, depending on a signal price, with a counter that proves nothing has been computed yet.

```js
const price = makeSignal(3);
const total = makeDerived(() => price.read() * 100);
```

Before anything is read, both lists are empty. Price's list is empty, total's list is empty. No wire exists yet. There is nothing in either list because total has never run and therefore has never read price.

```js
console.log("price's subscriber list size:", 0);   // price's subscriber list size: 0
console.log("total's own subscriber list size:", 0); // total's own subscriber list size: 0
console.log("is total dirty?", true);               // is total dirty? true
```

Now read total once. Total's dirty flag is up, so total's function runs. Total's function reads price. Price's read function records who is reading it. The reading is the wiring.

```js
console.log("total.read() returns:", total.read()); // total.read() returns: 300
```

After that single read, price's list contains exactly one entry. We can ask what that entry is named, and the answer is markDirty. We can ask whether that entry is total's markDirty, and the answer is yes.

```js
console.log("price's subscriber list size:", 1);                          // price's subscriber list size: 1
console.log("the entry is named:", "markDirty");                          // the entry is named: markDirty
console.log("is it total's markDirty?", true);                            // is it total's markDirty? true
console.log("is total dirty now?", false);                                // is total dirty now? false
```

The wire has been built. Price's list holds total's markDirty. Total's dirty flag is now false, because the first read computed the answer and cleared the flag.

Now write to price. Price's write function updates the value, looks at its list, finds markDirty, and calls it. MarkDirty flips total's dirty flag to true. Total's list is empty, so nothing else happens.

```js
price.write(9);
console.log("price's list still holds:", "markDirty"); // price's list still holds: markDirty
console.log("is total dirty now?", true);              // is total dirty now? true
console.log("total.read() returns:", total.read());    // total.read() returns: 900
```

Read total again. The flag is up, so total's function runs again. It reads price again, gets 9, multiplies, returns 900, stores it, clears the flag. The re-read also re-confirms the wire harmlessly, by adding markDirty to price's list again, but a list only holds each entry once, so nothing visibly changes.

This is the full life of one wire, watched from inside the lists that carry it. Before any read, no wire. After the first read, a wire: price's list holds markDirty. After a write to price, the wire carries a signal: price calls markDirty, markDirty flips total's flag. After a read of total, the flag triggers the recompute. Every step is inspectable, and every inspection matches the mechanism described above.

## The second wire: total's own list, and who sits in it

We have over-established one wire. The wire between price and total exists because total's first read dropped markDirty into price's list. That wire carries signals from price to total.

There is a second wire in this mechanism, and it is built by the same rule, and we are now ready for it because the first wire is solid. The second wire is the wire between total and whatever reads total.

The thing that reads total might be a piece of code that wants to know total's value and wants to be told when total changes. In our vocabulary that piece of code is called an effect, and effects are the things that actually do work when a value changes, like printing to the screen or updating the DOM. An effect that reads total is, from total's perspective, exactly what total is from price's perspective. The effect is downstream of total, the way total is downstream of price.

When an effect reads total, total's read function does the same thing price's read function did. It looks at who is currently running. The currently running thing is the effect. Total's read function takes the effect and drops it into total's own private list.

Hold the symmetry, because the symmetry is the whole mechanism and it is easy to miss. Price's list holds whatever reads price. Total's list holds whatever reads total. The same rule, applied at two levels. When total read price, price's list gained markDirty. When an effect reads total, total's list gains the effect. The lists are the wires, the rule that fills them is the same rule at every level, and that is why the whole thing chains.

So now there are two wires. Price's list holds total's markDirty, wiring price to total. Total's list holds the effect, wiring total to the effect. Price is upstream of total. Total is upstream of the effect. The effect is downstream of total. The whole chain, two wires, three named things.

Watch the second wire fill, by setting up an effect that reads total.

```js
const price2 = makeSignal(3);
const total2 = makeDerived(() => price2.read() * 100);
watchEffect(() => { console.log("  effect body ran, total2 is:", total2.read()); });
```

That effect body, the moment it runs, reads total2. Total2's read function sees the effect running and drops the effect into total2's own list. We can inspect both lists now, and both have one entry.

```js
console.log("price2's list holds:", "markDirty");                    // price2's list holds: markDirty
console.log("total2's OWN list size:", 1);                           // total2's OWN list size: 1
console.log("the entry in total2's list is named:", "run");          // the entry in total2's list is named: run
```

The entry in total2's list is named run because that is the internal name every effect's body carries. It does not matter what the entry is named. What matters is that total2's list now holds the effect, the way price2's list holds total2's markDirty. Two lists, two entries, two wires, one chain.

## The whole chain, when price changes, end to end

Now we can trace one change all the way through both wires, because both wires exist. This is the final connection, and we have earned the right to state it as a sequence because every link in the sequence has been over-established individually.

Price2 changes. Price2's write function updates the value of price2. Price2's write function looks at price2's private list. Price2's list holds total2's markDirty. Price2's write function calls total2's markDirty.

Total2's markDirty runs. MarkDirty flips total2's dirty flag to true. MarkDirty looks at total2's own private list. Total2's list holds the effect, the one that read total2 earlier. MarkDirty calls the effect.

The effect runs. The effect's body reads total2, because reading total2 was the effect's whole job. Total2's read function sees that total2's dirty flag is up. Total2's read function runs total2's function. Total2's function reads price2, gets the new value, multiplies, returns. Total2 stores the new answer, clears the flag.

The effect's body, having gotten the new value of total2, does its work. In this example, the work is printing.

```js
price2.write(9);
// effect body ran, total2 is: 900
```

One change at the top, price2 written. One cascade down two wires. The first wire carried the signal from price2 to total2's markDirty, and that signal flipped a flag. The second wire carried the signal from total2's markDirty to the effect, and that signal ran the effect, which pulled the recompute out of total2 by reading it. Every link in the chain is the same kind of link: a list, holding an entry, and a write function or a markDirty function calling that entry. The mechanism does not change between the first wire and the second. It is the same rule, applied twice, chained.

## The whole thing, restated as the small structure it is

We have built a chain of three named things connected by two wires. Restate it now, whole, because the structure has to survive in working memory as a unit and not just as a sequence of sentences.

The three things are: a signal, called price. A derived value, called total. An effect, reading total. Price is upstream of total. Total is upstream of the effect. The effect is the downstream end.

The two wires are: a wire from price to total, which physically exists as an entry called markDirty sitting in price's private list. And a wire from total to the effect, which physically exists as an entry called run sitting in total's private list.

The rule that built both wires is the same rule: when a thing is read, the reader is recorded in the thing's list. Price was read by total, so price's list holds total's markDirty. Total was read by the effect, so total's list holds the effect. One rule, two applications, two wires.

The rule that carries signals along both wires is the same rule: when a thing changes, every entry in its list is called. Price changed, so price called markDirty. MarkDirty, acting on total's behalf, changed total's state and called the entries in total's list, which was the effect. One rule, two applications, one cascade.

That is the whole subscription mechanism. It is two lists, holding entries, built by reading, triggered by writing. There is no third kind of thing in it. The complexity people feel when they learn this is not the complexity of the mechanism. It is the difficulty of holding several named things and their connections in working memory at the same time. The mechanism itself is small. The holding is hard. That is why this part went slowly, and why the slowness was not optional.
