## 1.2 The One Thing Nobody Tells You About A Framework: You Ship It To A Stranger

Here is a question I want you to actually sit with for a few seconds, because it sounds obvious and it is not. When you build an app with a framework, where does the framework run?

Think about it concretely. You have React on your machine, or Vue, or whatever it is. You write your components, you build your app. Now a stranger on the other side of the world, on a three year old phone, on a patchy train connection, opens your website. What actually travels across the network and lands on that stranger's phone?

The answer, for a traditional framework, is: your code, and the framework itself. Both. The framework is not something that stays behind on your computer like a workshop tool. It gets packaged up and shipped, in full, to every single visitor, and it runs inside their browser, on their battery, using their memory, every time they load your page. React has to be there in the browser to do React things. Vue has to be there in the browser to do Vue things. They are passengers that ride along in every visitor's pocket.

Now, for years, that was simply considered the price of admission. And to be fair, it buys you something real. It buys you the ability to write declarative code, code where you describe what the screen should look like and let the framework worry about making the real page match. That is a genuinely wonderful thing, and it is why we all stopped hand writing the painful old style of web code. So I am not here to mock the runtime framework. I am here to make you feel its cost clearly, because Svelte is going to hand you that same declarative wonderful feeling and quietly refuse to charge you the same price.

So what is the cost, exactly? It comes in two parts. Part one is the size: the framework's own code has to be downloaded and parsed by the browser before your app can even start, and that is weight on every visit. Part two is subtler and more interesting, and it is the part I want to spend real time on. Part two is the work the framework has to do, at runtime, in the browser, just to figure out what changed on the screen. Because here is the thing. A framework that runs in the browser does not magically know what changed when your data updates. It has to find out. And finding out costs.

Let me show you exactly what I mean, by walking through how the two frameworks you already have some faded memory of, React and Vue, each go about that finding out. Because Svelte is going to do something genuinely different, and you will only feel how different once these two are fresh in your mind again.

## 1.3 A Fair, Honest Refresher On How React Works

Let us start with React, because for many of you it was the first framework you ever touched, even if the details have gone soft with the years. And because we never, ever drop a mention of another framework without explaining it, I am going to rebuild your picture of React from the ground, slowly.

React is built on an idea called the virtual D O M. Let me unpack that phrase, because it is the whole story. The D O M, spelled d o m, stands for the document object model. It is the browser's living, breathing representation of your page: every heading, every button, every piece of text, held in memory as a tree of objects that the browser draws to the screen. When people say they are updating the page, what they really mean is they are reaching into that D O M tree and changing it.

Now, reaching into the real D O M and changing it is relatively expensive, as computer operations go. So React made a clever bet. React said: I will keep a lightweight copy of that tree, a pretend version, in plain JavaScript memory. That pretend version is the virtual D O M. It is cheap to build and cheap to throw away because it never touches the screen. It is just objects.

So here is how React runs. Whenever your data changes, React re runs your component, your whole component, from the top, and builds a brand new virtual tree describing what the screen should look like now. Then it takes that new pretend tree and compares it, node by node, against the previous pretend tree it kept from last time. It walks both trees and asks, at every single spot, did this change? Did this change? What about this? That comparison process has a name. It is called diffing, as in finding the differences. And only after the diffing is done, only once React has worked out the short list of things that genuinely differ, does it finally reach into the real, expensive D O M and patch just those spots.

Re run everything. Build a fresh pretend tree. Diff it against the old one. Then patch the real page. That is the React heartbeat.

Now I want you to feel where the waste is, because Rich Harris, the creator of Svelte, wrote a now famous blog post about exactly this, and the title tells you his whole opinion in four words. The post is called Virtual D O M is pure overhead. His argument is sharp and it is worth holding onto. He points out that diffing is not free. You cannot apply a change to the real page without first doing all that comparison work to discover what to change. And most of the time, almost all of the time, the structure of your page did not change at all. Some little piece of text went from one number to another. That is it. But React does not know that in advance. So it dutifully rebuilds the pretend tree, walks through it comparing every attribute and every node, and most of that walking discovers nothing, changes nothing, achieves nothing. Two thirds of the work, in his example, is pure ceremony. The framework is spending the visitor's processor time figuring out what changed, when in principle the answer could have been known ahead of time.

Let me say the heart of it again, slowly, because it is the pivot of this entire lecture. React does work, in the browser, at the moment of every update, just to figure out what changed. It has to, because it is a library running live, and a live library has no memory of the future. It cannot know on Tuesday what your data will do on Wednesday. So it waits, and reacts, and pays the cost of discovery every single time.

Let me make that waste concrete, because a number you can feel beats a principle you can only nod at. Picture the simplest component imaginable, one that shows a greeting: the word Hello, then a comma, then a name. Right now it shows Hello, Alice. Then the name changes to Bob. That is the entire change. One word swapped for another. But watch everything React must actually do just to discover that small fact. It re runs the component and builds a fresh pretend node for the greeting. Then it compares that fresh node against the old one it kept. It asks, is this still the same kind of element, a paragraph? Yes. It asks, are all the attributes the same? It walks through them, same, same, same. And then, finally, at the very end, it reaches the text, and there it finds the one real difference, Alice has become Bob, and it updates that text on the real page. Three steps of work, and only the third one accomplished anything at all. The first two, rebuilding and then comparing structure that did not change, were pure ceremony. They produced nothing.

Now let us do the thing we will do again and again in this course to turn a small annoyance into an obviously bad idea. Let us scale it. One greeting is nothing, you would never notice. But picture a real screen: a table with a thousand rows, each row holding a dozen cells, the data streaming in and updating as it goes. On every update, React rebuilds that entire pretend table and walks it, cell by cell, a thousand rows deep, comparing every single cell against its previous version, just to discover that, say, twelve cells across three rows actually changed. The framework spent the visitor's processor time, on the visitor's phone, walking thousands of nodes to find a handful of real differences. Differences that, in principle, could have been known ahead of time. That is the overhead the blog post was named for. It is not catastrophic, React works genuinely hard to keep it in check, but it is real, and it is paid again and again, on every device, for the entire life of your app.

That is React. A virtual copy of the page, a diff on every change, and the framework itself riding along in every visitor's browser to run the whole machine. It works. Enormous, wonderful apps are built on it. But now you can feel the weight of it. Hold that feeling, and let us look at Vue, which felt that same weight and tried to lighten it.

## 1.4 And How Vue Works, Because It Chose A More Surgical Path

Vue, the second framework some of you have touched and half forgotten, looked at React's re run everything and diff it approach and said: that is too broad. Why re run the whole component and rebuild a whole tree when only one little value changed? Let us be more surgical.

So Vue, again let me explain it properly rather than assume, built something called a reactivity system that runs at runtime. The idea is that Vue watches your individual pieces of data very closely. When you create a value in Vue, you wrap it in one of Vue's watchful containers. The most common one is called a ref, spelled r e f, short for reference. You hand your value to ref, and from then on Vue is paying attention to that value. The catch, the thing every Vue veteran remembers with a small wince, is that once a value is wrapped in a ref you can no longer touch it as a plain value. You have to go through a property called dot value every time you read it or write it. That little dot value, sprinkled across all your code, is the tax Vue charges. We will meet this idea again, by the way, when we study Svelte's state, because Svelte pointedly removes that tax, but that is a later lecture.

Here is why Vue makes you wrap things. Because the value is wrapped and watched, Vue knows precisely which parts of your screen read from which values. So when a value changes, Vue does not need to re run your whole component and diff a whole tree. It can go straight to the exact spots on the page that used that exact value, and update only those. People call this fine grained reactivity. Fine grained, meaning it gets right down to the individual dependency rather than working at the level of the whole component.

So Vue is cleverer about the update than React is. It does less wasteful work at the moment of change. That is a real improvement, and modern Vue is fast and pleasant. But, and here is the point I need you to see, notice what has not changed. Vue is still a library that runs in the browser. All that watching machinery, the refs, the dependency tracking, the system that remembers which spot on the screen cares about which value, all of that is framework code, and all of it ships to the visitor and runs live in their browser. Vue found a smarter way to do the work, but it is still doing the work at runtime, and it is still sending the whole apparatus along for the ride.

So let us tally what we have seen, because this tally is the launch pad for the whole idea of Svelte. React: re run and diff, the framework lives in the browser. Vue: watch and surgically update, the framework still lives in the browser. Both of them, for all their differences, share one deep assumption so basic that most developers never even think to question it. The assumption is that the framework is a thing that runs at runtime, in the user's browser, alongside your app. That the framework is a passenger.

Svelte questions exactly that assumption. And the moment it does, everything shifts.

## 1.5 The Third Answer: What If The Framework Did Its Work Before The Browser Ever Woke Up

Here is the idea. The whole course, really, in a single sentence. Svelte is not a library that runs in the browser. Svelte is a compiler that runs on your machine, before your app is ever shipped.

Let me say that again, slowly, because it is the hinge that everything else swings on. Svelte ... is ... a ... compiler. Not a passenger that rides along in every visitor's browser. A compiler that does its work ahead of time, on your computer, at the moment you build your app, and then steps out of the way.

Now what does that actually buy us? Cast your mind back to the deep problem we found with React. React has to figure out what changed, at runtime, because as a live library it cannot know the future. It cannot know in advance how your data will move. But a compiler is in a completely different position. A compiler reads all of your code, every component, every value, every place a value is used, before a single user ever shows up. And by reading your code ahead of time, the compiler can see, with its own eyes, exactly how things could change. It can see that this number is shown in that one spot, and nowhere else. It can see that when this value updates, precisely that piece of text needs to update, and nothing else.

This is the line from that famous blog post, and I want you to really hear it. Svelte is a compiler that knows at build time how things could change in your app, rather than waiting to do the work at runtime. Knows at build time. Rather than waiting. That is the entire difference, compressed into one sentence. React and Vue wait until runtime and then scramble to discover what changed. Svelte works it all out in advance, while it is reading your code, and then writes out the exact, surgical instructions ahead of time.

So think about what gets shipped to that stranger's phone now. Not a general purpose framework that has to be ready for anything. Instead, lean, specific, hand tailored JavaScript that already knows that when this value changes, go directly to that spot and update it. No virtual tree. No diffing. No big watching apparatus riding along. Just the precise wiring for your specific app, and almost nothing else.

This is why, in its early days, Svelte described itself with a wonderful phrase. It called itself the magical disappearing framework. Disappearing, because by the time your app reaches the user, the framework as a big chunk of riding along code has largely vanished. It did its job back on your machine, at build time, and then it dissolved into plain efficient JavaScript. These days the team uses a more poetic tagline, cybernetically enhanced web apps, but I actually prefer the older one for teaching, because disappearing framework tells you the mechanism. The framework disappears because it was never meant to be a passenger. It was meant to be a builder that finishes its work before the doors open.

Let me anchor this with a contrast you can carry in your pocket for the rest of the course. A library is something that is there when your code runs. A compiler is something that was there before your code runs. React and Vue are libraries: present at runtime, in the browser, every time. Svelte is a compiler: present at build time, on your machine, once. Same goal, keeping a screen in sync with your data. Opposite location for the work. That single relocation, from the user's browser back to your build step, is the streetwise insight that a developer who has felt the weight of shipping a runtime framework appreciates in their bones.

## 1.6 What A Compiler Actually Does To Your Friendly Little File

Now, the word compiler can sound intimidating and abstract, so let us make it concrete and even a little bit mundane, because the reality is friendlier than the word.

Think about what an architect does versus what a construction crew does. The architect works at a high level. They draw clean, readable, declarative plans: here is a window, here is a door, here is how the rooms relate. The architect does not personally pour concrete. The plans get handed to a builder, and the builder turns that clean high level design into the actual physical, low level, nailed together structure. You, writing Svelte, are the architect. You write clean, readable, declarative components. The Svelte compiler is the builder. It takes your friendly design and turns it into the actual low level JavaScript that does the real work in the browser. You stay up at the level of intent. The compiler descends to the level of mechanism. And crucially, the architect never has to think about the nails.

Now let me make this real with the small example in your booklet, the very first code block in the overview page. Look at it while I describe it. It is a tiny component, and it has three parts that we will study properly in lecture two, so do not worry about mastering them now. At the top there is a script area, and inside it a small function. The function is called greet, g r e e t, and all it does is pop up a little browser alert box that says welcome to Svelte. Below the script there is a single line of markup: a button, and the button has an onclick attribute wired to that greet function, so that clicking the button runs greet and the alert appears. And at the bottom there is a style area that simply makes the button's text larger, setting its font size to two e m, which just means twice the normal text size.

That is the whole thing. A function, a button, a style. It reads like plain, honest HTML and JavaScript that a developer from any background could glance at and understand. There is almost nothing to learn to read it. That readability is the architect's plan: clean and high level.

But that friendly little file is not what runs in the browser. And this is the part I really want to land. When you build your project, the Svelte compiler reads that file and rewrites it into something else: lean, tightly optimized JavaScript that creates the button, attaches the click handler, and wires up exactly the behavior you described, with no framework apparatus bolted on top. You wrote the comfortable version. The compiler emitted the efficient version. You never see the efficient version, and you never have to, the same way the architect never has to see the individual nails.

And here is my favorite part, because it turns this from a claim you have to take on faith into something you can see with your own eyes. The Svelte team runs a free online playground on their website, and in that playground there is a tab, labeled J S Output, that shows you the actual JavaScript the compiler produced from whatever component you typed in. I want you to promise me that at least once, early in this course, you will paste a little component in there and click that J S Output tab and just look at what comes out. Because there is nothing, truly nothing, that makes the compiler mindset feel real rather than magical like seeing your friendly five line component sitting next to the precise machine the compiler built from it. The mystery evaporates and understanding takes its place.

One more thing to plant now, gently, just a seed for later. Under the hood, when your data needs to be reactive, the modern Svelte compiler builds something the wider programming world calls a signal: a small, smart container that holds a value and quietly keeps track of everyone who depends on it, so that when the value changes, exactly the right things update. If that word signal means nothing to you yet, that is completely fine. We will build it up properly when we reach the reactivity lectures. The reason I name it now is only so you know that the lean JavaScript the compiler emits is not crude. It is doing something quite sophisticated and modern. It is just doing it ahead of time, and for your specific app, rather than as a general purpose machine riding along in the browser.

## 1.7 The Real Payoff: You Get To Just Use The Language

So let us ask the practical question. If the compiler is doing all this clever work behind the scenes, what does that feel like for you, the person actually typing? What is the day to day reward?

The reward is profound and it sounds almost too simple. Because the compiler is reading and rewriting your code, you get to write code that looks like plain JavaScript, and let the compiler add the reactivity for you. Rich Harris put it beautifully when he introduced this approach. He said, in effect, we can just use the language. Not an API bolted on top of the language. The language itself.

Let me make that concrete by previewing, very lightly, something we will study in depth later. In React, if you have a counter and you want to add one to it, you cannot simply add one to it. You have to call a special setter function that React gives you, every single time, and you must never just change the value directly, or React will not notice and your screen will freeze. That is an API standing between you and your own data. In Vue, as we said, you have to write dot value every time you touch the value, because the value is wrapped. That is also an API standing between you and your data, a smaller one, but it is there. In Svelte, because the compiler is rewriting your code, you get to write the most ordinary thing imaginable. You take your counter and you add one to it, exactly the way you would in plain JavaScript with no framework at all, and the screen simply updates. The compiler saw your plain assignment, understood that this value is reactive, and quietly wrote the notify everyone machinery for you, invisibly, at build time.

So the payoff of the compiler mindset is not just smaller, faster apps for your users, although it is that too. The payoff is also less code and less ceremony for you. There is a phrase I love that captures Svelte's whole spirit here: the best API is no API at all. The fewer special framework incantations stand between your intention and the screen, the more you are simply writing in the language you already know, and the more the friction between what you mean and what you type melts away. You think, add one to the count. You write, add one to the count. The compiler handles the rest.

I am keeping this section short and I am deliberately not teaching you the runes yet, because that is the whole next part of the course and it deserves room to breathe. I am raising it now only so that the compiler idea does not stay abstract. The reason state in Svelte can be just a plain variable, the reason you are spared the setter functions and the dot value tax, all of it traces straight back to the one idea of this lecture: a compiler read your code ahead of time and did the tedious wiring so you would not have to.

## 1.8 A Short History, And Why This Course Keeps Saying Svelte Five

I have been saying Svelte five, with the number attached, on purpose all hour, and you may quietly be wondering why a version number earns a place in the very name of the course. It is worth ninety seconds, because the history explains the present, and knowing it will save you real confusion the first time you stumble onto an older tutorial and find code that does not look like anything I am teaching you.

Svelte has lived a few distinct lives. The version that first made the wider world look up was Svelte three, released in twenty nineteen. That was the release that planted the compiler flag and the reactivity idea we have spent this whole hour on. Its rallying cry came from that talk and blog post by Rich Harris called Rethinking Reactivity, and it was exactly the line we quoted earlier: we can just use the language. In Svelte three, and in Svelte four after it, the way you made a value reactive was simply to declare an ordinary variable, nothing special on it at all, and the compiler would instrument your assignments behind the scenes so that changing that variable updated the screen. There was also one slightly cryptic piece of syntax, a dollar sign followed immediately by a colon, that you would place in front of a line to mark it as reactive, meaning, re run this line whenever its ingredients change. It was clever, it was wonderfully concise, and for simple cases it genuinely felt like magic.

But that older approach had cracks that only showed at scale, and the team watched them show. The magic was a touch too implicit. It was not always obvious, just from looking at a piece of code, what was reactive and what was inert, and the rules had quiet exceptions that tripped people. The reactivity worked beautifully inside a component file, but it did not extend cleanly to ordinary script files outside a component. And underneath, it was not as fine grained as the very best modern reactivity systems had since become.

So for the version this entire course teaches, Svelte five, released near the end of twenty twenty four, the team did something bold. They kept the compiler soul completely intact, the whole idea of doing the work ahead of time, on your machine, and shipping almost nothing. But they rebuilt the reactivity engine underneath on a modern foundation called signals, which are exactly the smart little reactive containers I planted as a seed a few minutes ago. And they made reactivity explicit and visible, through a new feature called runes, those keywords that begin with a dollar sign and that form the entire second act of this course. So instead of the compiler quietly guessing which of your plain variables are meant to be reactive, you now mark them yourself, clearly and deliberately, with a rune. The result is genuinely the best of both worlds. You still, for the most part, just use the language. You still write a plain assignment to update your state. But now it is explicit and readable what is reactive and what is not, the reactivity underneath is finer grained and faster, and, remarkably, it now works outside of components too, in ordinary script files, which the old style could never do well.

Why should you care about any of this today, beyond it being a nice story? Two reasons, one for orientation and one that is purely practical. The orientation reason: when this course says runes, when it says signals, when it says explicit reactivity, you now know that those are the signature of Svelte five specifically, the modern era, and not the older style. And the practical reason, which will save you an afternoon of confusion someday: a very large amount of the Svelte material still floating around the internet, the tutorials, the blog posts, the answers on forums, was written for Svelte three and four, in that older implicit style, with the plain reactive variables and the dollar colon lines. When you meet that older code in the wild, and you certainly will, I do not want you to feel lost, or to think you are looking at something broken or wrong. You are simply looking at an earlier dialect of the same language. We even have a whole lecture near the end of this course devoted to reading and migrating that older code, so by the time we finish you will be completely fluent in both dialects. For right now, just hold the timeline in your head. Svelte three made the compiler bet and moved reactivity into the language. Svelte five, our subject, rebuilt that reactivity on signals and made it explicit with runes. Same soul. Sharper body.

## 1.9 The Honest Tradeoffs: What The Compiler Mindset Asks Of You In Return

Now, I would be a poor teacher if I sold you a miracle with no fine print. Every real engineering choice is a trade, and conceptual clarity means seeing the cost as plainly as the benefit. So let us be honest about what the compiler approach asks of you in return. There are a few things, and none of them are dealbreakers, but you should hold them consciously.

The first thing the compiler mindset asks is that you accept a build step as a permanent, non negotiable part of your life. Because Svelte is a compiler, your dot svelte files are not something a browser can run directly. A browser has no idea what a dot svelte file is. The file must be compiled first, on your machine, into the plain JavaScript the browser understands. This means you cannot, the way you technically could in the very simplest old school React setup, just drop a single script tag into a plain HTML page and start writing Svelte right there in the browser. There is always a build tool in the loop, turning your source into shippable output. Now, in practice this is a complete non issue, because the tools we are about to set up handle it for you seamlessly and you will rarely think about it. But conceptually it is a real difference, and it is the direct consequence of the very thing that makes Svelte good. The compiler has to run sometime. That sometime is your build step.

The second thing, closely related, is that the code that actually runs in the browser is not the code you wrote. It is the compiler's output. Most of the time this is pure upside and you never notice. But it means that when you go to debug, there is a translation layer between your source and what is executing. Happily, the tooling is very good about this. Modern build tools generate what are called source maps, which are little behind the scenes guides that let your browser's debugger show you your original friendly code even though it is really running the compiled version. So in day to day work you debug your own code, not the machine output. But you should know the translation is there, because once in a long while it pokes through, and it will not frighten you if you were expecting it.

And the third thing is more of a mindset adjustment than a cost. You have to be willing to trust the compiler. For a developer coming from the runtime world, there can be a small initial discomfort in not being able to see, in your source, all the machinery that makes reactivity work. It feels almost too quiet. Where is the wiring? The wiring is real, it is just being written for you, at build time, where you do not have to look at it. Some developers feel a flicker of that is too magical when they first meet this. My answer, and I think it is the honest one, is that the magic is not magic at all once you have looked at the J S Output tab even once. It is just a program transforming your program, which is the most ordinary and well understood thing in all of computing. Trusting the compiler is not faith. It is informed delegation. You can always go and read exactly what it did.

So there is the fine print, laid out plainly. A mandatory build step. Compiled output rather than your literal source running in the browser. And a one time adjustment to trusting work you do not see by default. Weigh those against what you get, dramatically less code shipped to your users, no virtual D O M diffing tax at runtime, and the freedom to mostly just write the language, and I think you will find, as the whole Svelte community has found, that it is one of the better trades on offer in front end development. But now you hold both sides of it honestly, which is exactly where a thoughtful developer should stand.

## 1.10 Now Let Us Actually Build One: The SvelteKit Path

Enough philosophy for a moment. Let us get our hands dirty and put a real, running Svelte app on your disk, because the second job of today is making sure you finish the hour with something alive in front of you. Turn in your booklet to the getting started page, where you will find a small block of terminal commands. I will walk you through it line by line, and I will tell you not just what to type but what each command is for, because typing commands you do not understand is how people stay beginners forever.

The Svelte team's clear recommendation, and the path we will take, is to use SvelteKit. So let me explain what SvelteKit is, because we never name a thing without explaining it. Svelte, the part we have been talking about all hour, is the compiler and the component language: it is how you build the pieces of your interface. SvelteKit is the official application framework built around Svelte: it handles all the larger concerns of a real app, things like routing, which means deciding which page shows up for which web address, and server side rendering, and building your project for production. If Svelte is the engine, SvelteKit is the whole car built around the engine. And SvelteKit is powered underneath by a superb build tool called Vite, spelled v i t e, which is the thing that actually runs the compiler and serves your app while you work. You do not need to master Vite today. Just know its name, because you will see it mentioned, and now it will not be a stranger.

Now to the commands themselves. Look at the first line in the booklet's command block. Spoken aloud, you type n p x, then a space, then s v, then a space, then the word create, then a space, then a name for your app, and the booklet uses the name myapp as an example. Let me take that apart. The n p x at the front is a small tool that comes with Node, and its job is to run a command line program once without permanently installing it on your system. The s v part is the name of Svelte's own official command line tool, the letters s and v, short for Svelte. And create is the instruction you are giving that tool: create me a new project. And myapp is simply the name of the folder it will make. So the whole first line, read as a human sentence, means: hey, fetch and run Svelte's command line tool, and have it scaffold a brand new project in a folder called myapp. When you run it, it will ask you a few friendly questions about how you want your project set up, and you can just take the sensible defaults for now.

The second line in the booklet is short. You type c d, then a space, then myapp. The c d means change directory, and it simply walks your terminal into the new folder that was just created, so that the next commands run in the right place. You made a room. Now you step into it.

The third line: you type n p m, then a space, then the word install. N p m is the package manager that comes with Node, the tool that downloads and manages the outside code your project depends on. Saying install tells it to read the list of dependencies your new project needs and go fetch all of them from the internet, tucking them into your project. The first time, this takes a moment and creates a folder full of downloaded packages. You run it once at the start, and only again later if your dependencies change.

And the fourth line is the exciting one, the one that brings the whole thing to life. You type n p m, then a space, then run, then a space, then dev, d e v, short for development. This starts what is called the development server. In plain terms, it fires up Vite, which compiles your Svelte code and serves your app at a local web address on your own machine, usually something your terminal will print out for you to click. Open that address in your browser and there it is: your app, running. And here is the delightful part that will define your daily rhythm from now on. While that development server is running, every time you change a file and save it, the server recompiles just what is needed and updates your browser almost instantly, often without even losing your place on the page. That near instant feedback loop, edit, save, see it, is what makes building with these modern tools feel so alive, and you get it out of the box.

So let me gather those four commands back into one clean story, because repetition is how this sticks. Create the project with Svelte's command line tool. Step into the new folder. Install the dependencies once. Then start the development server and watch your app come up in the browser, recompiling itself every time you save. Four lines, and you have gone from an empty disk to a living application. That is the recommended path, and it is the one I would have almost everyone start with.

## 1.11 The Lighter Paths: The Vite Plugin, The Playground, And A Throwaway Sandbox

Now, SvelteKit is the recommended road, but it is not the only one, and a good craftsman knows the alternatives and when to reach for them. Your booklet lists a few, under the heading alternatives to SvelteKit, so let me walk you through when each one makes sense.

The first alternative is to use Svelte directly with Vite, without the full SvelteKit framework around it. There is an official piece called the vite plugin svelte, which teaches the Vite build tool how to handle Svelte files. The booklet shows you can get this by running n p m, space, create, space, vite, and the word latest after an at sign, and then choosing the Svelte option from the menu it presents. What you get this way is leaner: just Svelte plus Vite, without SvelteKit's routing and server features. When you are ready to ship, you run n p m, space, run, space, build, and Vite produces a folder, named d i s t, short for distribution, containing the finished HTML, JavaScript, and C S S files, ready to put on any web host. The trade is right there in the leanness. Because you skipped SvelteKit, you do not get its built in routing, so for anything beyond a single screen you will need to add a separate routing library yourself to handle moving between pages. So reach for this path when you want something smaller and more hands on than full SvelteKit, perhaps a single self contained widget or a simple single page app, and you do not want the larger framework's structure. For most full applications, though, you will be happier letting SvelteKit handle those concerns for you.

The second alternative is not really for building a shippable app at all, but it is the one you will probably use the most while learning, so do not underestimate it. It is the online playground on the Svelte website. It is a little Svelte environment that runs entirely in your browser, with nothing to install. You type a component on one side and see it run on the other, and, as I mentioned earlier and will keep mentioning because it matters so much, you can flip to that J S Output tab and see exactly what the compiler produced. For trying an idea, for testing a snippet, for following along with this course, for that priceless habit of peeking at the compiler's output, the playground is your fast, frictionless friend. No project, no install, no commands. Just an idea and a browser.

And the third option the booklet names is for when you want something a little more full featured than the playground but still instant and disposable: a service called StackBlitz, which can spin up a complete SvelteKit project in the cloud, in your browser, in seconds. The booklet even gives you the charming shortcut web address for it. Reach for this when you want to share a working example with someone, or prototype something that needs a real project structure, without setting anything up on your own machine.

So now you have the full map of ways in. Full SvelteKit for real applications, the path I would start almost everyone on. The leaner Vite plus the Svelte plugin when you want something smaller and more hands on. The online playground for learning, for quick experiments, and for studying the compiler's output. And the cloud sandbox for instant, shareable, throwaway projects. Different doors, same house. Pick the door that fits the moment.

## 1.12 Your Toolbelt: The Editor Extension, The Checker, And Where To Find Help

Before we recap, let me hand you three small tools that will make your whole experience smoother, all of them mentioned at the foot of the getting started page in your booklet. These are not glamorous, but the difference between a frustrating first week and a delightful one often comes down to exactly these.

The first, and the one I would not work without, is the editor extension. The Svelte team maintains an official extension for the popular editor V S Code, and there are community supported integrations for many other editors as well. Install it on your very first day. What does it give you? It teaches your editor to truly understand dot svelte files: it colors your code correctly, it autocompletes as you type, it shows you helpful information when you hover over things, and, most valuable of all, it underlines mistakes right there in your editor, in real time, before you ever run anything. Coming from the runtime world, this is part of how Svelte gives back some of what a compiler could otherwise hide from you. Because the compiler understands your whole component, the extension can warn you about problems early and specifically. So your editor stops being a fancy text box and becomes a knowledgeable pair of eyes reading over your shoulder.

The second tool is a command you can run yourself whenever you like. In your terminal you can type n p x, space, s v, space, check. That runs Svelte's own checker across your entire project and reports back any errors or warnings it finds: type mismatches, accessibility problems, little mistakes you might have missed. It is the same intelligence the editor extension uses, but pointed at your whole project at once from the command line, which makes it perfect for a final sweep before you share or ship your code, and perfect to wire into automated checks later. Remember it by its shape: s v, then check. Svelte, check my work.

And the third thing is not a tool but a direction to look when you are stuck, because everyone gets stuck, and knowing where to turn is itself a skill. The Svelte community runs a famously welcoming chat on Discord where you can ask questions, and there is a deep well of answers on the question and answer site Stack Overflow under the Svelte tag. The booklet has the links. I mention this deliberately because beginners often suffer in silence, burning hours on something a five minute question would have solved. Do not do that. The Svelte community has a genuine reputation for kindness to newcomers. Use it. Asking for help early is what experienced developers do, not what beginners do.

So there is your toolbelt for the road ahead. The editor extension, so your editor understands and watches your code. The check command, so you can sweep the whole project for problems on demand. And a warm community, so you are never truly stuck alone. Small things, but they compound, every single day.

## 1.13 The Full Recap, And The Road Just Ahead

We have covered a great deal of ground for a first hour, so let us gather it all up and tie it off, because hearing the whole shape again, in order, is what turns a pile of new facts into one solid idea you actually own.

We began with a question so basic that almost nobody asks it: where does a framework run? And we found the uncomfortable answer for the traditional frameworks. A runtime framework is a passenger. It is packaged up and shipped, in full, to every visitor's browser, where it runs live, on their device, costing them download size and processor time on every visit.

We then gave React and Vue an honest, careful refresher, because you had let the details go soft and because we never name a framework without explaining it. React, we remembered, uses a virtual D O M: on every change it re runs your component, builds a fresh pretend copy of the page, diffs that copy against the previous one to discover what changed, and only then patches the real page. And we felt the waste Rich Harris named in his blog post titled Virtual D O M is pure overhead: diffing is not free, and most of that discovery work finds that almost nothing changed. Vue, we remembered, is more surgical: it wraps your values in watchful containers like ref, accessed through dot value, so it can skip the whole tree diff and update just the exact spots that used a changed value. Cleverer about the update, but still, and this was the key, still a library doing its work at runtime, in the browser, with all its machinery shipped along.

And that shared assumption, that a framework is a runtime passenger, is the very thing Svelte refuses. The one idea of this whole lecture, the hinge for the whole course: Svelte is not a library that runs in the browser, it is a compiler that runs on your machine, before your app ships. Because it reads your code ahead of time, it can see exactly how things could change, and write out lean, surgical JavaScript that already knows what to update, with no virtual D O M, no runtime diffing, and almost no framework riding along. It knows at build time, rather than waiting to discover at runtime. That is why it once called itself the magical disappearing framework: by the time your app reaches the user, the framework has largely dissolved into plain efficient code. A library is there when your code runs. A compiler was there before your code runs. That relocation of the work, from the user's browser back to your build step, is the whole game.

We made the compiler concrete with the architect and the builder: you write the clean, high level, declarative plan, and the compiler does the low level construction, so you never have to think about the nails. We looked at the friendly little button component and saw that the readable thing you write is not the thing that runs, the compiler rewrites it into lean optimized JavaScript, and we promised ourselves at least one visit to the J S Output tab in the playground to see that transformation with our own eyes and dissolve the mystery. And we planted one seed for later, the word signal, the smart little reactive container the compiler builds for you under the hood, to be properly understood when we reach reactivity.

We named the real daily payoff: because the compiler does the wiring, you get to just use the language, writing plain assignments instead of calling setter functions like React or threading everything through dot value like Vue. The best API, as the saying goes, is no API at all. And we were honest about the price: a build step is mandatory because a browser cannot run a dot svelte file directly, the code that runs is the compiler's output rather than your literal source, smoothed over by source maps when you debug, and there is a one time adjustment to trusting work the compiler does for you out of sight, an adjustment that the J S Output tab turns from faith into informed delegation.

Then we got practical and put a real app on disk. The recommended SvelteKit path, four commands: create the project with Svelte's command line tool, step into the folder, install the dependencies once, and start the development server, after which every save recompiles and refreshes your browser almost instantly. We toured the lighter alternatives: Svelte with the Vite plugin for leaner hands on builds that produce a d i s t folder, with a routing library added yourself if you need one. The online playground for learning, quick experiments, and studying compiler output. And the cloud sandbox for instant shareable projects. And we picked up a toolbelt: the official editor extension so your editor understands and watches your code, the s v check command to sweep your whole project for problems, and a famously kind community for when you are stuck.

Through every part of this hour, one thread ran straight down the middle and I want it to be the last thing you hold as we close. Svelte moves the work. The work of keeping a screen in sync with your data, which React and Vue do live, in the user's browser, at runtime, Svelte does ahead of time, on your machine, at build time, and then it gets out of the way. Everything else in this course, the plain variable that is really reactive state, the markup that compiles to surgical updates, the small size, the quiet code, all of it flows from that one relocation. Hold the relocation, and you hold Svelte.

You now understand the why and the how. Next we turn to the what.
