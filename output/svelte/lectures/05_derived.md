## 5.2 The Real Problem: A Value That Lives Off Other Values

Picture a shopping cart. Inside it, a list of items, each with a price. That list is your state, your source of truth, the thing the user adds to and removes from. And on the screen, near the bottom, there is a total. The sum of all the prices. Now, the total is not a separate, independent fact you store and maintain. The total is entirely a function of the list. It is the list, added up. Change the list, and the total must change to match. The total lives off the list. It has no life of its own.

So how do you keep that total correct as the list changes? Let us try the obvious ways, with nothing clever, and watch each one fail, because feeling the failure is what makes the solution land.

Here is the first naive attempt. You compute the total once, when the component is born. You loop over the list, add up the prices, store the result in a variable called total, and show that variable on screen. Clean. And here is where it breaks, and it breaks for a reason you already know deep in your bones from the last two lectures. Cast your mind back to lecture two, where we learned that the script of a Svelte component runs once, a single time, when the component is created, and then never again. So that loop that adds up the prices runs exactly once, at birth, computes the total for the list as it was at that first instant, and then sits there frozen. The user adds a fifty dollar item to the cart. The list changes. But total was computed once and is now just a stale number, a photograph of a total that used to be true. The documentation says this almost word for word in a note I want you to find in your booklet: code in a Svelte component runs only once at creation, so without the derived rune, your computed value would keep its original value even when the thing it was computed from changes. That is the first failure. Compute once, and your value goes stale the moment its inputs move.

So you think, fine, I will not compute it once. I will recompute it every single time anything changes. Every time the user touches the cart, I will rerun the loop and rebuild the total. And this is the second naive attempt, and it fails in two different ways at once. The first way is waste. If you recompute on every change, you recompute even when nothing relevant changed. The user updates their shipping address, which has nothing to do with the prices, and you loop over the whole cart and add it all up again for no reason. On a small cart, who cares. On a big dashboard with fifty computed values, each recomputing on every unrelated change, you are burning the user's processor for nothing. And the second way it fails is worse, and it is the one that actually ships bugs. To recompute every time something changes, you, the programmer, have to remember every place that could change an input, and wire a recompute into each one. Add item, recompute. Remove item, recompute. Apply coupon, recompute. Change quantity, recompute. Miss one single spot, forget to wire the recompute into just one of those places, and your total is silently, intermittently wrong. It is right most of the time, which makes the bug even harder to find. You are now manually responsible for keeping a cached value in agreement with its source.

And that phrase, keeping a cached value in agreement with its source, is the heart of today's lecture, so let me name it properly. There is a famous joke among programmers, credited to a man named Phil Karlton, and it goes: there are only two hard things in computer science, cache invalidation, and naming things. Set the naming joke aside. Cache invalidation is our topic. A cache is a stored result of some computation, kept around so you do not have to redo the work. Your total is a cache: it is the stored result of adding up the list. And cache invalidation is the problem of knowing exactly when that stored result has gone stale and must be thrown away and recomputed. Too eager, and you recompute constantly and waste everything. Too lazy, and you show stale data. Get the timing even slightly wrong, in even one spot, and you have a cache bug: a value that is sometimes right and sometimes quietly wrong. This is genuinely one of the hardest problems in the field, and every front end framework is, in large part, a machine for solving it on your behalf.

So that is the real problem, felt in full. A computed value is a cache. Keeping it correct by hand means either staleness, or waste, or the bug-prone chore of invalidating it in every place its inputs might move. Now let us watch how the two frameworks you remember each try to take that burden off you, starting with React, because React's answer is exactly where the phrase cache bug earns its place in our title.

## 5.3 How React Answers It, And The Cache Bug In The Bargain

So you have a computed value in React, a total derived from a list. How does React keep it correct without recomputing it on every single render?

First, remember how React works, because we never lean on another framework without rebuilding it for you. From lecture one: React re runs your entire component function from the top on every change. So by default, your total would be recomputed on every render, the wasteful path we just described. To avoid that waste, React gives you a tool called use memo. The name is literally short for memoize, which is the computer science word for caching the result of a function so you can reuse it instead of recomputing it. So use memo is React's caching tool, right there in the name.

Now let me show you exactly how you call it, because the shape of it is the whole point. You write const, then a name for your computed value, then equals, then the word use memo. And use memo takes two arguments. The first argument is a function, the function that does the actual computation, the loop that adds up the prices. And the second argument, and here it comes, is a list, written in square brackets, of all the values that the computation depends on. For a total derived from a list, you would pass the function that sums the list, and then a square bracket containing the list. That square bracket is called the dependency array. And it is React's answer to cache invalidation: you, the programmer, declare, by hand, in that array, every value that should cause the cache to be thrown away and recomputed. React then keeps the last result, and on each render it checks: did anything in your dependency array change since last time? If no, it hands back the cached result, no recompute. If yes, it reruns your function and caches the new result. By the way, React keeps only the most recent result, a cache of size one, so it is genuinely just the last value.

Do you feel the catch? The dependency array is a list you write by hand and must keep perfectly in sync with the actual computation, forever. And this is precisely the cache bug from our title, made into a daily chore. If your computation reads some value but you forget to put that value in the dependency array, React will not recompute when that value changes, and your computed result goes stale. Right most of the time, quietly wrong when that forgotten input moves. The classic version: you compute filtered results from a list and a search term, you put the list in the array, but you forget the search term. Now the filter never updates when the user types. A real bug, shipped, from one missing entry in a hand maintained list.

Let me make that bug concrete, because you should be able to feel it, not just nod at it. You build a searchable list. You keep the full list in state, and the text in the search box in state too. You write a memoized filtered list: a function that filters the full list by the search text, and a dependency array beside it. It is late, you are tired, and you put the full list in the array and move on, because obviously the filter depends on the list. But it also depends on the search text, and that one slips your mind. Ship it. The page loads, the list appears, everything looks perfect. Then the user types a letter. The search text in state changes. But the filtered list is memoized against an array that never mentions the search text, so React checks its array, sees the list itself did not change, and confidently hands back the old cached filtered list. Nothing filters. The user types more. Still nothing. The search box is plainly broken, and yet every other part of the page works fine, and the filter function itself is flawless. You will stare at that filter function for twenty minutes before it occurs to you to look at the dependency array, because the bug is not in the logic, it is in the bookkeeping. That is a cache bug in the flesh: the cache, your memoized list, was not thrown away when it should have been, because you, the human, forgot to say so.

How real and common is this bug? Real enough that the React team ships a special linter rule, named exhaustive dependencies, whose entire job is to read your computation, figure out what it actually depends on, and yell at you when your dependency array does not match. Sit with that for a second. There is an automated tool whose whole reason to exist is to catch the mistakes people make keeping these arrays correct. That tool is a confession. It is React admitting that asking humans to hand maintain dependency arrays produces bugs reliably enough to need a robot watching over your shoulder. And even the linter is not perfect. It can be silenced, it can be fooled, and the moment your dependencies get dynamic it gets murky.

So React solved the waste problem, the recompute on every render problem, but the price it charged you is the dependency array: a hand written list, kept in sync by you, backstopped by a linter, and the source of a whole genre of stale value bugs. Hold that feeling sharply, the feeling of being handed a list to maintain, because Svelte is going to take that exact list away from you entirely, and the contrast is the most satisfying moment in this lecture. But first, Vue, which got much, much closer to the answer.

## 5.4 How Vue Answers It, And How Close It Comes

Vue looked at the dependency array and said, why are we making the human do this? The computer can see what the computation reads. Let the computer figure out the dependencies. And that single decision is most of the way to where Svelte lands.

Vue's tool is called computed. You write const, a name, equals, then the word computed, and you hand it a single function, the one that does the computation. And that is it. There is no second argument. There is no dependency array. You do not declare what it depends on, because Vue figures it out for you, automatically, by watching which reactive values your function actually reads while it runs. Read the prices inside the function, and Vue quietly notes, this computed value depends on the prices. The dependencies are discovered, not declared. The hand maintained list, and the whole genre of bugs that came with it, is gone. This is called automatic dependency tracking, and it is a genuinely better idea than the dependency array.

Vue's computed is also lazy and cached in a smart way. Lazy means it does not recompute the instant a dependency changes. It waits until something actually asks to read the computed value, and only then, if a dependency really did change, does it recompute. And cached means if you read it twice and nothing changed in between, it hands back the stored result the second time instead of redoing the work. So Vue gives you automatic dependencies, laziness, and caching. That is a very good answer. Honestly, on the conceptual core, Vue and Svelte agree almost completely here.

So where do they differ? In exactly the place they always differ, the place we have hammered since lecture one. Two spots. First, the tax we met last time: a Vue computed value comes back wrapped, so to read it you have to go through dot value, every single time, the same dot value tax that rides on Vue's refs. Write the computed total to the screen and you are writing total dot value, not total. And second, deeper: Vue's computed is a runtime function. You import it from the Vue library, you call it as your code runs in the browser, and Vue's machinery does the dependency tracking live, at runtime, shipping all that tracking apparatus to every visitor. Svelte, as you know by now, does this work at a different time and place. So Vue arrives at almost the same destination as Svelte, automatic dependencies and laziness and caching, but it gets there as a runtime library with a dot value tax, where Svelte gets there as a compiler with a plain variable. Same good idea. Different machine underneath, and a cleaner surface on top. Let us finally see that surface.

## 5.5 The Derived Rune: You Just Write The Expression

Open your booklet to the very first code block of this lecture, the doubled counter. It is the simplest possible derived value, and it shows you the whole idea in three lines.

Let me describe what you are looking at. In the script zone there are two lines. The first you already know cold from last lecture: let count equals state, with zero in parentheses. That is a piece of reactive state called count, starting at zero. The source of truth. The second line is the new one, and here it is in spoken form: let doubled equals derived, and inside the parentheses, count times two. So, let doubled equals derived of count times two. That second line declares a derived value called doubled, and its value is, always and forever, count times two. Below, in the markup, there is a button showing doubled, and a line of text saying count doubled is doubled. Click the button, count goes up by one, and doubled instantly becomes the new count times two, on the screen, with no further work from you.

Now look hard at what you did and did not write, because the absences are the lesson. You wrote the expression, count times two, and nothing else. You did not write a dependency array. You did not list count anywhere as a thing to watch. You did not wrap the value so you have to unwrap it later with dot value. You did not wire a recompute into the button's click handler. You wrote the formula, the relationship, count times two, once, declaratively, and you walked away. From that moment on, doubled is simply, permanently, count times two. Whenever count changes, doubled is the new count times two. It is not that doubled gets recomputed and reassigned by some code you wrote. It is that doubled is defined as that relationship, and the compiler guarantees the relationship holds.

Feel the contrast with the failures from the start of the lecture. The compute once approach gave you a stale value, because the script runs once. The derived rune does not have that problem: even though the line declaring doubled also runs only once, what that line does is establish a living relationship, not compute a frozen number. The documentation's note says it plainly, and it is worth finding in your booklet: without the derived rune, doubled would keep its original value even when count changes. The derived rune is the exact tool that fixes the once at creation staleness for computed values. You declare the formula once, and the formula stays true forever.

And the contrast with React is the headline of the whole hour. In React you wrote a function and a dependency array, a list you maintain by hand, watched by a linter, and the source of stale value bugs when you forget an entry. In Svelte you wrote count times two. That is the entire derived value. There is no array to forget an entry in, because there is no array. The cache bug from our title cannot happen here, because the thing that causes it, the hand maintained dependency list, does not exist. You did not solve cache invalidation carefully. You were never handed the problem in the first place.

One more small thing from the booklet, so you have the full shape. Just as you could put the state rune on a class field last lecture, you can put the derived rune on a class field too. If you are modeling your data with a class, and one of its properties is computed from the others, you mark that property with the derived rune, exactly as you would a plain variable. Same idea, same rune, just living on a class.

So that is the surface: you write the expression, and you are done. But a sharp programmer is now asking the obvious question, the same one React's whole dependency array exists to answer. If you never told Svelte what doubled depends on, how on earth does Svelte know to update it when count changes? Let us answer that, because the answer is the compiler mindset paying off yet again.

## 5.6 No Dependency Array, Ever: How The Compiler Finds Your Dependencies

How does Svelte know that doubled depends on count, when you never said so? The same way it knows everything: it reads your code, ahead of time, at build time, and it sees.

Go back to lecture one and hold the core idea next to this. React is a library running live in the browser, so it cannot look at your code and understand it. It can only run your function and check, after the fact, whether the values in an array you handed it have changed. That is why React needs the array: it is blind to the inside of your computation, so you have to describe the inside to it from the outside. Svelte is a compiler. It is not blind. It reads the expression count times two with its own eyes, before your app ever ships, and it can plainly see that this expression reads count. So it knows, with certainty, that doubled depends on count, because it literally read the line. There is nothing for you to declare, because the compiler already knows everything you could have told it, and knows it more reliably than you do, because it cannot forget.

Let me give you the precise rule the documentation states, because it is clean and you should carry it. Anything that is read, synchronously, inside a derived expression, is considered a dependency of that derived value. That is the whole rule. Read a value inside the expression, and it becomes a dependency, automatically. Read three values, you have three dependencies. Read a value that is itself a derived value, fine, the dependency chains through. You never list anything, because the act of reading a value inside the expression is the declaration. The reading is the wiring.

And here is the quiet victory in that, the one our title has been promising. Remember the broken search box from the React section, the filtered list that went stale because someone forgot to list the search text. In Svelte, you write that same filter as a derived value: the filtered list is derived from the full list and the search text, and you simply write the filtering expression. Because you read the search text inside that expression, the search text is, automatically, a tracked dependency. There is no array to put it in, and therefore no array to forget it from. The exact bug that haunts the React version cannot be written in the Svelte version, not because you were careful, but because the place where the mistake used to live has been removed from the language. That is what without cache bugs means, concretely.

And then the mechanism, which the documentation describes with two lovely words, dirty and recalculated, and which I want you to picture concretely. When one of those dependencies changes, count goes up, Svelte does not immediately recompute doubled. Instead it marks doubled as dirty. Dirty just means, this value is now possibly out of date, do not trust it until it is freshened. Then, the next time something actually reads doubled, say the screen needs to paint it, Svelte sees the dirty mark, recalculates count times two right then, gets the fresh value, clears the dirty mark, and hands over the correct result. So the value is always correct when you read it, and the recompute happens at the last responsible moment, only when truly needed. That dirty then recalculate on read behavior is not an incidental detail. It is the heart of how Svelte avoids the waste we worried about, and we are going to give it its own section in a few minutes, because it deserves it.

There is one careful edge worth a sentence, which your booklet flags, so that you are not surprised later. The rule says anything read synchronously inside the expression. Synchronously means, in the normal top to bottom flow, not inside some callback that runs later. There is also special handling for the await keyword inside a derived, where Svelte tracks dependencies that appear even after the await resolves, but that touches the async features we have not covered, so I am naming it and moving on. And if you ever have a value that you read inside a derived but specifically do not want treated as a dependency, there is an escape hatch, a tool called untrack, that lets you read something without it counting. You will reach for that rarely. The everyday truth is the simple one: read a value in the expression, and it is a tracked dependency, with zero ceremony.

So, to lock this in, because it is the crux of why derived has no cache bugs. React asks you to list dependencies because it cannot see inside your computation. Svelte never asks, because the compiler reads your computation and sees the dependencies itself. The array is not made easier or shorter or safer. It is gone, because the reason it existed is gone. That is the difference between describing your code to a runtime library and having a compiler simply read your code.

## 5.7 When One Line Is Not Enough: The Derived By Form

Now, count times two fits comfortably inside a pair of parentheses. But real computations are not always one tidy expression. Sometimes you need a loop. Sometimes you need a few intermediate variables, an if statement, several steps, before you arrive at the answer. You cannot cram a loop into a single inline expression. So Svelte gives you a second form of the rune for exactly these cases, and it is spelled derived dot by. The rune, then a dot, then the word by.

Turn to the second code block in your booklet, the one with the numbers and the total. Let me walk it. There is a piece of state called numbers, holding a small list, one two three. Then there is a derived value called total, and instead of derived with an expression inside, it is derived dot by, and you hand it a whole function. Inside that function there is real procedural code: it sets a running total to zero, it loops over each number in the list adding it on, and at the end it returns the total. So derived dot by takes a function, runs it to get the value, and gives you back a derived value just like before. Below, a button adds a new number to the list, and the displayed total updates itself, because, exactly as before, the loop reads the numbers list, so the numbers list is a tracked dependency, so when it changes the total goes dirty and recalculates on the next read.

Here is the simplest way to hold the relationship between the two forms, and the documentation states it outright: derived of some expression is just shorthand for derived dot by, with a function that returns that expression. They are the same tool. The plain form is the convenient shorthand for the common case where your computation is a single expression. The dot by form is what you reach for when the computation needs room to breathe, when it is a function with several lines and a return. Same dependency tracking, same laziness, same everything. The only difference is one gives you a snug one liner and the other gives you a function body. Use the short one until it stops fitting, then switch to dot by. That is the whole decision.

So now you have both shapes of the rune. For a formula, derived of the formula. For a procedure, derived dot by, of a function. Either way you describe what the value is, never how to keep it fresh, and never which inputs to watch.

## 5.8 Lazy On Purpose: Push, Pull, And The Update That Never Happens

I promised this section, and it is the one that turns you from someone who can use derived into someone who understands it. Let us talk about when, exactly, a derived value recomputes. Because the answer is cleverer than recompute when a dependency changes, and the cleverness is where the performance comes from.

Svelte uses a scheme with a name worth knowing: push pull reactivity. Two halves, push and pull, and they happen at different times. Here is the push half. When you change a piece of state, count goes up, Svelte immediately, synchronously, runs through everything that depends on that state, directly or indirectly, and notifies it: you are now dirty, you might be out of date. That notification is fast and it is eager. It happens the instant the state changes. That is the push. The change is pushed outward to everyone who cares, marking them dirty.

But, and this is the pull half, being marked dirty does not recompute anything. The actual recomputation is lazy. It is deferred. A dirty derived value does not freshen itself until someone actually pulls on it, until some piece of code, or the screen, actually reads its value. Only at the moment of reading does Svelte say, ah, you are dirty and someone wants you, let me run your formula now and get the current answer. So the work happens on read, at the last possible moment, and only if someone reads at all. If a derived value goes dirty but nothing ever reads it before it goes dirty again, its formula simply never runs for that round. The waste we feared at the start of the lecture, recomputing things nobody is looking at, Svelte structurally avoids, by never computing until pulled.

Now here is the most delightful consequence, and your booklet has the exact example, the one with large. Watch. There is state called count, starting at zero. And there is a derived value called large, defined as count greater than ten. So large is a true or false value: is the count above ten. The screen shows large. Now click the button and tick count up: zero, one, two, three. Every one of those clicks changes count. Every one of those clicks pushes a dirty mark to large, because large depends on count. But here is the beautiful part. Each time large is pulled and recomputed, the answer is the same: false, false, false, count is still not above ten. And Svelte notices that the new value of large is identical to its old value. And when a derived value recomputes to a result identical to what it had before, Svelte stops right there and does not bother updating anything downstream. The screen showing large does not repaint, because large did not actually change, it only got recomputed to the same answer. The text on screen updates only at the single moment large genuinely flips, when count crosses from ten to eleven and large goes from false to true. Nine clicks, and the screen tied to large does not move. The tenth click, and it changes once.

Let me say why that is such a big deal. It means derived is not just caching the computation, it is also caching the consequences. A change in your raw state that does not actually change a derived value produces no downstream work at all. You can have a noisy, fast changing piece of state feeding a derived value that only occasionally changes its answer, and everything downstream of that derived value stays perfectly still until the answer truly moves. You get that for free, just by expressing your logic as a derived value. No framework that recomputes eagerly and blindly can do this as cleanly. This is the quiet, deep payoff of laziness plus identity checking, and it is why, in Svelte, building things out of layered derived values is not just convenient, it is fast.

Let me make those layered derived values concrete, because they are how you will really build. A derived value is allowed to read another derived value, which reads another, a whole chain of them. A cart's subtotal derived from the items, a tax derived from the subtotal, a grand total derived from the subtotal and the tax. You declare each link as its own small, honest formula, and the chain simply works. Change an item, and the dirty mark pushes down the entire chain in one instant: subtotal dirty, tax dirty, total dirty. Then, when the screen pulls on the total, the chain freshens from the top, each link recomputing only if it is dirty, and the identity check we just met guards every single link, so if the subtotal happens to land on the same number, the tax and total below it do not stir at all. So a deep chain of derived values is not something to fear for performance. It is the opposite. It is the efficient way to build, because laziness and the identity check protect every link in the chain. Describe your interface as layers of small, pure formulas stacked on your state, and Svelte keeps the whole tower both correct and cheap, all the way down.

So the full picture of timing: change state, and the push instantly marks every dependent derived value dirty. Read a derived value, and the pull recomputes it, but only if dirty, and only then. And if the recompute lands on the same answer as before, the ripple stops there and nothing downstream stirs. Fresh when you read it, lazy about the work, and silent when nothing really changed. That is push pull reactivity, and now you know why your computed values are both always correct and rarely wasteful.

## 5.9 A Happy Surprise: Destructuring A Derived Is Safe

Now I get to give you a piece of good news, and it is going to feel especially good because it is the exact opposite of a trap I warned you about last lecture. You remember the destructuring trap from the state lecture. We said: if you destructure a piece of reactive state, if you pull a property out of a state object into its own loose variable, that loose variable is a dead copy. It was read once at the moment of destructuring and never updates again. Reactivity broken. We told you, with state, do not destructure what needs to stay live.

So your instinct, walking into derived, is to expect the same trap. And here is the surprise: with derived, destructuring is completely safe. Pull values out of a derived object into their own variables, and every one of those variables stays reactive. The connection is not severed. It holds.

Look at the destructuring example in your booklet. There is a function that returns an object with three properties, a, b, and c. And there is a line that says, let, open curly brace, a comma b comma c, close curly brace, equals derived of that function's result. In plain words, we are destructuring the three properties out of a derived object, into three standalone variables a, b, and c. And the documentation tells you what Svelte actually does under the hood, and it is the cleanest possible explanation of why this is safe. That one line is roughly equivalent to four lines: one derived value holding the whole object, and then a, b, and c each declared as their own little derived value reaching into that object for their one property. In other words, when you destructure a derived, Svelte does not hand you three dead copies. It hands you three fresh derived values, one per property, each with its own live relationship to the source. Each stays current because each is itself a derived value, with all the tracking and laziness we just learned.

Now hold the two cases side by side, because together they teach you something true about reactivity, not just a rule to memorize. Destructuring state severs the connection, because state destructuring is plain JavaScript reading a value out once. Destructuring derived preserves the connection, because Svelte rebuilds it as a set of small derived values, each one a living formula. The difference is not arbitrary. State is a value you are holding. Derived is a relationship you have declared, and a relationship can be split into smaller relationships without losing its livingness. So the rule of thumb, side by side: with state, reaching in fresh keeps it live and destructuring kills it. With derived, even destructuring keeps it live. One more reason that, when you can express something as a derived value rather than as raw state you maintain, you often should.

## 5.10 Deriveds Are Left As They Are, Not Wrapped

Here is a more subtle property of derived values, and it pays off in a specific, common situation, so let me set it up carefully.

Remember from the state lecture that when you put an object or an array into the state rune, Svelte wraps it in a deeply reactive proxy, layer upon layer, so that reaching into it and changing any nested property is tracked. State proxifies its contents, deeply. Derived does not do this. A derived value is left exactly as it is, not wrapped in a new proxy. Whatever your expression produces, that is what you get, unwrapped.

At first that might sound like a downside, but watch what it actually buys you in the common case, with the example in your booklet, the one with selected. There is a list called items, declared with the state rune, so it is a deeply reactive proxy, every item inside it reactive. There is a number called index, also state. And there is a derived value called selected, defined as items at position index. So selected is whichever item the index currently points at, pulled straight out of the reactive items list. Now, because derived did not wrap selected in some new separate proxy, selected is not a copy of the item. It is the very same reactive object that lives inside the items list. They are one and the same thing. And so, the documentation points out, you can change a property on selected, or bind an input to a property of selected, and it reaches straight through and updates the real item inside the items array, because it was never a separate copy in the first place. It is a live window onto the item in the list.

And then the documentation makes the point sharp with a contrast: if items had not been deeply reactive to begin with, mutating selected would do nothing, because there would be no reactivity underneath for the change to ripple through. So the behavior you want, editing the selected item and having it actually update the list, works precisely because of the division of labor between the two runes. State provides the deep reactivity underneath. Derived, by leaving its result alone rather than wrapping it again, lets you reach straight through to that underlying reactive thing. The two runes cooperate. State makes the data live. Derived gives you a clean, current view into it without adding a second layer of wrapping that would get in the way.

The everyday lesson, the one to actually carry: a derived value hands you back the real thing, not a copy of it. When that real thing is itself reactive state, you can work through your derived value to touch the source, which is usually exactly what you want, especially for things like selecting an item to edit. You will not think about this most days. But the day you derive a selected item and edit it and watch the list update, you will know why it worked.

## 5.11 Bending The Rule For Optimistic UI: Overriding A Derived

Everything so far has painted derived as a value you declare and never assign to directly. It is the formula, the compiler keeps it true, you read it. And ninety nine percent of the time, that is the whole story. But there is one important, real world situation where you want to break that rule on purpose, and modern Svelte lets you, so let us cover it, because it is genuinely useful and you will meet it in real apps.

The situation is called optimistic UI, and you have felt it a thousand times as a user without naming it. You tap the heart on a post to like it. The heart fills in and the count ticks up instantly, the very instant you tap. But your tap has to travel to a server far away, get recorded, and travel back, and that takes a moment. The interface did not wait for that round trip. It updated immediately, optimistically, assuming the like will succeed, because making you stare at an unresponsive heart for half a second feels broken. That is optimistic UI: show the likely result right now, reconcile with the server in the background, and quietly roll back if it turns out the server said no.

Now here is the tension with derived. Your likes count is naturally a derived value: it is derived from the post data that came from the server, the source of truth. So you would write likes equals derived of the post's like count. But optimistic UI needs to do something that sounds illegal: it needs to bump that derived likes value up by one immediately, locally, before the server has said anything, temporarily overriding the formula. Look at the optimistic example in your booklet. There is likes, declared as derived of the post's like count. And then, in the click handler, the first thing it does is likes plus equals one. It reassigns the derived value directly, by hand, shoving it up by one for instant feedback. Then it quietly calls the server. And if the server call fails, in the catch block, it does likes minus equals one, rolling the optimistic bump back. The screen showed the new count instantly, and either the server confirms it, or it silently reverts.

So Svelte lets you temporarily reassign a derived value, overriding its formula, as long as you did not declare it with const. The moment its real dependencies change again, the post data actually updates from the server, the formula reasserts itself and takes back over. Your manual override was just a temporary patch over the top, holding until the truth arrives. The booklet has a note here worth seeing: this ability is recent. Before a particular version, Svelte 5.25, derived values were strictly read only, and you could not do this at all. So if you read older material claiming you can never assign to a derived, that was true once and is true no longer. Today, overriding a derived for optimistic UI is a supported, blessed pattern.

I want to be careful you hear the proportion, though. This is the exception, not the rule. The overwhelming default for a derived value is: declare the formula, never assign to it, let the compiler keep it true. Overriding is a specialized tool for the specific case of showing instant feedback ahead of a slow source of truth. Reach for it there, and nowhere else. Most derived values you ever write will be pure formulas you never touch by hand.

## 5.12 The One Firm Rule: Keep The Expression Pure

We are near the end, and there is exactly one firm rule about derived that I have been saving, because it is the rule that draws the border between today's rune and next lecture's rune. Here it is: the expression inside a derived must be pure. It must be free of side effects. It is allowed to read your state and compute an answer, and that is all it is allowed to do.

Let me make pure and side effect concrete, because they are vague words until you ground them. A pure computation takes some inputs and produces an output, and changes nothing else in the world while it does so. It adds up the prices and gives you the total. It does not, along the way, also change another piece of state, or fire off a network request, or write to the page, or start a timer. Those other things, changing state, talking to the network, touching the world outside, are called side effects, effects on the side, off to the side of the main job of computing a value. And the rule is: a derived expression may not have them. In fact, Svelte actively enforces this. The documentation says it directly, and your booklet notes it: Svelte will disallow state changes inside a derived expression. If you try, inside your derived, to also bump some other counter, Svelte stops you. It is not a gentle suggestion. It is a guardrail.

Why so strict? Two reasons, and both come straight from what we learned today. The first is laziness. Remember, a derived value only recomputes when it is pulled, when something reads it, and it might be pulled zero times, or many times, at moments you do not control. If computing it also changed state somewhere, then whether that state change happened, and how many times, would depend on the unpredictable timing of reads. Your side effect would fire erratically, sometimes, unpredictably, which is chaos. A computation you can run lazily, skip, and repeat safely must not change anything, or laziness becomes a bug generator. The second reason is conceptual honesty. A derived value answers the question, what is this value, in terms of other values. That is a question about a relationship. Mixing in actions, do this, send that, muddies a clean relationship into a tangled procedure, and you lose all the guarantees, the freshness, the laziness, the safety, that came from it being a pure relationship.

So if a derived may not have side effects, but you genuinely need a side effect, something must change in the world when your state changes, what do you do? You do not force it into a derived. You reach for the rune built precisely for that, the effect rune, which is the entire subject of our next lecture. That is the clean division of labor in this trio. State holds your source of truth. Derived computes pure values out of it, no side effects allowed. Effect is where side effects are allowed to live, on purpose, in the open. Keeping them separate is not bureaucracy. It is what makes each one trustworthy. So when you feel the urge to do something inside a derived other than compute and return a value, that urge is information: it is telling you the thing you want is not a derived value at all, it is an effect, and it belongs next door.

## 5.13 The Full Recap, And The Road To Effects

We have covered a lot, so let us gather it all into one shape, because the recap is where a pile of features becomes a single idea you own.

We started with the real problem: a computed value, a total from a list, a full name from two names, is a value that lives off other values, and keeping it correct is genuinely hard. We watched the naive ways fail. Compute it once, and it goes stale, because a component's code runs only once at creation. Recompute it by hand on every change, and you either waste effort recomputing things nobody looks at, or you forget to wire the recompute into one of the many places its inputs can move, and ship a value that is quietly, intermittently wrong. We named that whole difficulty by its real name, cache invalidation, the problem of knowing exactly when a stored result has gone stale, one of the two famously hard things in computing, and we saw that a computed value is a cache, and a stale one is a cache bug.

We looked at how React answers it, with use memo and a dependency array: a hand written list of what the computation depends on, which you must keep in sync forever, which is so error prone that React ships a dedicated linter just to catch your mistakes, and which is the very cache bug our title warns about, because forget one entry and your value goes stale. We looked at how Vue answers it, with computed, which is much closer to right: automatic dependency tracking, no array, lazy and cached, but still a runtime library with the dot value tax on every read.

Then we met Svelte's answer, the derived rune. You write the expression, count times two, and nothing else. No array, no dot value, no manual recompute. You declare the formula once, and the compiler guarantees it stays true forever, fixing the once at creation staleness, because what you declared was a living relationship, not a frozen number. And we answered the burning question, how does it know the dependencies without you listing them: because Svelte is a compiler, it reads your expression with its own eyes at build time and sees what it reads, so anything read synchronously inside is automatically a tracked dependency. The dependency array is not improved, it is gone, and the cache bug with it, because the thing that caused the bug never exists.

We learned the second form, derived dot by, for when your computation needs a whole function instead of a one line expression, and saw that the plain form is just shorthand for it. We went deep on push pull reactivity: changing state instantly pushes a dirty mark to everything that depends on it, but the actual recompute is lazy and happens only when something pulls, when the value is read, and if the fresh answer is identical to the old one, everything downstream stays perfectly still. A noisy input feeding a rarely changing derived produces no downstream work until the answer truly moves. Always correct on read, lazy about the work, silent when nothing really changed.

We got the happy surprise that destructuring a derived is safe, the exact opposite of the state destructuring trap, because Svelte rebuilds each destructured piece as its own small derived value, each with its own living relationship. We saw that derived values are left as they are, not wrapped in a new proxy, so a value derived out of reactive state is a live window onto the real thing, letting you edit a selected item and update the underlying list. We learned that you may, in the one specific case of optimistic UI, temporarily override a derived by reassigning it, for instant feedback ahead of a slow server, with the formula reasserting itself when the real data arrives, a capability added in Svelte 5.25. And we learned the one firm rule, the expression must be pure, no side effects, no state changes, enforced by the compiler, because laziness and purity are what make derived trustworthy, and because the moment you want a side effect, you have stopped describing a value and started describing an action.

That last point is the door into next lecture. State holds your truth. Derived computes pure values from it. And effect, the third rune, is where actions on the world are allowed to live, deliberately, in their own room. We have now built the second of the three. The derived rune is, in the end, the cleanest answer I know to one of the oldest hard problems in the field: you describe what a value is, in terms of other values, exactly once, and a compiler that read your description keeps it true, fresh, and cheap, forever, with no list for you to maintain and no bug for you to ship. Computed values, genuinely without cache bugs.

So take a short break, look back over the derived blocks in your booklet while they are warm, the doubled counter, the derived dot by total, the lazy large example, and when you are ready, we will open the door to the third rune, and learn how to reach out and touch the world. I will see you in lecture six.
