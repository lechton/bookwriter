# 04 | The Watchful Layer: How Far Reactivity Reaches

## 4.1 Where This Hour Sits (no booklet section; orientation)

This is the fourth lecture, and it covers the same ground as the main state lecture, but it comes at that ground from the side, through the fifteen questions in your booklet. So a word on what it rests on. It assumes the first lecture, where Svelte turned out to be a compiler, a tool that reads your code ahead of time and rewrites it, rather than a library that rides along in the browser while the page runs. And it assumes the third lecture, the long one, where we built the world this whole course lives in: a news organisation, The National Times, with its articles, its readers, and the little count of claps under each story. That count, and that newsroom, are where every example here will live.

You may have the booklet open, the one with the fifteen questions, the maps, and the code. I will not read code to you, because code is for the eye, and the eye's work is on that page. What I will do, each time we step into one of the fifteen questions, is tell you which one, so you can look down and find it.

By the end, you should be able to look at any piece of state in a Svelte component and say, before you run anything, whether a change to it will reach the screen, and why. That is the whole skill, and it turns out to rest on a single idea.

## 4.2 Why A Plain Variable Cannot Move The Screen (booklet: "Making a value react to change", question one)

The smallest possible thing is the clap count on a story. It is a number. A reader taps the button, and you want the number to climb.

In ordinary JavaScript, you would keep that number in a variable, and when the tap comes, you would add one to it. And nothing would happen on the screen. That is the problem the entire chapter exists to solve. The variable and the thing the reader sees are two separate worlds. The variable is a value in memory. The number on the screen is text inside an element on the page. Changing the first does not touch the second. To make the screen agree with the variable, you would have to find that element yourself and rewrite its text, by hand, every time the number changed. One count, that is tedious. A page full of counts and totals and badges, and you are spending most of your code keeping the screen and the data in agreement.

So here is the question. What would it take for the number on the screen to move on its own, the instant the variable moves? You can imagine the machinery: something would have to be watching the variable, and know which spots on the screen show it, and update them. The good news is that you do not build that. You write the variable in a particular way, and Svelte builds it for you, ahead of time, when it compiles your code. The particular way is a small marker called the state rune. You wrap the starting value in it, the dollar sign followed by the word state. From that point the variable is what we call reactive, which means only this: Svelte is now watching it, and every place on the screen that shows it will move when it moves. And you change the variable the way you always would, you add one to it, with no special function to call. The booklet has the few lines under question one; the count lives on the article card.

That is the foundation, and it is almost disappointingly small. The interesting part begins the moment the thing you are holding is not a single number, but an object, or a list. Because a clap count is rare. Most state is an article, with a title and a body and a row of tags; or a list of articles; or a draft with a checklist. What happens to reactivity then?

## 4.3 The Watchful Layer, And How Deep It Goes (booklet: "When state is an object or a list", question two)

Picture a draft article in the newsroom, with a small checklist on it, a list of tasks, each task a little object with a label and a flag for whether it is done. You put the whole list in state. Now you reach into it, past the list, into one task, and you flip that task's done flag, deep inside.

Here is the question the section turns on. Does the screen move? A reasonable first guess is that it cannot. Whatever is watching your state is watching the variable, from the outside; and you did not change the variable, the list is the same list. You changed something buried three layers down inside it, and a watcher on the outside of a box has no business seeing a page turn deep inside the box. That guess is wrong, and the reason it is wrong is the idea the rest of this lecture turns on.

When you hand the state rune an object or an array, Svelte does not merely watch the variable from outside. It wraps the object in a thin layer that sits in front of it, a layer that gets to run a little code every time anyone reads a field or writes a field. Reading goes through the layer. Writing goes through the layer. So the layer sees everything, every read and every write, and it is the layer, not some distant watcher, that keeps the screen in step. That layer has a name, one you will meet in documentation and in error messages, so it is worth having: it is called a proxy. A proxy is exactly that, a wrapper that stands in front of an object and notices every read and write.

And it goes all the way down. If your object contains another object, that inner one is wrapped too; if it contains a list, the list is wrapped; layer inside layer, as deep as the data goes. This is why flipping the flag on one task, deep in the list, moves the screen: the task is itself wrapped, and its own layer noticed the write. The booklet calls this deep reactivity, under question two, and the map there shows the single component holding the list. You get the same thing when you add to the list. Push a new task onto the end, the plain way you would push onto any array, and the screen updates, and the new task arrives already wrapped in its own layer. Anyone coming from React will feel something here, because over there, changing an array in place like that is a known way to get a screen that silently refuses to update, and you are taught to replace the array instead. Here the layer is watching the array itself, so the plain, direct change is the right one.

So reactivity is not a watcher in the distance. It is a layer wrapped around the very thing you are holding, reaching into every corner of it. And that fact has a shadow, which is the next section. If reactivity rides on the layer, then everything depends on whether you are still holding the layer, or whether you have somehow stepped off it.

## 4.4 The Live Thing And Its Dead Copy (booklet: "When state is an object or a list", question three; and "Passing state around", question ten)

The booklet keeps the next two ideas far apart. One is question three, here under objects and lists. The other is question ten, much later, under passing state into functions. I am going to take them together, and pull the later one forward, because they are not two ideas. They are one idea, and it is the most common way people break their own reactivity without seeing how.

The idea needs a piece of plain JavaScript groundwork first, about the difference between a thing and a copy of its value. When you have an object in a variable, the variable does not hold the object; it holds a reference to it, a way of reaching the one object. Two variables can hold references to the same single object. But the moment you take a value out of the object and put it in its own plain variable, you have made a copy of that value, and the copy has no connection back to where it came from. It is a value sitting on its own. That is true in any JavaScript program, with or without Svelte.

Now bring back the watchful layer. Your reactivity lives in that layer, which wraps the object. As long as you reach through the layer, through the reference to the wrapped object, your reads and writes are seen, and the screen stays in step. So here is the trap. There is a tidy shorthand in JavaScript for lifting a field out of an object into its own variable; the booklet shows it under question three, pulling the title out of an article. You use it to save typing. And then you change the article's title later, and the heading on the screen does not move. The question is, what happened? It is tempting to think Svelte lost track, that something is broken. Nothing is broken. When you lifted the title into its own variable, plain JavaScript made a copy of the title's value, right then, a copy that was never inside the layer, that the layer has no knowledge of. The real title, behind the layer, did change. Your copy, sitting outside, did not, because nothing connects it to anything. It is frozen at the value it had the instant you copied it. The name for this shorthand is destructuring, and the lesson is not a rule to memorise. It is a thing you can now see for yourself: do not lift reactive values out into loose copies; reach in through the layer each time.

That same truth, worn as a different outfit, is question ten. There you have a function, and you call it with a piece of state, the way you would call a function to greet someone by their name. Does the function get a live connection to your state, so that it sees later changes? Or does it get the value as it stood at the moment of the call? It gets the value, copied in at that moment. This is how calling a function works in JavaScript; the language hands a function the values of its arguments, not living links to your variables. So if you take the result the function gave you and store it, that stored result is a copy, frozen, exactly like the destructured title. Change the state afterwards, and the stored result does not care.

There is a softening detail here, and it matters, because without it this sounds more dangerous than it is. Inside a component, when you call that function right in the markup, in the part of the file that paints the screen, Svelte runs that markup again whenever the state changes, and so it calls the function again, with the current value, and the result on screen stays current on its own. The frozen copy only catches you when you reach for the result and park it in a plain variable off to the side. And for the one case where you genuinely need to hand the live value to a function that will keep it and read it again later, perhaps a function in another file, there is a clean move: instead of passing the value, you pass a tiny function that, when called, fetches the current value. The other function holds that little getter, calls it whenever it needs the latest, and so it is never holding a stale copy. The booklet shows the frozen line and the current line side by side under question ten.

One idea, then, behind two of the booklet's fifteen. Reactivity is the live connection to the wrapped object. A copy is not on that connection, so a copy is dead. Everything left in this chapter is about where that connection reaches, and where it stops.

## 4.5 What The Layer Cannot Wrap (booklet: "Modeling data as a class", questions four and five; "Reactive built-ins and the variants", question six)

So far the watchful layer has appeared on its own, the moment you put a plain object or a plain array into state. But not everything is a plain object, and for the things that are not, the layer does not appear by itself. Three of the booklet's questions are about that, and I am going to gather them, even though the booklet files one of them elsewhere.

The first is the class. When your data grows past a loose bag of fields, you reach for a class, a blueprint you define once, the fields it holds and the actions it can take, and then stamp objects out of, each one an instance, which you make with the keyword new. A draft article is a natural class: it has its data, and it knows how to reset itself or publish itself. So you would expect that putting a draft in state wraps it in the layer, the way a plain object gets wrapped. It does not, and the reason is care, not oversight. A class is a more delicate thing than a plain object. It has methods, it may have private fields, it carries internal machinery of its own, and wrapping the whole instance in a layer that intercepts every read and write could quietly disturb that machinery. So Svelte leaves the class alone, and asks you to switch reactivity on field by field, by marking each field you want reactive with the state rune, either on the field itself, or on its first assignment inside the setup function that runs when an instance is born. The booklet has the draft class under question four; the fields you mark are reactive, the ones you do not are ordinary.

Question five sits right beside it in the booklet, and it is a footnote to the class, not a fact about reactivity at all. It is a pure JavaScript trap about the small word that means "the object this method belongs to." When you wire a button straight at one of the draft's methods, its reset, say, and the button is tapped, that small word inside the method no longer points at the draft. It points at the button, because in JavaScript what the word refers to is decided by how the function is called, not where it was written, and you handed the bare method over with nothing to anchor it. So the method runs against the wrong thing, and the draft never resets. The fixes are small, and the booklet shows them: call the method through the draft inside a tiny wrapper, or write the method in the form that nails that small word to the instance for good. It catches people often, and when it does, it is JavaScript catching them, not Svelte.

The third case is a family of built-in types. Beyond plain objects and arrays, JavaScript ships some special containers: a set, which holds unique values with no duplicates, and is exactly right for, say, the identifiers of the posts a reader has liked; a map, for looking a value up by a key; a date; a web address. These are not plain objects. Each keeps its contents in its own internal machinery, and the watchful layer cannot see through that machinery, so wrapping them does nothing. Put a plain set of liked identifiers in state, add one, and the heart on the screen stays dark. Svelte's answer is to ship reactive versions of these four, with the same names and the same methods you already know, but built with the watchful behaviour inside them from the start. You import the reactive set, written as the two words Svelte and Set joined into one, from a small package named svelte, slash, reactivity, and you use it as you would the plain one. The booklet has it under question six, with the liked posts.

Now, the booklet files that question with the variants, under the title about reactive built-ins and the variants. I have moved it back here, to sit with the class, because it is the same lesson as the class: the watchful layer is automatic only for plain objects and arrays. For a class you switch it on field by field. For these built-in types you reach for a version that already has it. The variants, which come next, are a different matter, because they are not about the layer failing to appear on its own. They are about you, deliberately, turning it down.

## 4.6 Turning The Layer Down, Off, And Sideways: The Variants (booklet: "Reactive built-ins and the variants", questions seven, eight, and nine)

The watchful layer is a fine default, and it is not free. Wrapping an object, and everything inside it, in layers costs a little, and most of the time that little is nothing you would ever notice. But the state rune gives you three deliberate adjustments to the layer, for the times you want them, and each is a knob on the same machine.

The first knob turns the depth off. Take a feed, the endless kind, that grows to thousands of posts, and that you only ever replace wholesale or add to at the end; you never reach in and edit one post in place. Wrapping every one of those thousands of posts, and every field inside them, in watchful layers, only so the list can be shown, is real work for no return, because you are never going to edit inside them. For exactly that, there is a raw form of the rune, written as the rune followed by the word raw, which holds the value without the deep wrapping at all. It comes with one firm condition, the other side of the same coin: since there is no layer inside watching the parts, changing a part does nothing. The only change the raw form notices is replacing the whole value with a new one. And that fits a feed, because the natural way to grow a feed is to build a new list from the old one plus the next page, and assign that, which is a replacement. The honest caution, which the Svelte team itself presses, is not to reach for this out of a vague worry about speed. The ordinary layer is fast enough for almost everything. Measure first, and use the raw form when you actually have thousands of things, or a value you only ever swap. The booklet has it under question seven.

The second knob strips the layer off, on the way out. Your reactive object, remember, is wrapped; what you are holding is the layer, not the bare object. That is right inside Svelte, but the moment you hand your data to something outside Svelte, a charting library, or the browser's own function for deep-copying a value, those outsiders were never built to understand the wrapper, and they choke on it. So there is a snapshot form, the rune followed by the word snapshot, which gives you back a plain, ordinary, unwrapped copy of the data, safe to hand to anyone. The booklet has it under question eight.

The third knob changes when the layer reports. Ordinarily Svelte is deliberate about timing, gathering changes and applying them together for a smooth result, and now and then a change that is waiting on something slow can lag by a beat. Almost always that is what you want. But once in a while you want an instant flicker of feedback to a tap, the section link the reader pressed a moment ago lighting up at once, before the next page has even loaded. For that there is an eager form, the rune followed by the word eager, which shows the change the instant it happens, skipping the usual coordination. It is a specialist's tool, for feedback to a direct action and nothing else. The booklet has it under question nine.

Three knobs, one machine. How deep the layer goes, whether to strip it for outsiders, and when it reports. With those, the watchful layer is in your hands, except for one last thing about it, which is where it gets built.

## 4.7 The Layer Is Wired One File At A Time (booklet: "Sharing state across files", questions eleven and twelve)

Everything so far has lived inside a single component. But some state belongs to no single component. The logged-in reader is the clearest case: the greeting at the top of the page wants it, the comment box deep in an article wants it, a dozen far-apart corners want the same one. Handing it down from corner to corner through every component in between would be miserable. So you lift it out of the components entirely, into an ordinary file that other files import from, a module, and you keep the reader there, for everyone to share.

There is one catch, and it reaches right back to the first idea of the course, that Svelte is a compiler. The state rune is not an ordinary function that runs in the browser; it is an instruction to the compiler, which rewrites your reads and writes of that state into the real watchful machinery when it processes your file. For that to happen in a plain module, the file has to be marked so the compiler knows to process it, which you do by ending its name in dot svelte dot t s, rather than plain dot t s. Now the runes work there. But here is the consequence that catches people, and it follows from the compiler working on one file at a time. If you put a piece of state in that module, export it, and then reassign it inside the module, another file that imports it was compiled separately, and that other file never learned that the name it imported is secretly reactive and needs the special handling. So it would receive, not your value, but the raw machinery underneath, and the connection would break at the border between the two files.

The way around it falls out of naming the cause. The trouble is the reassignment of an exported name across a file boundary, so you arrange never to do that. The first way, under the booklet's question eleven, is to export an object and change a property on it, rather than reassigning the variable that holds the object. You never reassign the export; you set a field on the shared object; and because that object carries its watchful layer with it, the change reaches everyone who imported it. The second way, question twelve, is to not export the state at all: keep it private inside the module, and export instead a small handful of functions, one to read it, others to change it. Every read and write then happens inside the one file the compiler fully controls, and the outside world only ever calls the functions. That second form has a quiet bonus: it gives you a single doorway, one place every change must pass through, where you could check it, or record it, or send it to the server. The booklet has both, with the logged-in reader.

So even the boundaries of reactivity are the compiler's boundaries. The layer is real, but it is laid down one file at a time, and your way of sharing state has to respect that seam.

## 4.8 Choosing Among The Three, And Two Loose Ends (booklet: "Going further", questions thirteen, fourteen, and fifteen)

The booklet's last three are gathered under going further, and one of them is the right note to end the teaching on, because it steps back from state and places it among its neighbours.

That is question fourteen, and it is a question of choosing. The clap count is a value you hold and change over time; that is state, and the state rune is its tool. But take the little label on a story that says it is a five minute read. That number is not held anywhere. It is worked out from the length of the article's body. A value that is computed from other values is not state at all, and forcing it into a state variable, recomputing it by hand whenever the body changes, is the surest way to get a label that quietly goes stale. It wants a different tool, the derived rune, which is the whole subject of the next lecture, and which recomputes itself whenever its ingredients change. And there is a third kind of thing again: not a value you hold, and not a value you compute, but an action on the world outside your data, saving to a server, drawing to a canvas. That is the effect rune, with its own hour later on. So the question to ask of any piece of behaviour is which of the three it is. Am I holding a value, computing a value, or doing something in the world. Holding is state, the rune you now know. Computing is derived. Doing is effect. Get that fork right, and most of the rest follows. This lecture has been the first of the three.

Two loose ends close the booklet, and they are quick. Question thirteen asks whether you can put two components in one file, the article card and the clap button together. You cannot; one file is one component, and to show two components, and how they talk, you write two files. Question fifteen is for anyone arriving from React or from Vue, and it is a translation. The state rune is, near enough, Vue's reactive value with the small ceremony around it taken away, and React's state hook without the separate setter and without the rule never to change the value directly. The deepest difference shows only later, in how the reusable pieces behave, and it can wait.

## 4.9 The One Mechanism (synthesis)

Step back, and the fifteen questions fall into one mechanism seen from fifteen angles.

Reactivity in Svelte is a watchful layer that the compiler wraps around your state, ahead of time. A single value gets wired to the screen. An object or a list gets the layer, reaching all the way down, so that changing anything inside it, or adding to it, moves the screen on its own. Everything else was a question about that layer. Where it reaches: deep, into every field. Where you step off it: into a dead copy, when you lift a value out by destructuring, or pass it into a function and store the result, because a copy was never on the connection. What it cannot wrap by itself: a class, where you mark the fields, and the special built-in types, where you import a version that already has it. How you adjust it on purpose: turn the depth off with the raw form for great masses of data, strip it off with the snapshot form for the outside world, change its timing with the eager form for an instant flicker of feedback. And where its boundaries lie: at the edges of a file, because the compiler lays the wiring one file at a time, which shapes how you share state between modules.

That accounts for every one of the booklet's fifteen. Two of them, question three and question ten, were the same idea, the live thing and its dead copy, and I took them together. Two more, four and five, were a class and a piece of plain JavaScript that travels with it. Question six I moved out of the variants to sit with the class, because it belongs to the same lesson. Nothing was left out, and nothing was claimed beyond what those questions hold.

The one tool this lecture kept pointing past, the derived rune, for values computed rather than held, is the subject of the next lecture. It rests directly on the layer you now understand, which makes it the natural next step.
