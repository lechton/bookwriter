### The Mathematical Scaling Geometry of Cover and Contain

To understand why responsive hero imagery frequently breaks on mobile viewports, one must examine the mathematical formulas governing the `background-size` property under the W3C CSS visual formatting model.

Both `cover` and `contain` share a foundational geometric requirement: they strictly preserve the image intrinsic aspect ratio (the proportional relationship between its natural width and natural height). Neither keyword will ever stretch, compress, or optically distort an image.

Where the two keywords diverge radically is in how they reconcile aspect ratio mismatches between the image and its containing box (the background positioning area).

The official W3C CSS Backgrounds and Borders Module Level 3 specification explicitly defines this distinction:

> contain: Scale the image, while preserving its natural aspect ratio (if any), to the largest size such that both its width and its height can fit inside the background positioning area. cover: Scale the image, while preserving its natural aspect ratio (if any), to the smallest size such that both its width and its height can completely cover the background positioning area.
*W3C CSS Backgrounds and Borders Module Level 3, `resources/spec/csswg-drafts/css-backgrounds-3/Overview.bs`*

#### The Formula for background-size: cover

Under `cover`, the browser engine calculates two independent scaling ratios: the ratio of the container width to the image width ($S_w = W_{box} / W_{img}$) and the ratio of the container height to the image height ($S_h = H_{box} / H_{img}$). To ensure complete coverage, the engine selects the maximum of these two scaling factors:

$$S_{cover} = \max(S_w, S_h)$$

The image rendered width and height are then computed as $W_{render} = W_{img} \cdot S_{cover}$ and $H_{render} = H_{img} \cdot S_{cover}$. Because $S_{cover}$ uses the maximum multiplier, one dimension will fit the container perfectly, while the other dimension will be equal to or larger than the container. Any portion of the image that exceeds the container boundaries is pushed outside the box and clipped. The result is total visual coverage with zero blank space, but with inevitable edge cropping whenever aspect ratios diverge.

#### The Formula for background-size: contain

Under `contain`, the browser performs the reciprocal calculation. It seeks to ensure that the entire image remains visible inside the box boundaries without any clipping whatsoever. The engine selects the minimum of the two scaling ratios:

$$S_{contain} = \min(S_w, S_h)$$

The rendered dimensions are computed as $W_{render} = W_{img} \cdot S_{contain}$ and $H_{render} = H_{img} \cdot S_{contain}$. Because $S_{contain}$ uses the minimum multiplier, the image scales until its largest dimension touches the container border. If the aspect ratios do not match, the other dimension falls short, creating empty margins. These empty spaces are rendered as letterboxing (horizontal bands along the top and bottom) or pillarboxing (vertical bands along the left and right).

To observe this difference in code, inspect the markup of a hero banner:

```html title="hero-banner.html"
<section class="marketing-hero">
  <div class="hero-overlay">
    <h1 class="hero-headline">Mayoral Corruption Dossier</h1>
  </div>
</section>
```

Now examine how the two competing background size keywords style this hero container:

```css title="cover-mode.css"
.marketing-hero.cover-mode {
  width: 100%;
  height: 480px;
  background-image: url("mayor-interview.jpg");
  background-size: cover; /* scales to smallest size covering entire box; clips overflow */
  background-repeat: no-repeat;
  background-position: center;
}
```

In this cover stylesheet, the background image expands until both dimensions cover the container. Any excess pixels extending beyond the 480-pixel height or 100% width are clipped.

Now compare that with the contain alternative:

```css title="contain-mode.css"
.marketing-hero.contain-mode {
  width: 100%;
  height: 480px;
  background-image: url("mayor-interview.jpg");
  background-size: contain; /* scales to largest size fitting inside box; zero clipping */
  background-repeat: no-repeat;
  background-position: center;
}
```

In this contain stylesheet, the photo scales down until the entire image fits inside the 480-pixel container. If the aspect ratio of the container does not match the image, empty gutters appear.

### The Mayor's Face Catastrophe: Aspect Ratio Mismatches on Mobile

Now consider the practical editorial disaster that struck The National Times. The photo department supplied a high-resolution landscape photograph with a natural aspect ratio of 16:9 (1920 pixels wide by 1080 pixels high). In this composition, the mayor stands slightly to the right of center at an outdoor press podium, with the municipal courthouse spanning the left two-thirds of the frame.

On a desktop browser displaying a 1200-pixel wide by 500-pixel high hero banner, the container aspect ratio is 2.4:1. The horizontal and vertical ratios are relatively close, meaning `background-size: cover` crops only a small sliver off the top and bottom of the sky. The mayor face remains clearly visible and well-composed.

#### The Geometry Flips on Smartphones

When a mobile reader visits the site on a smartphone, the viewport aspect ratio shifts from horizontal landscape to vertical portrait. The hero container is now 390 pixels wide and 480 pixels high, creating a narrow aspect ratio of 0.81:1.

Let us trace the browser scaling math:
1. $S_w = 390 / 1920 \approx 0.203$
2. $S_h = 480 / 1080 \approx 0.444$
3. $S_{cover} = \max(0.203, 0.444) = 0.444$

To satisfy `cover`, the browser must scale the photo by a factor of 0.444 to make its height fit the 480-pixel container. However, scaling the width by 0.444 results in a rendered width of $1920 \cdot 0.444 = 852$ pixels!

Because the container is only 390 pixels wide, the rendered image exceeds the container width by 462 pixels. With the default `background-position: center center`, the browser centers the 852-pixel wide bitmap horizontally, cutting 231 pixels off the left edge and 231 pixels off the right edge.

Because the mayor was standing on the right side of the frame, the right-side crop boundary slices right through the middle of the frame, cutting the mayor head in half! The reader sees a beautiful, pristine rendering of the empty courthouse brick wall on the left, while the human subject of the investigation is completely erased.

```html-figure src="md-lectures/figures/25-02-aspect-ratio-art-direction-crop.html" caption="The Mayor's Face Catastrophe: On desktop widescreen displays, cover displays the landscape composition cleanly. On vertical smartphone screens, cover scales image height to fill the frame, cropping the sides and bisecting the off-center subject."
```

#### Why contain Fails as an Editorial Fix

When junior developers encounter this facial cropping defect, their instinctive reaction is to switch `background-size: cover` to `background-size: contain`.

This solves the cropping problem: the mayor face is indeed visible. However, it creates a visual defect that is equally unacceptable for an editorial publication. Because `contain` selects $S_{contain} = \min(0.203, 0.444) = 0.203$, the rendered image shrinks to 390 pixels wide and only 219 pixels high.

Inside the 480-pixel high hero container, the photo occupies less than half the available height. Massive, awkward dark letterbox bars appear above and below the image. Headline text floating over the container now clashes unpredictably with the photo edge boundaries, breaking the visual rhythm of the page and looking like an amateur layout defect.

### Focal Point Anchoring: background-position and Object Coordinates

When you must use `background-size: cover` for full-bleed containers, how do you prevent the browser from blindly amputating critical visual subjects? The first line of defense is **Focal Point Anchoring** using `background-position`.

By default, CSS background positioning centers the image: `background-position: 50% 50%;`. In CSS percentage math, `50%` does not mean "place the left edge of the image at the 50% mark of the container". It means: "align the 50% point of the image with the 50% point of the container."

Similarly, `background-position: 80% 20%;` instructs the browser: "align the point 80% across the image width with the 80% mark of the container, and align the point 20% down the image height with the 20% mark of the container."

```css title="focal-anchoring.css"
.marketing-hero.anchored {
  background-image: url("mayor-interview.jpg");
  background-size: cover;
  background-position: 80% 25%; /* anchors crop coordinates directly to the mayor's face */
}
```

By explicitly declaring `background-position: 80% 25%`, you tell the browser that the focal subject lives near the upper-right corner. When the mobile screen forces the sides to crop, the browser anchors the right side near the mayor coordinates, discarding excess brick wall from the left side instead.

#### Dynamic Focal Coordinates via Custom Properties

In modern content management systems (CMS), photo editors should never be expected to write raw CSS rules for every uploaded photo. Instead, editorial platforms allow photographers or copy editors to click on the primary subject of a photo in the publishing UI, recording a pair of focal percentages (`data-focal-x="0.82"` and `data-focal-y="0.28"`).

These coordinates are injected dynamically into CSS custom properties:

```html title="focal-card.html"
<div class="story-hero" style="--fx: 82%; --fy: 28%;">
  <div class="hero-content">Breaking Investigation</div>
</div>
```

```css title="focal-card.css"
.story-hero {
  background-image: url("mayor-interview.jpg");
  background-size: cover;
  background-position: var(--fx, 50%) var(--fy, 50%); /* dynamic focal anchoring */
}
```

Whenever the viewport resizes, the browser calculates its `cover` crop boundaries while keeping the coordinate specified by `--fx` and `--fy` pinned firmly in place.

### True Art Direction: CSS Backgrounds vs Semantic HTML Picture Elements

While focal point anchoring rescues simple portrait crops, it remains a geometric compromise. Anchoring a 16:9 landscape photo to the right side on a mobile phone might keep the mayor face in view, but the face will appear tiny because the overall photo is scaled down. What the editorial team truly wants on mobile is a completely different crop: a tight, vertical portrait that fills the phone screen with dramatic visual presence.

This is the classic definition of **Art Direction**: altering the actual composition, framing, or visual content of an image across different device form factors, rather than merely resizing a single asset.

#### The Four Severe Defects of CSS Background Images

Relying on CSS `background-image` for editorial hero content introduces four critical architectural deficiencies:

1. **Accessibility Blackout (Zero Screen Reader Visibility)**: CSS background images cannot carry an HTML `alt` attribute. To assistive technologies like screen readers, a container with a CSS background image is completely invisible. Visually impaired readers will have no idea an image of the mayor exists.
2. **SEO Invisibility**: Web search crawlers (such as Googlebot Image Search) index HTML `<img>` and `<picture>` tags. They do not parse CSS background image URLs as editorial content, costing the publication significant image search traffic.
3. **Bandwidth Waste and Performance Degradation**: A desktop background hero is frequently a large, two-megabyte high-resolution image. When a mobile device on a cellular network downloads this desktop background, it destroys Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) performance scores.
4. **Art Direction Overhead**: Achieving different crops with CSS requires authoring multiple `@media` queries with separate background URLs, leading to bloated stylesheets and brittle build pipelines.

#### The Semantic Solution: HTML `<picture>` and object-fit: cover

The professional standard in modern web development is to replace CSS background images with the semantic HTML `<picture>` element, styled with `object-fit: cover` and `object-position`:

```html title="semantic-hero.html"
<div class="editorial-hero">
  <picture class="hero-picture">
    <source media="(max-width: 640px)" srcset="mayor-vertical-portrait.webp" type="image/webp">
    <source media="(max-width: 1024px)" srcset="mayor-square-crop.webp" type="image/webp">
    <img class="hero-img" src="mayor-wide-landscape.webp" alt="Mayor speaking at municipal press conference" loading="eager" fetchpriority="high">
  </picture>
  <div class="hero-text-overlay">
    <h2>Special Investigation: City Hall Behind Closed Doors</h2>
  </div>
</div>
```

Now examine the CSS rules that style this semantic markup:

```css title="semantic-hero.css"
.editorial-hero {
  position: relative;
  width: 100%;
  height: 480px;
  overflow: hidden;
}

.hero-picture,
.hero-img {
  width: 100%;
  height: 100%;
  object-fit: cover; /* replicates background-size: cover on replaced <img> element */
  object-position: center; /* replicates background-position on replaced <img> element */
  display: block;
}
```

```html-figure src="md-lectures/figures/25-03-css-background-vs-html-picture.html" caption="CSS Background vs Semantic HTML Picture: CSS backgrounds treat imagery as decorative wallpaper, lacking alt text and responsive crops. The HTML picture element combined with object-fit delivers fully accessible, SEO-indexed, tailored art direction."
```

### Glossary

- **background-size: cover**: A CSS property value that scales an image to the smallest dimension required to cover the entire container, clipping any excess overflow.
- **background-size: contain**: A CSS property value that scales an image to the largest dimension that fits entirely inside the container, leaving empty letterbox gutters if aspect ratios differ.
- **Intrinsic Aspect Ratio**: The natural proportional relationship between an image width and height before any CSS scaling is applied.
- **Focal Point Anchoring**: The technique of aligning background or object coordinates (`background-position` / `object-position`) to keep the primary subject in view during responsive cropping.
- **Art Direction**: The design practice of serving different image compositions, crops, or assets across different device form factors.
- **HTML Picture Element**: An HTML5 wrapper containing `<source>` elements that allows browsers to select different image assets based on media queries and format support.

### Summary

**The Geometry of Visual Cropping**

Using `background-size: cover` without understanding aspect ratio geometry is a primary cause of broken visual layouts on mobile devices. While `cover` ensures that a container surface area is covered without aspect ratio distortion, it achieves this by aggressively cropping overflowing edges. When a wide landscape photograph is forced into a tall portrait container on a mobile phone, horizontal edge cropping routinely bisects or completely eliminates critical editorial subjects.

Switching to `background-size: contain` avoids cropping by ensuring 100% of the image fits within the frame, but introduces unwanted letterbox or pillarbox gutters that destroy visual hierarchy. To achieve robust visual presentation, developers must either anchor focal points using `background-position` or transition to true semantic art direction using the HTML `<picture>` element paired with `object-fit: cover`. Replaced HTML image elements ensure full screen reader accessibility, search engine indexing, and device-tailored image downloads. Here is your streetwise review.
