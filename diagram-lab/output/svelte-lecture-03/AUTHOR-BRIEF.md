# AUTHOR BRIEF — writing one lecture for svelte-lecture-03

Paste this brief plus the question row into the writing model. The full rules live in `instructions.md`; this page is the working subset that fits a small context. When the build log is clean, the lecture is mechanically done.

## The demo (structure only)
Before writing, open ONE lecture in `../react-lecture-01/md-lectures/` (for example `01.md`) and study ONLY its structure: the order of sections, the shape of the opening ladder, where the `components` panel sits, how the code comments are disciplined, how the Summary and closing table are built, and how deep it goes. Copy NO content from it, in any shape or form: no scenarios, no examples, no metaphors, no code, no interview tips, no phrasing. Your content comes from exactly two sources: the question row and the local Svelte documentation. `react-lecture-01` is the **only** project you may look at. Do not open ANY other project folder (`svelte-lecture-01`, `svelte-lecture-02`, `online-demo-01`, anything else): the demo's form, this brief, the question row, and the Svelte docs are the whole standard.

## Input you receive
- One row from `../../questions/questions.md`: number, tier (`❱ CORE` / `❱❱ MORE` / `❱❱❱ ADVANCED`, each possibly suffixed ` (Kit)` for Q101–Q210), topic tag, question text, hook. The hook is the seed of your opening scenario; grow it, do not replace it.

## Write `md-lectures/{n}.md` with this skeleton, in this order
1. Line 1, exactly: `# Lecture {n}: {Short Title}`
2. Line 2, exactly: `> INTERVIEW QUESTION | {tier} | {question text copied verbatim}` (tier includes ` (Kit)` when the row carries it)
3. Blank line, then the Opening Ladder: 5–7 numbered beats that open the body, in this order: the scene, the code moment, the question (a clear, direct sentence stating the visible conflict without cryptic brevity), the danger, the mystery, the promise. No sentence over 20 words, no em-dashes, and never name the lecture's mechanism term in the ladder (the term is earned later, in the body). The ladder is always numbered; bullets are for unordered lists. The Interview Question box shows the question only: never write a leading scene line before the ladder, and never write the "Imagine this scenario:" lead yourself — the build generates the lead and wraps the ladder in its own styled section. Scenario clarity is absolute: write the beats for a tired B2 English reader with a headache, because the ladder is the reader's first contact with the topic and must be the clearest part of the whole lecture. Every noun in a beat is exactly one thing — a person (named with an unambiguous human role, e.g. "a journalist", "a subscriber", "a reader" — never add unnatural phrases like "a real person"), a thing visible on the screen, a thing in the code, or a machine event — never a bare overloaded word: "editor" (say "the journalist" for the person, "your code editor, the program" for VS Code), "live" (say "the site is open in the reader's browser right now"), "script", "log" (say "print to the console"), and the Svelte mechanism words in disguise: "state", "props", "rune", "reactive", "render", "mount", "compile", "trigger" — write the visible behavior instead ("the page keeps showing the old headline"). Each beat must make sense read alone and out of order. The ladder also carries the logic, never just the scene: every value that becomes the problem (a total, a count, a price) has its business rule stated in an explanatory sentence when introduced and then repeated attached to the on-screen value in the next beat (for example "The paper pays by the word." then "That total counts the words of every story, because the paper pays by the word.") — a value whose computation was never given is a cipher, and the reader cannot fear its loss; the change event (a new story lands) is shown before the stale screen, so the "why" beat points at an input the reader just watched move; and no technical shorthand ("needs a loop over every story, plus a safety check for broken records") may stand in for logic — unpack it into operations the reader can picture ("visit every story, add its words, skip any story with no word count"), re-define taught terms like "a computed value" in plain words in the same beat, and earn any "does not fit one line" wall by first classifying the value as one that needs steps, not one formula. Critical mandates with worked failing and passing ladders live in the "Opening Ladder" section of `instructions.md` (the three case studies: role rotation and phantom routines, the jargon and inside-out engine traps, and the Mismatch Law). Never force the question into a cryptic 4-word riddle — write a natural, complete question that explicitly states the visible conflict (e.g. "Why did London stay as the registered location, even after the update?"). Obey the universal laws: single actor throughout (never rotate reporter/correspondent/journalist), no phantom actors ("background routines"), no metaphorical questions ("Why did nothing move?"), no niche jargon ("bureau", "portal", "wire", "bulletin", "dispatch desk"), and the Mismatch Law (the beat that sets up the scenario must directly expose the contradiction between what the tool was told to do vs what the screen actually needs).
4. Right after the ladder comes the first `### ` section: a catchy title that names the chapter's idea, then the paragraphs that open the teaching and lead into the first code. Never leave a bare paragraph between the ladder and the first heading.
5. Body sections with `### ` headings only (`## ` forces a PDF page break; save it for `## Beyond the basics` audit sections). EVERY lecture includes one ` ```components title="... — Component Explorer" ` panel, mandatory with no exceptions: place it after the prose that names the visual arrangement and before the first code fence that implements the mechanism. The tree must contain EVERY file the lecture shows or imports (fence titles and import paths) and nothing else. Canonical scaffold: top-level folders, files one level deep — `components/` holds every `.svelte` file, `state/` holds `.svelte.js`/`.js`/`.ts` logic and shared-state modules and appears only when one exists; write bare filenames in the block (never path prefixes; the build groups by file type); imports reflect the structure (`./Name.svelte` within `components/`, `../state/cart.svelte.js` from a component into a module). Module entries appear in the left file tree ONLY — the canvas shows rendered components only, and the builder filters modules out of it automatically, so indent a module under its importing root without worrying about the canvas. Only exception: SvelteKit questions (Q101+) whose subject is the folder structure itself (`src/routes/`, `+page.svelte`). The third field (visual kind) must be one of: `shell`, `header`, `navigation`, `profile`, `badge`, `counter`, `card`, `form`, `table`, `button`, `branch`, `instances`, `generic`. The fourth field is the component's real rendered UI, lines separated by `;;`, with the values COMPUTED by hand from the lecture's actual code — `<p>Total payout tonight: {totalPayout}</p>` over three stories of 800, 1200, and 950 words at rate 0.5 becomes `Total payout tonight: **1475**`. Every END component gets the field (a literal string is computable too); a container whose children render inside it stays unfilled on purpose (the children are its UI); a logic module never renders; and every word in a fill must trace to the code block's markup, the code block's starting values, or the prose's description of the screen — filled beats empty, but traced beats invented. The build warns on any lecture with zero panels and on any shown or imported file missing from the tree.
6. Code fences: ` ```svelte title="RealName.svelte" `. Real, runnable examples. Every line inside a fence renders as a numbered row, blank lines included, so keep fences dense. Keep every line under about 80 characters: break long template attributes and object literals across rows yourself, one attribute or property per row — the built output wraps overflow as a safety net only.
7. Comments: `code; // annotation` at the END of the code line, never on their own line. Lead verdicts with `**RIGHT:**` / `**WRONG:**` and put one caps load-bearing word in `**bold**`. Never write `✔️`/`✖️`: the build strips them. Only `**bold**` works inside comments.
8. At least one `> [!TIP]` callout with a `**To impress the interviewer:**` lead, placed at the moment the tip matters. Use `> [!KEY]` for one-line insider takeaways. Full markdown works inside callout bodies.
9. Right before the Summary, a `### Where you will meet this` section: 3 to 5 one-line uses of today's concept in real apps the reader knows; the first line may be tonight's own case; each line is one pictureable moment plus what the concept does there — a concrete situation, never an abstract category. This list is the one place the lecture widens beyond its own world.
10. Close with `### Summary` written as a Streetwise Review from an experienced developer's daily perspective ("when will you write this?", "why does it matter in daily practice?"):
    - Lead the summary body with an authoritative **Technical Title** in bold (`**Technical Title**`) on its own line, followed by an empty line before the opening paragraph.
    - Use `❒ {Subtitle}` section headers.
    - Under each subtitle, use numbered points (`1.`, `2.`) with indented sub-bullets formatted with `<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(a) ...` so they stay on one continuous line without hard-wrapping.
    - Highlight core principles with `➔ NEVER / ALWAYS / IF ... THEN ...`.
    - Bold load-bearing words or phrases in every bullet.
    - NEVER open the summary with conversational filler like "Look," or "Look, in practice...". Open directly with the developer frequency, architectural role, or consequence.
    - End with the comparison table as the LAST block of the file.

## The closing table (exact shape)
```
| | **COLUMN B**<br>(subtitle) | **COLUMN C**<br>(subtitle) |
| ---: | :--- | :--- |
| **Dimension** | value | value |
```
First header cell always empty; header titles are `**CAPS**` plus `<br>` plus a one-word subtitle; the divider row is exactly `| ---: | :--- | :--- |`; every body row starts with a bold dimension; table columns in PDF are narrow (~240px), so ALWAYS separate prose from code with `<br>` (e.g. `Declared with<br>`$state()``) and manually wrap multi-part code across separate backticks with `<br>` (e.g. `let { label }`<br>`= $props()`) to prevent ugly PDF padding breaks. Never put `<br>` inside backticks.

## Hard format rules
- One continuous line per paragraph, bullet, and table row. Never hard-wrap.
- No em-dashes anywhere in the lecture.
- Keep every technical term, and immediately define it in plain English in the same sentence.
- **Define through experience, never only through other words.** Start every new concept from what the reader has already done or seen in this course: a line they wrote, a prop they used, a file ending they know. Define the concept as that known thing plus one change, and teach only the change. If the concept is visible in code, define it by its shape: files so far contained A, this file adds one extra B there; without it the problem you already showed, with it the fix. After each definition the reader must be able to point at something and say what it does.
- **Build the lecture around the one thing.** Every concept reduces to one visible change — one line written differently, one prop added, one file created; even an invisible concept is created by one line or kept in one file. Find it, show it early and alone, and center the lecture on it. Then move from that concrete thing toward the abstract only through the reader's own question, asked out loud at the moment it arises: Why this? We already know a way. Answer it; every abstraction must answer a live question.
- **If the topic is a new part of the file, that growth is the headline.** Say plainly in the first section: your file grows a new part today. The story demonstrates the part; it is not the headline. The ladder's last beat promises the new part, not only the story's outcome.
- **Teach structural surprises before the fence that shows them.** If a construct breaks what the reader believes a Svelte file can hold so far (a `$` rune where a plain `let` was expected, template logic like `{#if}` inside the markup, braces where HTML holds a string, a `.svelte.js` module with no markup), teach the surprise in prose first: name the model the reader holds, tell the reader the construct is allowed and normal, and say what marks the difference — before the first fence that shows it. Never reuse wording from these instructions in the lecture.
- **Raise the strongest naive alternative first.** The first "how else could we do this?" must be the one the course equipped the reader to think of (why not just pass it as a prop? why not a plain variable? why not recompute in the handler?); answer it before any weaker strawman. A tool whose real competitor goes unasked never becomes necessary.
- **Search the deck before baptizing a term.** Grep `md-lectures/` for the hook or concept first. If an earlier lecture taught it, re-anchor with its one-line reminder plus `(see Lecture N)` and deepen — never re-teach from zero with a fresh metaphor.
- **No invisible owners.** Any prose actor that owns the mechanism (the page that stamps out the copies) must appear in a fence or the components panel, or be cut from the prose; ground compound names at first mention (one sentence saying what each noun of the name means).
- **The ladder's last beat promises, never explains.** Promise what today brings, in plain words; the answer to the ladder's own question belongs to the body's first section.
- Length: 800–1,200 words (about 1,000). ADVANCED questions may reach 1,500. Do not pad; do not rush the mechanism either.
