Design description: A side-by-side comparison of two cards on off-white paper, teaching the single practical lesson of awaiting `tick()` before a DOM measurement: without it the read is stale, with it the read is accurate. The left card is labeled "without tick()" and shows a small chat-log UI mock as a stacked block of three message rows where the third row sits just below the visible scroll boundary, marked with a short caption beneath reading "scroll lands one short"; a small crossed-out token labeled `await tick()` sits in the corner to make the absence explicit. The right card is labeled "with tick()" and shows the same chat-log mock with the same three rows but with the scroll boundary now positioned flush against the bottom of the third row so the latest message is fully visible, marked with a caption beneath reading "scroll lands at the bottom"; a small mustard-yellow token labeled `await tick()` sits in the corner of this card. Both cards share identical thick black outlines and hard offset shadows; the only mustard-yellow accent in the entire diagram is the `await tick()` token on the right card, marking it as the prescriptive choice. Exactly four visual elements total: the left card, the right card, the two captions counted as one set, and the contrasting `await tick()` tokens counted as one set. No timeline, no flush internals, no $effect comparison — the diagram argues its point by showing the visible UI consequence (the scroll boundary position) in each card, nothing else.
