# Brief 003 — The pedagogy layer: diagrams that resolve confusion, not depict topics

**Date:** 2026-07-19 · **Scope:** embedding pedagogical principles into the card design step (text + diagram) · **Status:** proposal; upgrades the brief-002 block to v2 and amends exp-002
**Builds on:** `docs/briefs/260719_001.md` (quality gap), `docs/briefs/260719_002.md` (metaphor casting)

## 1. The question

Can pedagogical principles be embedded in the design step — a battery of questions about what is pertinent and key, a short brainstorm of what the average reader commonly confuses or holds vague, and a requirement that the design clarify exactly that vagueness? How should the model think, step by step, for both the text and the design?

## 2. Core principle

**Target the misconception, not the mechanism.** A diagram of the mechanism is a picture; a diagram of the misconception's resolution is a lesson. The two strongest pieces of evidence are already in the lab:

- **Good: `$derived` vs `$derived.by` (svelte-demo 18).** The learner's confusion is a choice: "is `.by` just longer syntax?" The design shows one line of `$derived` beside a full-function capacity list for `$derived.by` with "Must **return**." The asymmetry is the answer. The design resolves the confusion without depicting any mechanism at all.
- **Bad: closure (flashcards_app_02 card 11 / javascript-demo-02).** The learner's real confusion is a violated expectation: "local variables die when the function returns — so how is `count` still there?" The Lexical Environment scene depicts the mechanism statically (outer → retained scope → inner, ACCESSES) and never stages the expectation failing. The card's text says "garbage collector" and "impenetrable vault" — the words carry the pedagogy the design never shows. That is the dumbing-down: not simple, but *aimed at nothing*.

## 3. The confusion typology: three genres of vagueness, three genres of design

Not every confusion is the same shape, and each shape has a design genre that resolves it. This is the answer to "what questions should we ask about what is pertinent": first diagnose, then match genre.

| Confusion type | Learner's state | Design genre | Examples in the lab |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Violation** — expects X, reality is Y | has a wrong model that feels right | **Contrast**: stage the expectation, cross it out, show the actual rule | closures (vars die), `$state` (let updates page), `untrack` (every read subscribes) |
| **Fog** — no model at all | vague, ungrounded, "where does this even live?" | **Grounding scene**: give the invisible a familiar physical form | `$inspect` (Developer Console), proxy traps (Checkpoint), `$effect.root` (manual vs auto cleanup zone) |
| **Choice** — two options, unclear when | can't decide between A and B | **Asymmetric comparison**: identical chrome, one differing dimension | `$derived` vs `.by`, `$state` vs `$state.raw`, old way vs universal state |

The lab's existing winners all land in a genre; the losers (closure, svelte-demo 01–04) are genre-less topic-pictures. Pertinence test: if the card had to be remembered as one sentence, that sentence names the key — and the design genre must match the confusion type, or the card teaches nothing.

## 4. How the model should think: the DIAGNOSE phase (emitted, not silent)

Inserted before brief 002's CAST, and produced as visible reasoning. For text and design alike:

- **A1. STUMBLE.** Brainstorm two or three things the average reader commonly confuses or holds vague about this topic. Pick the most common. Phrase it as the learner's silent expectation. ("Local variables die when the function returns.")
- **A2. WHY IT FEELS RIGHT.** One sentence: what prior experience teaches the wrong expectation? (It is true for normal functions.) This keeps the card honest — the confusion is reasonable, not a strawman.
- **A3. THE KEY.** One sentence: the single distinction or operation that resolves it. This is the card's payload; everything else is cut (the USMLE filter).
- **A4. TYPE + SCENE.** Which confusion type is it (violation, fog, choice)? What scene stages the resolution — expectation crossed out, invisible made physical, or options side by side?

Then brief 002's CAST proceeds, with one amendment: candidate scenes must be able to *stage the resolution*, not merely depict the mechanism. MAP gains one requirement: the wrong expectation must have a visible place in the scene — a crossed-out box, a dashed dead lane, a grayed ghost.

**Text rules derived from the same phase:** the card's `**Q.**` is the learner's confusion phrased as they would ask it (the javascript-demo deck already does this well: "claps was never defined on the object, so why does article.claps = 5 get logged?"); `**Answer.**` gives the verdict first; `**Why it works.**` explains why the reasonable expectation fails. The thinking itself is kept in an optional sidecar file (`01_closure.thinking.md`) inside the experiment folder — auditability without polluting the card, consistent with the more-files-beats-fewer convention.

## 5. The upgraded instruction block (v2)

```text
## Card design step (mandatory, before any markup)
PHASE A — DIAGNOSE THE LEARNER
A1. STUMBLE: list two or three things the average reader commonly confuses or holds vague here. Pick the most common; phrase it as their silent expectation.
A2. WHY IT FEELS RIGHT: one sentence — what prior experience teaches this expectation? Never strawman the learner.
A3. THE KEY: one sentence — the single distinction that resolves the confusion. This is the card's payload; cut all else.
A4. TYPE + SCENE: classify the confusion (violation, fog, choice) and say what scene would stage its resolution.
PHASE B — CAST AND RENDER
B1. CAST: three candidate scenes that can stage the resolution (not merely depict the mechanism), one line each, different families.
B2. MAP: each code element = one scene element, and the wrong expectation must appear visibly (crossed out, dashed, ghosted). Unmapped parts → recast.
B3. NAME: concept name, fresh against the Used Metaphors registry.
B4. RENDER: print-safe; arrows are operations; zones named; the resolution is the visual center, not a footnote.
B5. CHECK: hand the diagram to an imaginary confused reader — does it answer THEIR question, or restate the topic? If it restates, return to A1.
TEXT: **Q.** is the learner's confusion in their own words; **Answer.** verdict first; **Why it works.** why the reasonable expectation fails.
```

## 6. Worked examples: existing cards re-diagnosed

| Card | STUMBLE (wrong expectation / vagueness) | Type | THE KEY | Design that stages it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Closure (redo) | "count dies when createCounter() returns" | violation | the returned function holds the environment alive | crossed-out RIP tombstone for `count` beside a live vault labeled SURVIVED, key held by the inner function |
| `$effect` cleanup | "old timers don't matter, the effect just re-runs" | violation | every re-run runs the previous cleanup first | two timelines: without cleanup three timers pile up; with cleanup one timer, predecessors crossed out |
| `$derived` vs `.by` | ".by is just longer syntax" | choice | capacity: expression vs full body, must return | existing asymmetric boxes — keep as the reference card for this genre |
| `untrack` | "every state read in an effect subscribes" | violation | a read can skip subscribing | existing triggers-vs-ignored lanes — already correct genre |
| `$bindable` | "props are one-way, a child cannot write upward" | violation | bindable opens a two-way channel | existing Two-Way Street — correct genre |
| Prototype | "publish() must live on piece" | violation | one shared method, reached by walking up | sharpen the existing chain: show the failed own-lookup first (not here, crossed out), then the walk upward |
| `$state` | "changing a let updates the page" | violation | $state wires the variable to the DOM | exp-001 Dead Wire vs Live Wire — already correct genre |
| `$inspect` | "where do runes even show themselves?" | fog | runes report through the console | existing Developer Console — grounding scene, correct genre |

Pattern across the table: where the design genre matches the confusion type, the card already teaches; where the design merely depicts the topic (closure), no amount of styling saves it.

## 7. Where the current instructions cause bad design

Three phrases to fix, identified from the user's observation that instructions themselves can embed bad design:

1. README: "adapt a creative way to present the topic of each card in a unique diagram that **fits the message of the card**" — "fits the message" aims the model at topic-pictures. Rewrite: "stages the resolution of the learner's confusion."
2. README: "this is a review card, **not a tutorial**" — misread as "dumb it down". Rewrite with the USMLE filter: "isolates the one confusion a reviewer must resolve; depth lives in the key, not in coverage."
3. `docs/Gemini_instructions.md`, the Designer's Mindset — entirely about form (function dictates form, spatial efficiency, semantic layouts), zero about the learner. Add the fourth principle: every diagram must answer a confused reader's actual question, per the DIAGNOSE phase.

## 8. Risks and guards

- **Invented confusions.** The model may fabricate a plausible-sounding misconception that real learners do not have, producing artificial drama. Guard: A2 forces a reason the confusion is reasonable; if no honest reason exists, the topic is probably fog-type, and a grounding scene is the honest design.
- **Contrast monoculture.** If every card becomes expected-vs-actual, the deck gets a new skeleton and we recreate the original disease one level up. Guard: the typology (violation, fog, choice) forces genre variety; the Used Metaphors registry should record genre too, and a deck should mix all three.
- **Pedagogy theater.** A crossed-out box is not automatically teaching. B5's imaginary-reader check is the guard: the diagram must answer the confused reader's question, not merely contain drama.
